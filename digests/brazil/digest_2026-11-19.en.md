# Brazil: The Economic Pulse — 2026-11-19

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-11-19*

The dollar ended Wednesday (the 18th) quoted at R$5.87, renewing its highest closing level in nineteen months — and the move was far more than a routine currency swing. It signaled that financial markets received the Senate's approval of the "Moretti plan" with muted relief rather than enthusiasm. The Ibovespa fell 1.34% in the same session, and future rates climbed, pressured by the combination of a dollar eroding inflation projections and hawkish remarks from Federal Reserve directors that reinforced bets on another U.S. rate hike in December. The external environment, as UBS's macroeconomics team warned, will not be Brazil's ally in 2027.

The market's message is precise: the approved fiscal adjustment is necessary but insufficient. The package, which Senate rapporteur Eduardo Braga estimated at R$50 billion after negotiations — down from the R$56 billion originally projected — now heads to the Lower House, where Speaker Hugo Motta has called an extraordinary session for Monday (the 23rd). The urgency has its reasons: on Tuesday (the 24th), the government must release the Revenue and Expenditure Assessment Report, and the absence of legislative approval could force an additional contingency freeze on top of the R$13.6 billion already blocked. Planning Minister Bruno Moretti argued that changes approved in the Senate did not compromise the bill's impact potential, but resistance was considerable. A wing of the PT openly worked against the measures, and President Lula himself had to record a video to rein in allies, arguing that the plan does not represent cuts. That a freshly re-elected government, with political capital generally elevated at this stage, has faced such turbulence to pass an adjustment still considered modest by the market says a great deal about the degree of difficulty awaiting Brasília in 2027.

Consultancy Eurasia assesses that the government will need to go beyond the Moretti plan, proposing a constitutional amendment to reduce mandatory spending, but the big unknown is Lula's behavior as the economic slowdown deepens. FGV's GDP Monitor recorded a 0.2% drop in the third quarter — the first contraction after twenty consecutive quarters of growth — and Finance Minister Dario Durigan admitted concern about 2027, though he ruled out a recession. The consumer thermometer is also flashing yellow: the pre-Black Friday period showed a decline in sales, according to a survey by Itaú, and Comsefaz data revealed that Brazilians transferred more than R$40 billion via Pix to betting platforms in the first seven months of the year — a volume that drains household income and contributes to a delinquency rate that already affects more than half of adults.

The main political fracture in the Moretti plan lies not in the cuts it fails to make, but in the taxes it raises. Brazil Journal reported that two central measures of PLP 299/26 represent a significant increase in the corporate tax burden: the linear cut in tax benefits rises from 10% to 20%, and the limitation on tax offsets — kept by the Senate despite criticism from business associations — may in practice function as an advance of revenue for 2027 that weighs directly on companies' cash positions. Among economists, there is a split between those who view the measure as tolerable and temporary and those who see it as a tax hike disguised as an adjustment. The regulatory environment is also pressing from another flank: the ANP decided to proceed with the tariff review of gas pipelines, ignoring sector requests for suspension, while the electricity sector carries a growing dispute around battery auctions, with transmission companies pressing Aneel not to share revenues with consumers.

The industrial sector also sent its own stress signals. Braskem, whose shares trade on both B3 and the NYSE, saw creditors reject its most recent debt restructuring proposal, with only a few hours left for the parties to reach an agreement before a potential bankruptcy protection filing. Copel, in turn, officially declared it will not invest in new wind farms as long as generation curtailments due to oversupply persist — a sign that the curtailment phenomenon has begun to paralyze capital decisions in renewables. The signal contrasts with the optimism of last week's energy auctions, where average discounts of just 0.4% to 2.4% revealed that generators and traders remain confident in the long-term value of their assets, even with spot prices pressured by El Niño.

On the agribusiness side, the moment is ambiguous. The 2026/27 soybean crop is heading for records even under a strong El Niño, and it is the very appreciated dollar — a source of anguish for monetary policy — that is widening producers' margins, according to consultancy AgRural. Corn is gaining new dynamics: the Northern Arc route and the expansion of the ethanol industry are increasing competition for the grain, raising prices in producing regions and reducing historical discounts, even as exports lose steam. These are two markets that benefit, paradoxically, from the same currency fragility that erodes the purchasing power of the urban consumer.

BlackRock's entry into Brazil's asset management market — by acquiring SulAmérica Investimentos from Rede D'Or for R$850 million — is the day's corporate move with the greatest structural implication. The world's largest asset manager expands its local platform at a moment of pronounced volatility, betting on the long-term growth of Brazil's capital market. In the automotive sector, Toyota inaugurated its second production line in Sorocaba, confirming production of the Corolla Deck pickup with a hybrid flex option, while Chinese carmaker Jetour announced R$400 million in investments to produce electrified vehicles in Brazil — with the plant's location yet to be revealed in the first quarter of 2027. The simultaneous arrival of an established Japanese manufacturer and an expanding Chinese brand reflects the competitive redesign pressuring General Motors, which this week launched a voluntary dismissal plan in São José dos Campos with incentives that include either an Onix or R$85,000 in cash.

What to watch in the coming hours and days is clear: the vote on the Moretti plan in the Lower House on Monday will be the defining event for the mood of Brazilian assets in the short term. If approved without significant changes, the government avoids new immediate contingency freezes. If modified, it returns to the Senate and the deadline for the bimonthly report becomes critical. In parallel, the negotiation between Braskem and its creditors may reach an inflection point this week, and any news on the CBS rate — whose definition awaits the Senate and could be reduced if projections of a drop in tax evasion under the tax reform are revised upward — will have a direct impact on the planning of thousands of companies. The dollar at R$5.87 is not just a number: it is the current scoreboard of an open dispute between the fiscal credibility the government is trying to signal and the skepticism the market has yet to abandon.
