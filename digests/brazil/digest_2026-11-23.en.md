# Brazil: The Economic Pulse — 2026-11-23

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-11-23*

The real is the world's worst-performing currency since Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva's reelection — and that statistic, published by Folha de S.Paulo based on BIS data, now functions as a lens that concentrates and reveals the full set of tensions piling up in the Brazilian economy on the eve of the fourth presidential term.

The 17.65% depreciation of the real against the planet's 20 most-traded currencies is not an isolated number. It is the FX expression of a deterioration cycle involving fiscal policy, monetary policy, institutional confidence and geopolitical positioning — and one that gains decisive new chapters this week. The commercial dollar ended last week at R$5.93, up 4.87% in just five trading sessions, resisting even the intervention of the Central Bank, which sold US$1 billion in the spot market without managing to reverse the pressure. Foreign investors closed the November 19 session as net buyers of R$3.75 billion in dollar futures, according to data tracked by trader Léo Molini and reported by InfoMoney — a sign that the bet against the real is not fleeting.

UBS Global Wealth Management, in its "Year Ahead 2027" report, now openly uses the word the Brazilian market had been avoiding: stagflation. Solange Srour, director of macroeconomics for Brazil at UBS GWM, argues that the risk of a recession with elevated inflation grows with each day the government postpones effective fiscal measures. With the dollar at current levels, the IPCA could exceed 6% next year — which would force the Central Bank to resume the Selic hiking cycle even in the face of a decelerating economy. The institution already operates with the Selic at 13.75% per year, and the market projects it will remain at that level through the end of 2026, according to the Central Bank's Focus survey — but FX and inflationary pressure make that stability increasingly fragile.

At the center of this picture lies the fiscal question. The Chamber of Deputies is expected to vote today on an adjustment package, and the president of Febraban classified the so-called "Plano Moretti" — a nickname coined by Lula himself for the new Planning Minister, Bruno Moretti, 46 — as "a good start," according to Valor Econômico. But the nickname itself encapsulates the moment's ambiguity: Moretti is described by Folha as a technocrat trying to simultaneously satisfy the government's political wing and reassure financial markets, without having fully convinced either. The 2027 budget proposal his ministry presented reserves R$8.8 billion for civil servant pay adjustments — an amount that, according to Folha, is insufficient to offset projected inflation for federal Executive branch employees, adding yet another source of spending pressure to the scenario.

Meanwhile, President Lula publicly attacked the "Faria Lima yuppies" on Saturday, declaring that the financial market will not teach him how to govern. The rhetoric aggravates the perception of risk at a moment when net public sector debt has already climbed from 57.5% to 70% of GDP between the end of 2022 and September 2026 — 12.5 percentage points in less than four years, according to analysis published by Brazil Journal. The Movimento Brasil Competitivo, in turn, updated its methodology and estimates the "Custo Brasil" at R$3.5 trillion per year, equivalent to 27.5% of GDP, now incorporating the costs of high real interest rates and public insecurity — a figure that contextualizes the structural dimension of the problem beyond short-term fluctuations.

In this environment of retrenchment, the banking sector faces a problem of its own that could worsen the picture: a stock of R$163 billion in deferred tax assets (DTAs) on the balance sheets of the country's six largest banks, reported by Valor Econômico. A 2022 law determines that this stock begin to be deducted based on 2026 results, assessed at the start of next year, which could impact institutions' capital and reduce credit supply — precisely when the economy would most need financing. Banco do Brasil, specifically, is arranging the conversion of up to R$14 billion of these DTAs, according to Valor, while the economic team discusses names to replace the institution's president.

On the sector front, Petrobras releases its five-year investment plan today, which should hover around US$115 billion — above the previous plan — with expansion in diesel and biofuels and, in parallel, the growing logistical challenge of exploring oil at the mouth of the Amazon, where local infrastructure still operates in precarious conditions, as described in a Folha report filed from the Oiapoque airfield. The greater exposure to capital-intensive projects worries part of the market in a context of falling Brent crude, which opened the week down 3.35%, trading at US$89.39 per barrel, pressured by consecutive inventory builds in the United States.

Mining, in turn, is going through what CSN CEO Fábio Schvartsman described directly on a conference call as "the biggest crisis of recent years," with elevated freight rates and simultaneously falling commodity prices — a combination that squeezes margins and calls business models into question. While this sector suffers, Brazil accumulates a strategic paradox: it holds the world's second-largest rare earths reserve — 25% of global reserves — but accounts for only 1% of production and zero of the refining stage, according to a study by the Centro de Gestão e Estudos Estratégicos presented at the "Voices 2026" event. With China tightening export controls on these critical minerals and dominating 90% of global processing, Brazil is seen as a geopolitical alternative — but the window may close before the country organizes a coherent industrial policy for the sector.

In foreign trade, pressure comes from another angle. Donald Trump publicly declared that he demanded the Philippines cancel agricultural deals with Brazil starting in January, representing one more vector of uncertainty for Brazilian agribusiness in its market diversification strategy — a strategy that, according to Folha, still runs into regulatory barriers and protectionism in Southeast Asia, the region toward which the country is trying to redirect exports in the face of the American tariff hike.

The week that begins will therefore be one of the densest of the year: the fiscal adjustment vote in the Chamber, publication of the Focus Report, the Treasury's macrofiscal bulletin, trade balance data and Petrobras's strategic plan compose an agenda that the market will read as a credibility test for the government. The outcome of the adjustment vote and the tone of the new Focus will be the first indicators of how much room the real still has to fall.

### Banco do Brasil (B3: BBAS3)
The government's economic team is initiating a selection process for names to replace the institution's president, while the bank arranges the conversion of up to R$14 billion in deferred tax assets to preserve its capital position — a pressure that, if not resolved, could force a reduction in credit origination in 2027.
