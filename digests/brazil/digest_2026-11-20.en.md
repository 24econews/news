# Brazil: The Economic Pulse — 2026-11-20

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-11-20*

The real suffered its second-worst week of the year yesterday, with the dollar closing Thursday's session at R$5.931 — its highest level since April 2025 — and foreign analysts polled by Valor Econômico already projecting that the greenback could test R$6.50. What makes this moment particularly revealing is not just the magnitude of the FX pressure, but the convergence of three simultaneous failures: a fiscal adjustment approved by the Senate that the market has already deemed insufficient, a central bank intervention that aggravated stress rather than contained it, and a presidential declaration that Lula "is in no hurry" to name his economic team for his fourth term. Rarely has the cost of political uncertainty translated so visibly into real-time market numbers.

The Banco Central do Brasil's intervention — a US$1 billion spot sale in an extraordinary auction — became the epicenter of the day. Sérgio Goldenstein, former head of the central bank's open market department, labeled the operation a mistake. The diagnosis from other market participants was equally blunt: by intervening, the BC implicitly signaled that there is an exchange rate level it will not tolerate for inflation-model reasons, which led agents to simply migrate risk premiums from FX to the futures yield curve. The result was a surge in medium- and long-term DIs, with the January 2029 contract jumping from 14.93% to 15.30% and the January 2037 contract advancing from 15.19% to 15.44% — the highest levels since January 2025. Tesouro Direto temporarily suspended trading on some securities amid the volatility. The Ibovespa, in turn, technically entered bear market territory after retreating more than 20% from its October peak.

At the center of the storm is the so-called "Moretti plan," approved by the Senate on Wednesday and now heading to the Chamber of Deputies, where lawmakers from both the government base and the opposition indicate that the rapporteur, Representative Mauro Benevides, is unlikely to alter a single line of the text. Deliberations should begin on Monday the 23rd. The problem is that the market has already made its read: the package is a Budget adjustment, not a genuine fiscal adjustment. Banco Pine analysts were direct in stating that the approved bill "visibly is not a fiscal adjustment." Consultancy Eurasia adds that the government will likely need to propose cuts to mandatory spending via constitutional amendment at some point, but the big unknown is Lula's reaction when the economy decelerates — which looks increasingly certain for 2027, with Finance Minister Dario Durigan admitting concern about the slowdown, though ruling out recession.

Criticism of the plan isn't coming only from financial markets. Brazil Journal detailed that two of the package's central measures — raising the linear cut to tax benefits from 10% to 20% and the lock on tax offsets — in practice represent sizable tax hikes on companies, especially those on the presumed-profit regime. For the productive sector, the diagnosis is simple: this isn't a spending-cut blockbuster, it's a tax-hike blockbuster. FGV Ibre reinforced the criticism of the offset lock, warning that the government will have to honor those credits in some other way down the road. Meanwhile, the Instituição Fiscal Independente (IFI) noted that the government still needs to find space in the 2027 Budget for R$20 billion in Lula campaign promises, with no clarity on how those expenditures will be accommodated.

Layered on top of this macro backdrop are structural pressures that have taken a backseat but deserve attention. André Esteves of BTG Pactual sought to put the moment into perspective by stating that Lula's fourth term does not begin on "scorched earth," but acknowledged that fiscal adjustment is the central problem. Bradesco was more somber: its CEO, Marcelo Noronha, in an interview in New York, preached "feet on the ground and prudence" for 2027, warning that Brazilian households are committing nearly 29% of their income to debt — a level he says he has not seen in 15 to 20 years. The Movimento Brasil Competitivo, in turn, released a study estimating the "Brazil Cost" at R$3.5 trillion per year, equivalent to 27.5% of GDP — a figure that, combined with the Selic at nearly 14%, creates a prohibitive investment environment for much of the productive sector.

Amid the stress, a few background stories are worth noting. The decree regulating the end of the 6x1 work schedule, signed by Lula on Thursday, is beginning to take practical shape: the divisor for calculating hourly wages drops from 220 to 210 as of December 16, making overtime more expensive and adding labor costs at a moment when the business community is already absorbing the impact of fiscal tightening. In agribusiness, IBGE data revealed that cattle slaughter fell 13.4% in the third quarter versus 2025, signaling the end of the herd liquidation cycle and growing pressure on beef prices — a trend that converges with the corn ethanol boom, which by producing record volumes of animal feed byproducts is accelerating cattle confinement and should sustain supply expansion in the medium term.

On the technology front, Brazil has become more exposed to geopolitical tensions: U.S. restrictions on exports of advanced AI chips took effect Thursday, adding a bureaucratic layer to imports and potentially delaying the Lula government's ambition to acquire a R$1 billion supercomputer — a project that, according to civil servants heard by Folha, will depend on White House approval. Pix's integration with Alipay+ and WeChat, platforms that together have 3 billion users, points in the opposite direction: the quiet internationalization of Brazilian financial infrastructure, with meaningful potential for tourism and trade with China.

What to watch next week: the Moretti plan vote in the Chamber, slated to begin Monday, will be the immediate thermometer of market mood. Any signal that the fourth Lula government's economic team is contemplating additional measures — including possible cuts to mandatory spending via constitutional amendment — could stabilize the yield curve and ease FX pressure. In the absence of such signaling, the dollar's trajectory toward R$6.00 and beyond remains open. The naming of key ministers, especially at the Finance Ministry, is the political variable the market is most eagerly awaiting.
