# Brazil: The Economic Pulse — 2026-11-10

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-11-10*

Lula's reelection has produced an immediate and visible fracture between financial markets and the government, and the debate over who's right dominates Brazil's economic agenda this Monday with an intensity that transcends domestic politics and finds echoes in assessments from global institutions. While the Ibovespa climbed 1.85% to 171,627 points and the dollar retreated in a session buoyed by a more benign external backdrop — including a drop of more than 5% in oil following U.S. President Donald Trump's statement that fresh strikes on Iran are "highly unlikely" — the structural backdrop remains unsettling: the Focus survey, released by the Banco Central this Monday, shows economists have abandoned expectations of rate cuts and are now projecting the Selic parked at 13.75% through the end of 2026.

A single day's rally in markets does not erase the deeper deterioration that set in after the runoff. According to Folha de S.Paulo, inflation expectations for 2027 and 2028 jumped with confirmation of the PT victory, and Faria Lima's reaction has turned into a social media battlefield — on one side, investors pointing to real fiscal risk; on the other, government supporters accusing the market of blackmail. UBS gave voice to the gloomier reading: with debt equivalent to 80% of GDP, a deficit around 10% of GDP, and interest rates between 14% and 15% a year, "that sucks all the oxygen out of the room," said Alejo Czerwonko, CIO for emerging markets in the Americas at the Swiss bank. For UBS, as long as the government fails to put forward a credible fiscal adjustment with congressional backing, the fundamentals of Brazilian companies take a back seat.

This structural pressure is showing up in concrete data piling up today. In just 12 months, the composition of federal domestic public debt has undergone a striking shift: Selic-linked bonds have doubled their share, shrinking the available cushion for short-term rollover — a classic sign that investors are refusing duration and demanding liquidity. The country's investment rate has remained below 20% of GDP since the second quarter of 2014, a level not even recovered after the pandemic. High interest rates and a cramped fiscal space form the noose tightening that variable. For 2027, Bradesco Asset Management projects first-half GDP growing near zero and accumulated inflation close to or above 6%, with no room for the Banco Central to start an easing cycle before the second half — the "CDI Empire," in the asset manager's phrase, is set to drag on.

The government, for its part, signaled this Monday the kind of economic policy profile likely to prevail in the fourth term: interventionist on prices, generous on subsidies, and cautious toward the regulated private sector. Federal tax exemptions on gasoline and ethanol were extended for another 30 days, and the Gás do Povo program — which distributes cooking gas for free — was extended through 2031. These are measures with an explicit fiscal cost that the market reads as incompatible with the adjustment it demands. A study by economist Sergio Firpo of Insper, released today, points out that a package of 30 structural reforms could unlock fiscal space equivalent to 2.78% of GDP over ten years — but none of them are on the Palácio do Planalto's immediate horizon.

The clash between Petrobras and the ANP over the tariff review of gas pipelines illustrates with surgical precision the governance tensions surrounding the fourth term. The agency suspended the process after threats of litigation and investment cuts from Nova Transportadora do Sudeste — controlled by Brookfield and Itaúsa — and pressures orchestrated by Petrobras itself with ministries. ANP director Pietro Mendes, a former chairman of the state-owned company, pulled no punches at a board meeting: "A method has been created to prevent the ANP from acting. And the one who taught this method is Petrobras." The suspension of the process heeds a request from the Ministry of Mines and Energy, in an intervention that, according to Brazil Journal, the regulator itself viewed as an institutional embarrassment. The outcome undermines the signal of regulatory security that Brazil needs to send in order to attract long-term foreign capital into infrastructure.

And foreign capital is precisely what the country is trying to mobilize on a monumental scale. Valor Econômico publishes today extensive coverage of Infralog, the industry conference, which reveals a country on the verge of hitting a record in infrastructure investment — estimated at R$300 billion in 2026, according to Abdib — but which still carries an annual gap of R$225 billion versus what is needed. In sanitation, R$650 billion are missing to meet the universalization target by 2033. In urban public transport, R$22 billion a year would be needed just to close the deficit in two decades. The Bioceanic Corridor, which promises to cut export times to Asia by as much as 12 days, faces incomplete works in all four countries it crosses. The auction for the Rota Arco Norte, scheduled for Thursday, received only one bid — from the current concessionaire — a snapshot of what happens when fiscal risk and regulatory insecurity persuade investors to stay on the sidelines.

Lula's reelection has also rekindled Chinese appetite for mergers and acquisitions in Brazil, according to Bradesco BBI. The sale of CSN Cimentos to China's Huaxin for about R$9 billion in cash, at an enterprise value of R$12.7 billion, is the most concrete example: the buyer outbid Votorantim and paid below the sector's historical median multiple, betting on the potential of a market where cement consumption per capita is only 30% of the Chinese level. Bradesco BBI sees the post-election environment as a catalyst for more deals along these lines.

Corporate earnings season adds further nuance to the picture. Itaúsa posted recurring profit of R$5.1 billion in the third quarter, up 25%, anchored by the performance of Itaú Unibanco and contributions from Aegea and NTS itself — ironically, the same NTS now squaring off with the ANP. JBS reported a 72% drop in profit to US$164 million, pressured by multiple divisions, but at the same time announced the acquisition of 100% of Flora for US$542 million, diversifying into hygiene and cleaning. Braskem is racing against the clock to close its out-of-court restructuring by Wednesday. Samarco, a joint venture of Vale and BHP, posted a loss of US$544.3 million, still carrying the costs of the Rio Doce reparations. And the Philippines imposed a 60-day restriction on pork imports — a direct blow to Brazil, its main supplier.

This week's agenda will be decisive on several fronts simultaneously: Braskem must close its agreement with creditors by Wednesday, Petrobras reports third-quarter results — with dividends estimated between US$3.6 billion and US$4.2 billion — and Congress faces the expiration deadline for the provisional measure on rural debt renegotiation, which, with only R$18.6 billion renegotiated, risks lapsing without a vote. The fourth-term finance minister has yet to be named, and Valor Econômico describes the pressures awaiting him as having "plebiscitary contours" — a euphemism for saying that the fiscal adjustment the market demands and the subsidies the political base demands are, for now, incompatible.

## Corporate Watch

### JBS (B3: JBSS3 / NYSE: JBS)
The company reported net profit of US$164 million in the third quarter, a 72% year-on-year decline, pressured by weak results across multiple divisions; at the same time, it announced the acquisition of 100% of Flora — a company owned by the same controlling J&F group — for US$542 million, signaling diversification into the high value-added cleaning and personal care market.

### Braskem (B3: BRKM5 / NYSE: BAK)
Shareholders and creditors of the petrochemical company are working to wrap up by this Wednesday negotiations for an out-of-court restructuring of multibillion-dollar debt, according to sources heard by Reuters; the outcome will define the capital profile of the largest producer of thermoplastic resins in the Americas and its access to future financing.

### CSN / Huaxin (B3: CSNA3)
Companhia Siderúrgica Nacional closed the sale of its cement division to China's Huaxin for about R$9 billion in cash, at an enterprise value of R$12.7 billion and a multiple of roughly 7x EBITDA; the deal, subject to approval by Cade and Chinese regulators, represents the largest inflow of Chinese industrial capital into Brazil's construction materials sector and reflects Beijing's renewed appetite for Brazilian assets following Lula's reelection.
