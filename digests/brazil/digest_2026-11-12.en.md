# Brazil: The Economic Pulse — 2026-11-12

The collapse of Banco do Brasil's earnings — a 66.5% drop in the third quarter, to R$1.27 billion, against an analyst consensus of R$3.16 billion — dominates the Brazilian financial landscape and exposes a fracture that extends far beyond the balance sheet of a single state-owned bank. Delinquency in agribusiness, a sector that had been treated as the country's growth anchor and export pride, has become the epicenter of a credit crisis that is now forcing BB to cut its payout to the legal minimum — from 30% to 25% — preserve capital, and revise guidance. The accounting result, R$1.2 billion, was the weakest since the fourth quarter of 2016, when Brazil was in the middle of an outright recession. Return on equity collapsed to 2.8%, down from 8.4% in the same period last year — a figure already considered modest. Loan loss provisions totaled R$21.2 billion in the quarter, of which R$13.2 billion is directly tied to the rural portfolio — a number that reveals the scale of a problem that has been building quietly since 2024, when delinquency among rural producers began to deteriorate.

The irony is acute: Brazil exported 1.2 million head of live cattle in 2026, 14% more than in all of the previous year, according to Athenagro data compiled from Secex, and continues to set records in the agribusiness trade balance. Yet this export bonanza coexists with a highly indebted domestic production sector unable to meet its bank obligations. In the same week, the Senate approved — in a symbolic session and at risk of expiration — the provisional measure on rural debt renegotiation, a concession to agribusiness that illustrates the government's political dependence on the sector, even as it bleeds the country's leading public bank.

That backdrop would make any fiscal announcement a credibility challenge. The Lula administration unveiled the so-called Moretti Plan — named after Planning Minister Bruno Moretti — with a projected impact of between R$50 billion and R$60 billion in 2027, though estimates from the economic team itself, shared with Senate rapporteur Eduardo Braga, place the figure between R$43 billion and R$54 billion. The package includes lowering the real spending growth cap from 2.5% to 2%, creating a mandatory contingency floor of R$12.8 billion — irrevocable even if revenues improve — and authorization to exclude up to R$10.5 billion in diesel subsidies from the 2026 fiscal target. The bill was filed by Senator Jaques Wagner (PT-BA) and now heads to a vote.

Markets responded with partial relief and structural skepticism. In parallel, the BC released results of a questionnaire showing economists expect the eventual elimination of the 6x1 work schedule to add 0.3 percentage point to inflation and shave 0.2 percentage point from GDP in 2027 — another source of pressure on a monetary authority already in tightening mode.
