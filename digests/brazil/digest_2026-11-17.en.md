# Brazil: The Economic Pulse — 2026-11-17

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-11-17*

Brazil's financial markets delivered an unambiguous verdict yesterday on the "Moretti Plan": the fiscal adjustment approved by the Senate last week failed to convince. The dollar surged 2.17% to close at R$5.7777 — its highest level since April 2025 — while the Ibovespa retreated 1.83% to 169,295 points, with future rates posting a sharp climb. Since the runoff election, foreign investors have accumulated nearly US$17 billion in net purchases of currency derivatives on B3, according to data obtained by InfoMoney, signaling a systematic de-risking that extends well beyond the volatility of a single trading session.

The market's reaction is revealing for what it implies about the nature of the package. As Brazil Journal pointed out, the Moretti Plan is less a "spending shock" than a "tax shock": of the R$45 billion to R$56 billion in estimated fiscal impact for 2027, only one-third would come from spending cuts. The remainder would be financed by revenue increases — including raising the linear cut on corporate tax benefits from 10% to 20% and broadening taxation on presumed-profit regimes, which could push the effective rate on service providers from 32% to 38.4%. Mid-sized companies, already squeezed by exceptionally high real rates, will bear the brunt. An opinion column in Brazil Journal summed up the dilemma with precision: the government won the elections, and now the tax rates go to the victor.

The central contradiction markets are pricing in is this: a primary deficit of just 0.5% of GDP coexists with a nominal deficit of 9.5% of GDP — the gap, as economist Cecília Machado noted in Folha de S.Paulo, is the cost of interest on public debt. An adjustment that raises corporate taxes without structurally tackling the dynamics of mandatory spending and debt service is unlikely to alter that equation in any sustainable way. André Esteves, chairman of BTG Pactual, acknowledged that the market has flagged urgency on fiscal rebalancing, but said he believes President Lula "will deliver what is expected" — a statement that, in this context, functions more as a wager than as a certainty. The president himself returned to Brasília on Sunday night after a week abroad and begins today to build support for the package in the Lower House, which should receive the text this week.

Internal dissent within the government is an additional source of uncertainty. São Paulo Governor Tarcísio de Freitas publicly criticized the Moretti Plan and defended the option of postponing tax reform. At the same time, foreign managers are closely monitoring not only the debate over the fiscal package but also speculation about the next Finance Minister — with Vice President Geraldo Alckmin's name circulating behind the scenes, even though he himself brushed off the question with humor when asked.

Added to this unsettled fiscal backdrop is an adverse macroeconomic data point: the Central Bank's Economic Activity Index (IBC-Br) showed a 0.9% contraction in the third quarter, confirming the expected slowdown in the second half of the year. This week's Focus survey brought downward revisions for 2026 GDP and inflation, but economists raised their projections for the Selic rate in 2027 — a sign the market does not believe the fiscal package, in its current form, will open room for a rate-cutting cycle. Morgan Stanley went further: it downgraded its recommendation on Brazilian equities from neutral to underweight, projecting the Ibovespa at 160,000 points next year, a roughly 7% decline from the reference level used in the report released on Sunday.

Inflationary pressures are gaining concrete reinforcement. The end of the subsidy on imported diesel raises fuel costs by R$0.42 per liter, according to one major sector player's calculations, with pass-through to the pump depending on distributors' and stations' commercial policies. Residential rents continue rising above inflation, though at a less intense pace in recent months. And data from Conab show that Brazil is entering the El Niño period with low stocks of rice and wheat, with domestic production declining and prices climbing — a food-price risk that could further complicate the IPCA trajectory.

On the technology and geoeconomic front, two opposing forces are shaping the business environment. Brazil's inclusion in the US list of countries subject to restrictions on importing high-performance chips for data centers — even when manufactured outside US jurisdiction — creates a concrete obstacle to the country's ambitions of becoming a regional AI hub. Washington's decision collides directly with Paraguay's strategy, which, amid the political crisis that ousted President Santiago Peña earlier this month, had sought to leverage its Itaipu energy quota to attract crypto mining and data centers with US backing — an episode that intensifies pressure on the renegotiation of the bilateral treaty governing the plant. In the opposite direction, Chinese cloud and AI providers are advancing in Brazil with prices up to 40% below those of US big tech and contracts with the federal government, while the Lower House is expected to vote this week on a bill regulating digital markets that, according to a study by the Finance Ministry's Economic Reforms Secretariat, could generate economic gains of 0.1% to 0.4% of GDP.

In the coming hours, the Senate will conclude voting on amendments to PLP 299 before sending it to the Lower House. The path through the Lower House — where the government's base is more fragmented and where internal resistance has already surfaced — will be the real credibility test for the package. In parallel, the National Monetary Council may take up this week Banco do Brasil's proposal to ease loss-provisioning rules for rural credit — a negotiation that, if approved, would ease the bank's balance sheet but raise questions about the regulatory soundness of the system at a moment when the Master case, whose total cost already exceeds R$75 billion, has yet to produce the structural reforms needed to prevent recurrence. With the dollar flirting with R$6 and the market in risk-off mode, any sign of wavering in the Lower House or weakening of the package could fuel another round of pressure on the currency and long-end rates.
