# Brazil: The Economic Pulse — 2026-10-15

The Senate's approval, by 75 votes to 1, of the constitutional amendment abolishing the 6x1 work schedule was not merely a legislative victory for President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva on the eve of the runoff election — it was also the opening shot of a bill that Brazil's productive sector has already begun to tally with alarm, and that comes at a moment when the country's economy is navigating increasingly murky fiscal waters.

The business reaction was immediate and sweeping. Fiesp called the measure a "ballot-box amendment," accusing the government of having instrumentalized Congress for electoral gain. Abrasel, which represents bars and restaurants, labeled the end of the 6x1 schedule a "potential socioeconomic disaster," estimating payroll cost increases of up to 20%. The healthcare sector projects an annual impact of between R$7 billion and R$12 billion. In retail, consultancies calculate a 10% rise in wage costs per hour worked, before accounting for new hires. Civil construction — labor-intensive and already under strain from the high-interest-rate environment — warns of the risk of extended project timelines and, paradoxically, lower earnings for workers paid on productivity. For the shopping-mall sector — whose annual revenues exceed R$200 billion — the consensus is that costs will squeeze tenants and intensify competition for commissions among salespeople. The vote, however, was broadly cross-partisan: even Flávio Bolsonaro, the opposition candidate in the runoff, voted in favor, laying bare the political calculus that ran through the entire debate.

The timing of the measure cannot be divorced from the broader fiscal context. Folha de S.Paulo reported that the government will need to carry out an adjustment of up to R$39.9 billion in the 2027 budget proposal to accommodate measures announced on the eve of the election — among them, the forgiveness of R$15 billion in debts owed by individual microentrepreneurs and the increase in the MEI revenue ceiling, approved by the Chamber of Deputies under urgent procedure. Added to this are the R$16 billion already disbursed by Petrobras in fuel-price subsidies, under a program that private refineries are already threatening to challenge in court, arguing that it favors importers at the expense of domestic production. The government also zeroed out federal taxes on gasoline in the days leading up to the first round.

The fiscal picture resonates with a diagnosis gaining traction among economists. Valor Econômico highlights the concept of "fiscal fatigue": according to Manoel Pires, coordinator of the Fiscal Policy Center at FGV Ibre, Brazil reached that threshold when net general government debt surpassed 52.5% of GDP — a level now exceeded by a wide margin, with the indicator standing at 69.6% of GDP, according to Banco Central data. At this stage, the government loses its capacity to react in order to stabilize debt, approaching what economists call the risk of repudiation or capital flight. Economist Roberto Luis Troster, of Cefeb/Fipe, sees 2027 as a window for reversing the trajectory — but only if consistent fiscal and structural measures are adopted after the elections, which would require a degree of political discipline that this week's events do not suggest.

Markets reacted with restraint to the avalanche of news. The dollar closed up 0.14%, at R$5.030, pressured both by electoral uncertainties and by U.S. inflation data running above expectations in September — which revived bets on a more hawkish Federal Reserve stance and sent Treasury yields surging. The Ibovespa retreated, trading below 204,000 points in the morning before recovering some ground. Currency flows, however, offered a revealing counterpoint: Brazil recorded net inflows of US$2.4 billion in the week following the first round alone, according to Banco Central, a sign that the electoral outcome — with Lula and Flávio Bolsonaro advancing to the runoff — was received with relative relief by international investors, at least in the very short term.

The Banco Master crisis thus remains without a clear institutional resolution, with its ramifications simultaneously reaching the TCU, the STF, and Congress.
