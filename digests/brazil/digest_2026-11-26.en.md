Washington's proposal to impose a 10% tariff on Brazilian aircraft and a surcharge on sugar — announced by the Office of the U.S. Trade Representative (USTR) on Wednesday — has dominated Brazil's economic landscape over the past two days and laid bare Brasília's vulnerability at a moment when the country is already contending with intense fiscal pressure and political uncertainty over the economic team of the incoming administration.

The American move, described by the USTR as a response to Brazil's refusal to correct "unreasonable acts, policies and practices," is widely read as direct retaliation for Brasília's decision to challenge the Trump administration's tariff package at the World Trade Organization — a filing blocked by the U.S. itself just one day before the new measures were announced. Washington's message is as strategic as it is economic: according to analysts cited in local press, the measure is "surgical" and designed to force Brazil back to the negotiating table, particularly after the Lula government emerged victorious from the runoff and began a new term showing willingness to confront American trade barriers. Data from the Ministry of Development, Industry, Trade and Services (MDIC) indicate that the potentially affected products account for roughly 7% of Brazil's total exports to the United States — a meaningful share, but not enough to trigger an immediate macroeconomic shock.

The counterpunch, however, is not straightforward: analysts and U.S. airline executives have warned that the tariff will raise operating costs for American carriers themselves, which have no domestic alternative in the regional jet segments where Embraer is dominant. The so-called "Moretti plan," already signed into law after approval in the lower house, was well received by the market as a signal of the government's willingness to move forward with adjustment, but the investor consensus is that its measures — with estimated impact between R$46 billion and R$56 billion in 2027 — fall short of structurally containing the growth of mandatory expenditures.
