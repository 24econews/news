Brazil woke up on Tuesday as the world's largest importer of Chinese automobiles — a distinction no analyst would have predicted five years ago and one that, more than any other single data point, captures the speed at which trade geopolitics is reshaping the country's industrial fabric. From January through May 2026, Brazil purchased US$5.2 billion worth of Chinese-made vehicles, a 146.9% jump from the same period last year, overtaking Russia and Belgium and vaulting from sixth to first place in the global importers' ranking, according to Chinese customs data reported by Valor Econômico. The surge is dominated by electrified vehicles, which account for 87% of total imports — up from just 33% in 2021 — and which alone reached US$2.7 billion in April and May combined.

And Sany, the construction machinery manufacturer, has just taken over Mercedes-Benz's plant in Campinas, launching operations with 200 hires and testing local vehicle production — a sign that the Chinese playbook is advancing from imports into domestic manufacturing. The central contradiction of this movement is that it is unfolding precisely as the Lula government fights a tariff war with Washington, faces mounting restrictions in the Chinese beef market, and tries to convince small and mid-sized businesses that the Mercosur-EU deal will open doors for them. Brazil has never been so exposed to the Chinese economic cycle — and never had so little control over it.

On the trade front, the day brought multiple simultaneous moves. The government formally filed a complaint at the World Trade Organization against tariffs imposed by the Trump administration, which reach 37.5% on some Brazilian products and affect 47.3% of national exports to the United States, according to Planalto's own estimate. The request for consultations under the WTO's dispute settlement mechanism is another signal that Brasília does not intend to negotiate under pressure, but the WTO route is slow and its outcomes uncertain — especially at a moment when Washington has deliberately hollowed out the multilateral system. President Lula had already published an op-ed in the Washington Post branding the U.S. tariffs "a strategic mistake" and flagging reciprocity mechanisms, including the Reciprocity Law approved by Congress last year.

Lula and Chinese President Xi Jinping also spoke by phone to accelerate talks on a Mercosur-China agreement, and a working group has been set up to advance negotiations with Seoul. Brazilian trade diplomacy has rarely been so busy — nor so pressured.

The fiscal snapshot, meanwhile, remains worrying: the IMF, in its annual report on Brazil, projects gross debt at 96.5% of GDP under the Fund's methodology and recommends a more ambitious and less gradual adjustment than the one currently planned. To counterbalance that, a Senate committee approved in a lightning vote a bill that, according to Folha de S.Paulo, would add more than R$1 trillion in extra costs to electricity bills — a fiscal time bomb embedded in an energy policy text that was born with two articles and reached the floor at eighteen pages.
