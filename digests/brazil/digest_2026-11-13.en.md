# Brazil: The Economic Pulse — 2026-11-13

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-11-13*

Brazilian financial markets delivered a harsh verdict on the "Moretti plan" yesterday: the dollar advanced 1.60% to R$5.65, its highest level since June 2025, while the Ibovespa retreated 0.43% to 171,324 points, and the DI contract maturing in January 2031 jumped from 14.87% to 15.10%. Investors' message was unmistakable — the fiscal package unveiled by the Lula administration on Wednesday (11) is insufficient to restore credibility to public accounts, and the Senate's approval of the base text by a 45-to-31 vote was not enough to ease pressure on Brazilian assets.

The so-called "Moretti plan," named after Planning Minister Bruno Moretti, estimates a fiscal effort between R$45.1 billion and R$56.1 billion in 2027, combining spending cuts and revenue increases. The government frames the package as the measure required to seek the midpoint of the primary result target — a demand from the Federal Court of Accounts — and to secure approval of the 2027 Budget Bill by mid-December. But the political operation that enabled the Senate vote required significant concessions: rapporteur Eduardo Braga accepted 25 amendments, including preservation of the transfer rules for the Federal District's Constitutional Fund and the exclusion of information technology and semiconductor sectors from additional cuts to tax incentives. Four highlighted provisions, including the controversial measure restricting companies' ability to offset tax credits, were postponed to next Tuesday.

That specific point is where the most organized private-sector resistance has coalesced. Business associations have vigorously criticized the attempt to cap tax offsets — a measure that Congress itself had already rejected in 2024, when the then-Senate president returned a similar proposal to the Executive. The government is pressing on. The PT's left wing is pushing back on cuts to civil-service spending and social benefits. At the same time, Congress approved, in a joint session, R$149.4 billion in budgetary credits to fund payments of Bolsa Família, BPC, and Renda Mensal Vitalícia — laying bare the structural tension that no fiscal adjustment package can, for now, resolve: Brazil keeps approving expenditures even as it debates how to contain them.

The government has also proposed removing up to R$10.5 billion in diesel subsidies from the 2026 fiscal target, which amounts to accounting relief without any real reduction in spending — further evidence that the ongoing adjustment is too surgical to alter the trajectory of public debt. A study by FGV Ibre, cited in this edition by Folha de S.Paulo, reinforces that Brazil's high real interest rate problem extends beyond fiscal policy: the structurally elevated Selic has institutional and credibility roots that fiscal policy alone cannot dispel.

Against this backdrop, third-quarter corporate earnings season told two parallel stories. On one side, a deepening crisis in the rural credit book: the four largest publicly traded banks in the country booked a combined R$49.9 billion in loan-loss provisions during the quarter — more than double the institutions' aggregate net profit, according to Folha de S.Paulo. Banco do Brasil was the epicenter of the shock. With a 66.5% drop in adjusted net income and a negative pre-tax operating result of R$906 million — avoiding a loss only thanks to tax credits generated by the provisions themselves — shares of the state-controlled bank tumbled 8.34% to R$15.48. The bank's CEO, Tarciana Medeiros, described the moment as the "most severe cycle" in BB's history in rural credit, with delinquency concentrated among midsize and large soybean, corn, and cattle producers in the Center-West who, according to the executive, chose not to renegotiate their debts. Agribusiness associations, such as Aprosoja, rejected that characterization, calling it a "sweeping accusation of bad faith." The diagnoses differ, but the numbers do not: agribusiness, which consumed abundant credit when the Selic was near 2%, cannot service debt at rates that have since multiplied sixfold. Caixa Econômica Federal also saw recurring profit fall 7.4% to R$3.5 billion, pressured by delinquency in agribusiness and among individuals — although mortgage lending, with a portfolio of R$1.043 trillion growing 15.5% over twelve months, helped shore up results.

On the other side, the nimbler and more diversified private banks navigated the same environment with skill. BTG Pactual posted a record quarterly profit of R$5.33 billion, swimming against the sector's credit-slowdown tide, and announced a share buyback program. Nubank, whose NYSE-listed shares rose about 2% after-hours following the result, reported a profit of US$1.1 billion, with its credit portfolio growing 33% year-over-year to US$41.6 billion. CFO Rob Livingston acknowledged that delinquency had risen — both the 15-to-90-day and the over-90-day indicators advanced 0.6 percentage point year-over-year, to 4.9% and 7.3%, respectively — but characterized the deterioration as the result of a deliberate decision to expand credit card offerings, the highest-risk and highest-return segment.

October inflation brought some numerical relief, with the IPCA registering a rise of just 0.14% — below market estimates, following September's 0.82%. But the food component tells a different story: food inflation was the highest in four years, with tomatoes up 89.76% year-to-date, onions rising 11.29% in October alone, rice advancing 4.81%, and meats 2.27%. The combination of weather events and a depreciated currency continues to pressure the shopping baskets of low-income families, limiting the disinflationary impact of the relief seen in services. The total fixed-income stock on B3 surpassed R$10 trillion for the first time — a milestone that illustrates how high interest rates redistribute resources from the real economy to financial instruments, making productive credit more expensive while generously rewarding financial capital.

In infrastructure, the Rota Arco Norte auction — the highway linking Mato Grosso to Pará and a strategic route for Brazilian soybean exports — was awarded to Grupo Conasa with no competing bidders and a trivial 0.01% discount on the base toll. The episode underscores the warning from Motiva CEO Miguel Setas that high interest rates and the asset profile of upcoming auctions could reduce private-sector appetite for infrastructure concessions — precisely when Brazil most needs private investment to offset fiscal constraints.

In the coming days, the market will watch Tuesday's vote on the Moretti plan's highlighted items, including the tax offset issue, which has the potential to further sharpen the conflict between the government and the business sector. The trajectory of agribusiness delinquency will remain under close watch, especially Banco do Brasil's posture toward a portfolio that, in the words of its own analysts, has yet to hit bottom. And October retail data, expected Friday (13), will offer a fuller read on whether the Brazilian consumer — squeezed by costlier food and still-elevated interest rates — is beginning to show signs of retrenchment, a scenario that would further complicate the government's fiscal adjustment plans for 2027.
