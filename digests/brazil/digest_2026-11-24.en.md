# Brazil: The Economic Pulse — 2026-11-24

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-11-24*

The Moretti Plan was approved by the Chamber of Deputies by 353 votes to 128 on Monday evening, but Brazil's financial markets greeted the Lula administration's legislative victory with conspicuous indifference — and that neatly captures investor sentiment at this moment. The dollar briefly broke above R$6 during the session, a level not seen since April 2025, before easing slightly on rumors that the Planalto Palace is inclined to nominate "market-friendly" names for the two vacant directorships at the Central Bank. The Ibovespa closed down 0.95% at 164,796 points, marking the third consecutive session of losses and consolidating its entry into bear-market territory — a drop of more than 20% from the October peak of 209,000. The message from markets is clear: fiscal adjustment approval was already priced in and, on its own, is not enough.

The bill in question foresees savings of between R$45 billion and R$56 billion and opens up roughly R$11 billion of budget space in 2027. Unchanged from the Senate version, the text now heads to presidential sanction. Finance Minister Dario Durigan said the government's priority is to build a trajectory of rising primary surpluses in order to regain the country's investment-grade rating. BCB President Gabriel Galípolo echoed the point, arguing for a review of fiscal rules that, in his view, tend to perpetuate the growth of public spending and pressure inflation, making the job of monetary policy heavier. Galípolo's remarks were read by the market as a signal that the Copom has, for now, no immediate appetite to resume Selic hikes — which drove futures yields lower across the curve by the end of the day, with the January 2029 DI falling from 15.305% to 15.075%.

The problem is that economists consulted by Folha de S.Paulo view the Moretti Plan as necessary but far from sufficient. The risk of stagflation — high inflation combined with a stagnant economy — is increasingly being cited as a plausible scenario. The Finance Ministry itself worsened its macro projections, cutting its GDP growth estimate and raising its forecasts for the dollar and inflation in both 2026 and 2027. The market consensus captured by the Focus survey already puts 2027 IPCA at 5.12%, above the 4.5% ceiling of the tolerance band, in a six-week streak of consecutive upward revisions. Warren Investimentos projects inflation of 5.6% for next year. With the real having depreciated by 17.65% since Lula's victory — the largest decline among the 20 most-traded currencies in the world, according to a survey based on BIS data — imported costs and FX pressure on prices are set to intensify. Add to that the shocks lined up for 2027: the end of the 6x1 work schedule, implementation of the tax reform, and the removal of fuel subsidies.

São Paulo Governor Tarcísio de Freitas said the plan "does not attack the root of the problem," while allies within Lula's own government are pressing the president to put forward a new, more robust fiscal package before the December congressional recess. Congressional leadership has reportedly committed to voting on a new proposal by December 15, if one exists. Lula is also signaling that he may soon announce picks for the Finance Ministry and for the future Ministry of Public Security of his fourth term — moves the market is watching with heightened attention given the deteriorating expectations backdrop.

On the corporate front, the day was dominated by two moves from Petrobras, whose ADRs trade on the NYSE. The state-controlled company released its 2027-2031 Business Plan, with investments of US$122 billion — up 11.9% from the previous plan — and announced extraordinary dividends of R$15.6 billion. Even so, the shares fell through the session, weighed down by a drop of nearly 4% in oil prices — WTI slipped below US$80 for the first time in three months — and by concerns over political interference following a meeting called by Lula to discuss the company's situation. Separately, Petrobras announced the acquisition of 42% of Neomille, a corn ethanol producer controlled by CerradinhoBio, for R$2.8 billion, marking the company's return to the biofuels sector after years of divestment under the Temer and Bolsonaro administrations.

The other major corporate event of the day was the restructuring agreement at Braskem, negotiated on the brink of a judicial reorganization filing. According to Brazil Journal, lawyers arrived on Sunday with the Chapter 11-equivalent paperwork ready to go — the protection period against enforcement actions was expiring on Monday. A final round of negotiations produced an understanding that envisages total capitalization of US$4.9 billion, comprising US$1.3 billion in an immediate capital injection from Petrobras and US$3.6 billion via debt-to-equity conversion by creditors. Braskem's remaining debt will stand at around US$7 billion. The parties will ask the courts for another 20 business days to turn the commercial agreement into a formal out-of-court restructuring plan.

In the broader macro picture, household and corporate delinquency reached R$371.1 billion in the third quarter of 2026, the highest level since the series began in 2011, according to FecomercioSP — up 26.7% from the same period a year earlier. Even so, CNC projects that Black Friday this week will deliver record sales not seen in 12 years. Sector estimates vary widely, from a drop of 2.5% to a gain of 20%, reflecting the bifurcation of Brazilian consumption: subsidized credit and the 13th-salary payment — whose first installment is due by the 30th — are supporting short-term demand, while high interest rates and indebtedness are eroding medium-term recovery capacity. This contradiction — resilient consumption built on a fragile financial base — is what Itaú Unibanco CEO Milton Maluhy Filho encapsulated when he said that Brazil's main economic challenge is to reduce the cost of capital. Luiz Carlos Trabuco, chairman of Bradesco's board and the new head of the Movimento Brasil Competitivo, took the same line, pointing to the cost of capital as the central obstacle to growth.

On the commodities geopolitics front, European industry has formally asked the European Union to impose strict conditions on the sale of Anglo American's Brazilian nickel mines to a company controlled by the Chinese government — the latest episode in the global contest over critical minerals, in which Brazil occupies an increasingly central position. What to watch in the hours and days ahead: market reaction to the bimonthly revenue and expenditure report released on Tuesday; the names Lula will nominate to the Central Bank, which will largely set the mood for domestic assets through year-end; and the possible rollout of a new, more comprehensive fiscal package, which the market is clearly demanding as a condition for any stabilization of the exchange rate and inflation expectations.
