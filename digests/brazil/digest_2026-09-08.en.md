> TITLE: Brazil's Fiscal Crisis Escapes Campaign Silence as Debt Hits 82.5% GDP

# Brazil: The Economic Pulse — 2026-09-08

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-09-08*

The arithmetic of Brazilian public debt rarely presents itself with such clarity as it did this week, when Roberto Campos Neto — the former Central Bank president — signed a column in Folha de S.Paulo bluntly describing what the market already knew but few government officials had said out loud: gross general government debt reached 82.5% of GDP in July, consuming R$10.9 trillion, the interest bill devours 8.7% of GDP per year, and the nominal deficit is approaching 10% of GDP. With the Selic at 14% per annum — a rate that, according to InfoMoney, Copom has just cut to this level and where the market expects it to remain at least through this week's meeting — the government funds itself at a cost far above the economy's growth pace. Campos Neto's message is not new, but its timing is: ten days before the first round of the presidential election, with a Quaest poll showing a statistical tie between Lula and Flávio Bolsonaro, the fiscal question has ceased to be a technical debate and has become the axis of the campaign.

The problem is that none of the candidates offer convincing details on how to resolve the impasse. Valor Econômico reports that all leading contenders promise fiscal adjustment, but none spells out how to reconcile that commitment with maintaining the indexation of pension benefits to the minimum wage and the constitutional floors for health and education. Planning Minister Bruno Moretti — described by Lula himself as the "magician" of the Budget — argues that a second PT term could strengthen public accounts without deep cuts, merely by decelerating expenditure growth. Analysts consulted by Valor are gloomier: a gradual adjustment, they say, is no longer viable. Against this backdrop, the Federal Court of Accounts made a surgical intervention by ordering the government to begin presenting debt trajectory projections that consider the lower bound of the primary result target — that is, the floor of what the fiscal framework rule permits. The measure forces the government to show, in upcoming budgets, the most pessimistic plausible scenario within its own rules, something markets had been demanding for months.

To cover the gap in 2027, the government has already earmarked a risky bet: R$22.4 billion to be raised through an extraordinary pre-salt auction, according to the annex of the budget bill sent to Congress. The revenue would be crucial to deliver the first primary surplus in years. The bet has some operational backing — Ibama authorized Petrobras, whose ADRs trade on the NYSE, to drill three additional exploratory wells in the Foz do Amazonas Basin, in ultra-deep waters off Amapá, according to a document seen by Reuters. Lula plans to mention this discovery, alongside rare earths, in his September 7 speech as a symbol of national sovereignty, weaving a narrative of natural wealth that contrasts vividly with the debt figures Campos Neto has just published.

The external backdrop offers Brazil little relief. Oil is approaching US$100 per barrel, pressured by the escalation between the United States and Iran and by reduced traffic through the Strait of Hormuz, according to InfoMoney. While higher oil prices favor Petrobras's revenues and the pre-salt auction itself, they also reignite global inflationary pressures that complicate the Federal Reserve's rate-cutting trajectory — and, by extension, limit the Brazilian Central Bank's room to loosen monetary policy further. The stronger-than-expected U.S. payrolls release on Friday had already pushed the dollar to R$5.128, up 0.49% on the session, with the B3 closing flat. Even so, funds managed by BlackRock and JPMorgan Asset Management, according to Folha, continue to see value in emerging markets precisely because sovereign bonds in developed economies face selloffs. The MSCI index for emerging market equities rose 1.7% on Monday, hitting its highest level since June 25, with the South Korean won leading the asset class's currencies.

On the trade front, the drop in beef exports is the day's most concrete surprise. Foreign sales fell to 225,000 tons in August, a 23% contraction versus the same month of 2025 and a 29% drop from the record 317,000 tons registered in June, when advance purchases heated the market. The cause is direct: reduced Chinese buying. The data arrives at a moment when Italy, newly converted to the Mercosur-European Union agreement, projects €14 billion in additional exports to the South American bloc over the next ten years — an estimate that ICE Agency president Matteo Zoppas considers undersized, as it counts only tariffs and ignores the removal of non-tariff barriers. The geometry of Brazilian foreign trade is therefore being reconfigured: less China in animal protein, more potential Europe in the overall balance.

Household financial stress permeates almost every segment of the economy. An unprecedented study followed by Folha quantified the impact of online betting (bets) on the delinquency and credit access of its users — damage that adds to an already deteriorated picture. Large retailers restricted their consumer credit lines in the first half, with six chains analyzed by Valor financing R$40.95 billion, a real decline of 1.2%, to avoid larger provisions for doubtful debtors. Desenrola 2.0, extended through the end of August, has not resolved the structural problem either: a study by economists from UFRJ and FGV concluded that the program's first edition had only a temporary effect on household indebtedness and encouraged riskier behavior on the part of institutions that extend credit to high-risk borrowers — creating moral hazard that new rounds of the program tend to aggravate. In this environment, the Central Bank published new rules for contesting Pix fraud, allowing users to attach police reports and screenshots directly within banking apps — a direct response to Kaspersky's warning about a virus capable of altering QR codes on Brazilian e-commerce sites without leaving traces.

Tax reform, in turn, has moved off paper but not off doubt. Companies across all sectors are debating the rules of the new system even after the constitutional amendments were approved and extensive regulations published, according to Folha. The first lawsuits focus on benefits for specific segments, with limited impact on revenue collection — a sign that litigation will be atomized rather than frontal. One of the reform's most significant revelations is the emergence of a new category: nano-entrepreneurs, very low-income workers whose registration the new legislation contemplates. A study commissioned by Natura and conducted by economist Fernando Blumenschein estimates there are at least 19 million such workers in Brazil. This universe connects to another data point released by the Ministry of Labor: roughly 30% of existing MEIs are former formally employed workers who migrated to the corporate regime — a structural PJ-ization that tax reform will have to absorb without creating fresh imbalances.

In the coming hours and days, the market will be watching Tuesday's Focus Bulletin to check whether inflation expectations remain anchored after the oil surge, the Copom decision the following week, and the outcome of the dispute over Tecon 10 at the Port of Santos — where Cade president Diogo Thomson de Andrade requested a fresh analysis of the pre-established agreement between Maersk and MSC with BTP, blocking for now an operation approved without restrictions by the council's technical division. In the background, the question dominating every scenario is the same: whoever assumes the Planalto Palace in January will inherit a fiscal arithmetic that Campos Neto, with rare frankness, has already described as openly hostile.
