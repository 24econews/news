# Chile: Economic Pulse — 2026-10-20

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-10-20*

The AFP Fund E crisis has erupted this Monday as the most disruptive phenomenon on Chile's economic landscape so far this year, bringing together in a single episode the frictions between pension risk management, regulatory oversight, and monetary policy at a moment when the dollar is losing ground and copper is facing its own headwinds.

The cumulative real decline of 10.39% so far in 2026 for the system's most conservative fund —precisely the one intended for those approaching retirement— has triggered an unprecedented political and judicial reaction. Two affiliates filed protection appeals with the appellate courts of Santiago and Puerto Montt against AFP Capital and AFP UNO, respectively, alleging arbitrary and illegal omission in the administration of their savings, breach of fiduciary duty and, in the case of AFP UNO, failure to deliver the four-monthly statement for the May–August period. The appeals request, among other measures, the restitution of the depreciated sums and a no-innovation order. The Senate is weighing calling a special session, and ruling-coalition legislators announced the creation of an investigative committee that would target the role of former Superintendent of Pensions Osvaldo Macías.

The technical origin of the controversy is precise: a significant share of the Fund E deterioration is explained by the AFPs' heavy exposure to U.S. interest-rate derivatives —particularly swaps— which extended the duration of their portfolios beyond what was required to match a life annuity. Both the Banco Central and the Superintendencia de Pensiones had been warning about this risk at least since the previous year. Fernando Mejido, an academic at UST and member of the Superintendency's Technical Investment Council, was blunt: the AFPs "were speculating on the behavior of U.S. interest rates." Frente Amplio and the Communist Party announced bills to curb the fund managers' latitude, while La Moneda did not rule out pursuing its own legal action, stating that "no one plays games with Chileans' savings."

What further complicates the picture is that the episode is unfolding just as the local market begins to price in the possibility that the Banco Central may raise its policy rate this year. Consultancy Gemines warned that the real exchange rate is misaligned with its fundamentals, a pressure the central bank cannot ignore. The dollar eased on Monday toward $980, recovering some ground versus prior sessions, driven by a mild recovery in the dollar index and the impact of France's political crisis on European sovereign markets —a reminder that external tensions remain relevant variables for the local currency. Should the Banco Central opt to raise rates, the effect on fixed income —where Fund E concentrates its portfolio— would be an additional sensitivity factor for the fund managers.

The mining sector adds its own layer of complexity to the picture. Escondida, the world's largest copper deposit operated by BHP, reported a 23% drop in production in the third quarter versus the same period last year, attributed to lower ore grades, adverse weather in July and August, and the temporary suspension of activities following the death of a worker on September 23. Even so, BHP maintained its annual guidance. In parallel, the end of the strike at Antofagasta Minerals' Centinela mine —after ten days of stoppage— eased pressure on the copper price, which retreated from the previous Monday's US$ 6.723 per pound, its second-highest nominal level in history, to US$ 6.575. Goldman Sachs, however, raised its price forecast for the metal, and Antofagasta Minerals' CEO, Iván Arriagada, bet on the Southern Cone's potential to supply between 35% and 40% of global production, conditional on institutional agility in Chile, Argentina and Peru. The most urgent flank in state mining is Codelco's Potrerillos smelter, idled since 2025 after a chimney collapse: legislators, mayors and unions from Atacama went to La Moneda on Monday to ask President Kast to intervene and prevent the permanent closure of a facility that supports more than four thousand jobs.

On the fiscal front, the week opens with two highly contentious legislative processes. The 2027 budget for regional governments remains in limbo after a tie in subcommittee, with Finance Minister Quiroz seeking to unblock the negotiation before the tiebreaker scheduled for Tuesday. Simultaneously, amendments to the Capital Markets Reform bill (MK4) revealed that the fiscal cost of Fonavi and the voluntary housing savings mechanism jumped from $260.677 billion to $943.795 billion —close to US$ 960 million— driven in part by the VAT exemption for fund managers. The Chilean Association of Municipalities warned of cuts in health and social programs, and economist Daniela Sugg argued that the real increase in the Health Ministry's budget is 2.4%, not the 4.7% claimed by the government, due to a change in the comparison baseline.

With the gradual implementation of the Reconstruction Law underway —the SII and Treasury began on Monday to roll out financial relief measures and activity incentives— Chile continues to rank 24th among 38 countries analyzed by the Tax Foundation on tax competitiveness, although the reintegration of the income tax is cited as a relevant advance. In aviation, September broke a 13-month losing streak with a 0.2% rise in passenger traffic, pulled by domestic flights, although the international segment has posted seven consecutive months of declines, dragged by Brazil. Latam Airlines, whose CEO Roberto Alvo tops the digital reputation ranking of IPSA managers according to a Brandex measurement, warned that fuel costs are running near US$ 190 per barrel, well above the US$ 130–147 projected, a pressure that threatens the regional airline industry's margins regardless of what happens in the Middle East.

What to watch this week: the courts' ruling on the protection appeals against the AFPs, the outcome of the regional governments' budget negotiation, the vote in the Finance Committee on the MK4 bill —with Fonavi as the central axis— and any signal from the Banco Central regarding its monetary policy stance in the face of exchange-rate pressure. The confluence of these factors will determine whether the Fund E episode remains a contained controversy or becomes the catalyst for a structural reform of the pension system under the Kast administration.
