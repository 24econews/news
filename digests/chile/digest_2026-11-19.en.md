# Chile: The Economic Pulse — 2026-11-19

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-11-19*

The Chilean dollar broke above $1,007 for the first time in 22 months, and the Central Bank confirmed that GDP contracted 1.1% in the third quarter, worse than anticipated: those two figures, landing on the same day, capture with brutal precision the moment Chile's economy is passing through.

The copper slide is the thread tying both data points to global markets. The red metal plunged as much as 4.6% on the London Metal Exchange, touching US$5.72 per pound, its lowest level since April and extending its losing streak to nearly 9.4% over seven consecutive sessions. The trigger was not a conventional macro print but a reassessment of the role artificial intelligence plays as a driver of industrial metals demand: markets began questioning whether enthusiasm for copper as a critical input for data centers and electronics had been pricing in, too quickly, a demand that has yet to materialize. Tin, another metal tied to the AI ecosystem, fell as much as 8.2% in the same session. Ole Hansen of Saxo Bank described the move as a technical capitulation: "it looks like people are throwing in the towel after breaking through many technical levels." For Chile, whose exports are structurally dependent on the red metal, the simultaneity of a weak peso and copper in free fall represents a particularly adverse combination.

The blow landed at the worst possible moment for the government of José Antonio Kast, who was in Shenzhen attending the APEC Summit and defending before the forum's leaders a framework of "free and fair trade with the same rules for everyone." The president assured that 2027 "will be a good year" for Chile, a line that was being tested that very day by the Central Bank's data. Third-quarter GDP fell 1.1% year-on-year, against the 0.8% contraction that the Imacec readings had signaled. Mining collapsed 14.7%, investment retreated 6.7% —its steepest drop since the fourth quarter of 2020— and the manufacturing sector barely advanced 0.2%. Only agriculture, fisheries and power generation delivered positive readings. Rodrigo Cruz, economist at Banco Santander, noted that "the surprise was not only in the drop in mining, but also in a more widespread weakness across sectors and in the deterioration of investment," and estimated that GDP will close the year with a slight contraction, which would make it the worst print since 2020. The chief economist for Latin America at Banco Itaú, Andrés Pérez, was more direct in warning that "the starting point for 2027 is weakening."

From the Finance Ministry, macroeconomic coordinator Alejandro Guin-Po ratified a growth forecast of 3.3% for next year, arguing that since July a recovery has been visible in the non-mining component of the economy. That figure, however, is facing growing skepticism. The political climate surrounding the budget debate in the Senate added another layer of tension: Finance Minister Jorge Quiroz had to make significant concessions —including a US$160 million contribution to the Autonomous Pension Protection Fund— to secure the necessary votes and reinstate Article 3 of the bill, which authorizes the Executive to take on public debt of up to US$25.6 billion in 2027. That article had previously been rejected in the lower house, and its reinstatement with 31 votes in favor was made possible by unexpected support from Communist Party senators, in addition to the DC and the PPD. Meanwhile, the possibility of a constitutional impeachment of Quiroz remains in play, although President Kast played it down from China with a rhetorical question: "Does an interpellation solve the problems?"

Against that tense budgetary backdrop, the Santiago Stock Exchange closed sharply lower, below 10,400 points, pressured both by the copper rout and by the behavior of SQM, whose shares suffered heavy selling despite the company reporting extraordinary results. The non-metallic miner posted earnings of US$1,569 million in the first nine months of the year, a 288% jump from the same period of 2025, driven by the rebound in lithium prices. In the third quarter, its Novandino division —the former SQM Salar, now a joint venture with Codelco— produced 73,300 tons of the mineral, 16% more than a year earlier. CEO Ricardo Ramos projected global lithium demand growth of 30% this year, with prospects of surpassing three million tons by 2026. Still, the market reacted negatively to the price guidance, which showed a sequential moderation: the average selling price in the quarter was US$19.6 per kilo, against US$21.8 in the second quarter. SQM's paradox —spectacular results, share price under pressure— illustrates the difficulty facing the critical minerals sector when long-term narratives collide with the current phase of the cycle.

The cyberattack against payments fintech Khipu, detected on Monday and contained, according to the company, with no asset damage or data leakage, added another note of uncertainty to the day. The firm reported the incident to the National Cybersecurity Agency and proceeded to replace some 150 internal services, though it did not specify how many merchants were affected or the attacker's initial access vector, details that will continue to raise questions about the resilience of local fintech infrastructure.

On the energy front, the CEO of Enel Chile, Gianluca Palumbo, ramped up pressure on the regulator by demanding formal remuneration for the services that provide grid stability. Palumbo pointed to the "cost of indecision" and recalled that the Electric Coordinator has already proposed three ancillary services to the regulator, with no decision yet taken. The debate intersects with Chile's commitment at COP31 to reach 35% electrification of global energy consumption by 2035, a target the government backed while warning that it requires significant investment in transmission, storage and distribution.

On pension funds, the Superintendency of Pensions revealed for the first time the concrete impact on pensions of the drop in Funds D and E: 937,000 retirees are in Fund D and 157,000 in Fund E, with older participants the most hurt by excess-duration strategies that several analysts have already described as herd behavior. AFP PlanVital defended its positions in Fund E, arguing that "there is not a single comma of duration regulation," which former superintendent Alejandro Ferreiro considered insufficient as an explanation.

What lies ahead is a pile-up of fronts. The Senate must clear this Thursday the final line items of the 2027 Budget. Markets will be watching the U.S. inflation print to confirm whether the Fed has room to cut in December —a decisive factor for the dollar index, which climbed 0.3% to fresh 20-month highs— and, with it, for the Chilean peso. Copper will need a concrete signal of physical demand to break its losing streak. And the government will have to convince markets, before the year is out, that the third-quarter contraction does not foreshadow a 2026 in the red.

## Corporate Watch

### SQM (NYSE: SQM / Santiago: SQM-B)
The miner reported cumulative earnings of US$1,569 million through September, a 288% year-on-year jump, driven by lithium, which accounted for 72% of revenue and 76% of gross margin. Despite the results, the stock sold off on signs of moderation in the mineral's price and the global bearish pressure on metals tied to the artificial intelligence narrative.

### Bci (Santiago: BCI)
The bank announced a reshuffle of its top executive line: Eugenio von Chrismar will step down as CEO of the bank on January 18, 2027 to take over as CEO of holding company Bci Group, which is preparing a tender offer for the bank's shares; Gerardo Spoerer, a 30-year veteran of the institution, will take the CEO role at the bank. The transition coincides with the consolidation of a holding structure designed to manage Bci and City National Bank of Florida separately.

### CMPC (Santiago: CMPC)
The Matte group's pulp and paper arm cut production at its Campos Novos plant in Brazil, with around 70 workers departing, while facing a fresh environmental dispute over its Natureza megaproject —valued at US$4.6 billion— as it seeks to modify the boundaries of a protected reserve to move the initiative forward.
