# Chile: The Economic Pulse — 2026-11-12

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-11-12*

The open-ended truckers' strike that has blocked key routes across the country has turned the price of diesel into the fulcrum of a crisis that simultaneously touches fiscal policy, the stability of the government, and Chile's export credibility — and Wednesday's events made clear that none of those fronts is close to resolution.

The sixth consecutive fuel price hike triggered by the Mepco price stabilization mechanism, effective Thursday — $41.9 per liter for diesel and $38.7 for 93 and 97 octane gasoline, according to ENAP — will push prices to historic highs and lit the fuse of a negotiation the government failed to close. Interior and Segegob biminister Claudio Alvarado led a marathon of meetings at La Moneda that culminated in an expanded proposal: reversing this week's hike, freezing fuel prices for roughly 90 days through February, and committing by law to a transport registry. The Executive even withdrew — and promised to re-submit to Congress — the price stabilization bill in order to incorporate the new concessions. Even so, the Confederación de Transportistas Fuerza del Norte did not stand down. "The strike continues, indefinitely," its leader Cristian Sandoval declared upon leaving the Palace, demanding the elimination of the specific fuel tax — a threshold the government is unwilling to cross.

The economic weight of the conflict is hard to overstate. The trucking sector employs roughly 250,000 people, counts more than 81,000 taxpayers registered with the SII — three out of four classified as micro or small businesses — and moves 92% of national freight, representing roughly 6% of GDP, according to Diario Financiero. Airlines Latam, SKY and JetSmart have already warned passengers of delays in transfers to affected airports, while Chilealimentos president Juan Manuel Mira publicly cautioned that the blockades generate direct costs for agribusiness exporters and erode Chile's reputation as a reliable supplier, calling for ports to be declared critical infrastructure. Juan Araya, the long-standing leader of the Confederación Nacional de Dueños de Camiones and a reluctant participant in stoppages, was more bluntly direct: "The small carrier today is bankrupt."

The strike arrived at a moment of macro vulnerability that the government itself acknowledged this week. In an interview with Bloomberg, Finance Minister Jorge Quiroz admitted for the first time that Chile is in a technical recession, and set out a target of 4% GDP expansion for 2028. Economists polled by Diario Financiero received the goal with skepticism, noting that without additional measures — especially the streamlining of investment permits — the figure looks out of reach. The unemployment rate for the July-September quarter stood at 9.8%, with more than one million jobless, the highest number since the pandemic, and employment fell 1.1%, its sharpest contraction since early 2021. An analysis by Horizontal published today reveals that workers over 50 now take more than 12 months to return to employment, versus nine months in 2019. Former Finance Minister Andrés Velasco, at Marsh's 2026 Risk Forum, was scathing: "Neither the right nor the left has shown much interest in employment."

SOFOFA, which wrapped up a tour of the country's 16 regions, identified a diagnosis that repeats from Arica to Magallanes: permitting obstacles, lack of legal certainty, and infrastructure gaps are holding back projects that, according to the business group, could create more than 170,000 direct jobs and bring unemployment down from 9.6% to 8%. The signal from capital markets, by contrast, was more upbeat: Moneda Patria's Renta CLP fund raised US$54 million in a new capital injection oversubscribed 4.2 times, pushing assets above US$1.1 billion. And a Santander survey found that 62% of Chilean companies increased revenues over the past year, above the study's 50% global average, suggesting corporate resilience coexists with a macro deterioration that disproportionately hits the most vulnerable segments.

On the external front, President José Antonio Kast is leading a business delegation to China, Vietnam and Japan in the context of the APEC summit and Chile Week, with a formal bilateral meeting with Xi Jinping on the agenda. The tour comes at a moment when lithium — of which Chile accounts for 17% of global supply, according to Cochilco, down from 19% in 2025 — has doubled in price, even as the country continues to lose share to Australia and other competitors. Codelco, for its part, signed a memorandum of understanding at LME Week in London with Kutch Copper Limited, a subsidiary of Indian billionaire Gautam Adani's Adani Group, to advance exploration at the Kaburkaala copper project in the Calama mining district. The initiative represents the state-owned company's bid to attract international private capital for exploration projects, but it triggered an immediate political storm: Quiroz's comments to Bloomberg on the possibility of incorporating private capital into Codelco over the long term unleashed an opposition offensive in the Senate that branded the idea "disguised privatization," forcing the government to clarify that state control would remain intact.

In local markets, the Chilean stock exchange suffered a late-session drop that contrasted with the rally on Wall Street, driven by strong results from CoreWeave, a company specializing in artificial intelligence infrastructure. The Chilean peso closed stable after rising $13 on Tuesday on the back of the US inflation surprise; with no cues from US fixed income — closed for Veterans Day — the exchange rate found no fresh catalysts. In the pension system, new regulations that forced the AFPs to close out interest rate swap positions are estimated to have crystallized losses of between US$3.4 billion and US$3.7 billion in October, equivalent to between 0.9% and 1.6% of the funds, though the decline in rates also contributed positively to portfolios.

What comes next matters on several fronts at once. Thursday is the immediate key date: the government must re-submit the fuel stabilization bill to Congress, the historic trucker leaders have a meeting scheduled with the Executive, and the ENAP price hike takes effect regardless of what happens at La Moneda. Beyond the short term, the Kast government's ability to deliver a structural solution for the trucking sector — without sacrificing the fiscal discipline Quiroz has flagged as a priority — will determine whether this conflict is closed out this week or becomes the first major political wear-and-tear of the election year ahead.
