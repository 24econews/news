# Chile: The Economic Pulse — 2026-10-26

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-10-26*

What began as a financial controversy confined to the AFPs' Fund E has escalated this week into a systemic crisis of political, regulatory, and reputational proportions that threatens to redefine Chile's pension debate for years to come. The Chamber of Deputies will vote this Monday on the creation of a special investigative commission after the 62 required signatures were gathered on Sunday, led by PPD independents, to shed light on losses exceeding US$7.7 billion over the 2018-2026 period. What started as an asset management episode has become, according to Diario Financiero's own editorial summary, "a regulatory and political case."

The depth of the reputational damage is laid bare by the isolation in which the administrators now face scrutiny. Economists, former authorities, and even figures historically aligned with the system have distanced themselves. José Antonio Guzmán, former chairman of AFP Habitat and the CPC, acknowledges that "there was a lack of control by the boards and a belated reaction," that "unnecessary and imprudent risks were taken," and that, in this particular case, voluntary compensation "would apply," though he warns that the actual damage has been magnified and that over 45 years the system has delivered "spectacular returns." From the opposite side, UDP academic Salvador Valdés argues with technical precision that the first validity requirement to justify the purchase of derivatives in funds D and E — replicating the portfolio of life insurance companies — was simply not met in 2025, and that the correlation between annuity prices and 20-year bonds was a mere 8% at monthly frequency that year. That figure dismantles the industry's central defensive argument.

The Kast administration, for its part, has backed the sanctioning process initiated by the regulator without extending bridges to the AFPs, a stance that closes the perimeter of political support available to the sector. In that context, AFP Capital's general manager, Renzo Vercelli, defended in an interview with Diario Financiero the investment strategy executed since his arrival in August 2024 — which led to a drop of more than 10% in Fund E between January and September — and stated that he would make the same decisions again, conceding only that they should have pushed harder for the creation of a Fund F. It is a stance unlikely to ease legislative pressure.

The pension crisis is dominating the headlines, but it does not exhaust the week's economic landscape. The Santiago Stock Exchange closed with a weekly loss of 2%, its lowest level in four months, amid a broad-based risk-off mood that connects local turbulence with the sustained rise in international oil prices. Next week will mark the start of the third-quarter earnings season for IPSA companies, with utilities, Coca-Cola Andina, and Parque Arauco the first to report. Those numbers will land against a backdrop of more fragile investor sentiment.

On the sovereign front, Moody's joined Fitch and S&P this week in affirming Chile's credit rating, highlighting the government's commitment to fiscal spending adjustment and improved prospects for 2027. It is a signal of institutional stability that contrasts with the strains in the pension market, though it does not neutralize them. Delinquency on real estate tax payments has exceeded pandemic-era levels, according to Treasury data, with all arrears indicators rising as of June compared to the same period last year. It is a silent thermometer of the financial stress on households and firms that persists despite the recovery narrative.

In mining, the medium-term horizon has its own complexity. Large-scale mining will face 31 collective bargaining processes in 2027, with Codelco accounting for 16 of them and BHP three, including what promises to be the most demanding negotiation of all: the one between the Australian miner and Union No. 1 of the Escondida mine. In that scenario, the growing presence of KoBold Metals in Chile takes on added relevance. The U.S. company, founded in 2018 and backed by Bill Gates, Jeff Bezos, and Sam Altman — along with BHP as a strategic investor — uses artificial intelligence, proprietary sensors, and historical geological data to identify copper, lithium, and nickel deposits. Its CTO told Diario Financiero that Chile is "definitely underexplored," a statement that resonates powerfully considering that KoBold has close to 50 global projects and a mine worth over US$2.3 billion under construction in Zambia.

The investment agenda also offers institutional good news. The National Investment System has reduced the average approval time for public projects to 111 days, the lowest figure in five years, a step forward that SOFOFA has welcomed even as it insists that environmental permitting remains the main obstacle to private investment. The business association's president, Rosario Navarro, after touring the country's 16 regions, identified common obstacles ranging from a lack of legal certainty to infrastructure gaps, and warned that materializing the projects currently under environmental review would reduce national unemployment from 9.6% to 8%, creating more than 170,000 direct jobs. At the same time, the Coquimbo desalination plant is facing warnings from experts over key works lacking environmental assessment, which could result in delays and higher costs, although the Ministry of Public Works for now rules out risks to the project's timeline.

The week ahead will be dominated by the Chamber's vote on the AFP investigative commission, the start of the IPSA earnings season, and the 2027 budget debate, where friction has already emerged over the Sence tax credit, funding to promote Chilean pisco abroad, and cuts to the cultural sector. The capital markets reform, which moved to the floor in a swift first-stage process, is expected to be voted on the week of October 26 before heading to the Senate.
