# Chile: The Economic Pulse — 2026-10-23

The collapse of Fondo E —the most conservative multi-fund in Chile's pension system, designed precisely to shield the savings of those on the verge of retirement— has become the epicenter of a crisis that extends beyond finance and threatens to reshape the political debate over pension reform just as the government of José Antonio Kast unveils its own AFP overhaul.

The slump in the most conservative fund was, in theory, not supposed to happen. The long-dated fixed-income instruments that compose it should act as a buffer against volatility. But the swap strategy adopted by the fund managers —betting on a decline in U.S. rates that never materialized as quickly as expected— turned that supposed haven into a trap. Fintual, which has just officially confirmed the creation of its own AFP under the government's pension framework, claims it warned about this excessive risk-taking back in 2023, adding an extra layer of institutional accountability to the scandal. The Superintendencia de Pensiones has already opened sanctioning proceedings against all seven AFPs, and Labor Minister Tomás Rau today urged members close to retirement not to make hasty decisions: "We need to see this investigation through, see whether there will be sanctions, whether there will be restitution or not," he said from La Moneda. For those who cannot afford to wait, the recommendation is to understand the differences between programmed withdrawal and life annuity before acting. Vector Capital's deputy head of fixed income, Agustín Lara, was more blunt: "Under no circumstances is it advisable to switch from this fund into Fund A," he warned —a signal that Fund E's partial October recovery could deepen if the rate environment continues to improve.

And here lies a striking paradox. On the very day pension savers face the consequences of having bet on falling long-dated U.S. rates, that decline is finally happening —but for the wrong reasons. Global markets endured a risk-off session fueled by fears of an escalation in the Middle East conflict and renewed doubts about the artificial intelligence outlook. The Nasdaq tumbled 2.6%, with Meta falling nearly 7% and SpaceX more than 9%. The 10-year Treasury yield shed 16.8 basis points to drop below 5%, its classic safe-haven role activated once again. In Chile, the IPSA posted its sharpest drop since May, sliding 1.88% to 10,780 points, dragged down by the same global storm. The dollar closed higher above $985, also pressured by surging Brent crude —now above US$112 a barrel— while copper fell 1.8% in London, delivering a double blow to an economy whose fiscal health depends to a significant degree on the red metal.

That structural dependence is precisely what crystallizes the day's second major storyline: Chile's inability to break through its long-term growth ceiling. The Latin American Consensus Forecast projects average growth of just 2.3% for 2032-2036, exactly the same figure estimated six months ago —suggesting that no recent data has moved the needle on structural expectations. For 2026, the picture is even bleaker: the October Consensus Forecasts cut its projection from 0.8% to 0.2%, cementing Chile as the second most lagging economy in Latin America this year, trailing only a contracting Bolivia. Economist Alejandro Urzúa, of FEN UNAB, puts it succinctly: "We are practically stagnant, and that has concrete consequences: weaker investment momentum, difficulties generating employment and, most worryingly, consumption that continues to show weakness."

The productive status quo is being challenged on multiple fronts. SOFOFA wrapped up a tour of the country's 16 regions with a unanimous diagnosis: lack of legal certainty, slow permitting processes and infrastructure gaps are the common obstacles from Arica to Magallanes. According to the business association, executing the projects currently under environmental review would bring the unemployment rate —currently at 9.6%— down to 8%, creating more than 170,000 direct jobs. The Ministerio de Obras Públicas announced measures to shorten tender timelines from six to four months, an official acknowledgment of the problem. And the government launched the Estrategia Nacional de Demanda Laboral por Inversión (Enadi), a platform to coordinate labor supply with the investment project pipeline, in a ceremony led by President Kast himself, who spoke of a "jobs emergency" but also of "light at the end of the tunnel."

That reactivation narrative will be the centerpiece of Kast's address at Wednesday's Enade, his first appearance at the country's most important business gathering since taking office. The message, prepared alongside Finance Minister Jorge Quiroz, will seek to position a "historic opportunity" to recover growth and employment —a framing that must contend with the hard reality of projected 0.2% growth in 2026 and persistent weakness in domestic demand. Former Finance Minister Ignacio Briones has no doubt that 2027 will be better than this year "if only due to the base effect," but warns that to restore the country's structural potential "much remains to be done."

On fuels, the $91.5-per-liter diesel price hike that took effect Thursday raised tensions in the transport sector, although the Confederación Nacional de Transporte de Carga ruled out a strike, opting for dialogue after reaching an agreement with four ministers to implement an automatic tariff adjustment mechanism. Fenabus, however, declared itself on alert, warning that companies must choose between paying for fuel or meeting financial obligations, with a US$1.4 billion leasing portfolio at risk of default. The Mepco —the fuel price stabilization mechanism— has notably less headroom than under previous administrations: the government has margin to absorb roughly $300 per liter, versus the US$4.5 billion former Finance Minister Marcel had at his disposal. That fiscal constraint is real and pressing.

On the external front, the Subrei signaled that the amendment to the foreign investment screening bill could be submitted to Congress in early 2027, following talks with 20 countries, including China. Simultaneously, SOFOFA took a convergence agenda with Peru to Lima as part of the XXI Reunión del Consejo Empresarial Chile-Perú, where the business association pushed for private-sector participation in the upcoming binational cabinet and proposed that Chile serve as a bridge between the European Union and the CPTPP. The first formal meeting of foreign ministries under the Kast and Keiko Fujimori governments —held this Thursday in Lima— reinforced that agenda with agreements on security, migration and border cooperation.

What to watch closely in the days ahead: the tone of Kast's Enade speech on Wednesday and how the Chilean business community —arriving at that forum with the IPSA at a four-month low and the dollar above $985— processes the combination of reforms underway with growth that still refuses to take off. At the same time, the Superintendencia de Pensiones investigation into Fondo E could deliver its first formal findings in the coming months, determining whether there will be restitution and, with it, the real scope of political damage to the pension system as a whole.
