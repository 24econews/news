## Part 1 — Main Narrative

Chile hit a threshold this Friday that the labor market had been dreading for months: the unemployment rate for the August-October quarter reached 10%, its highest level since March-May 2021, with 1,036,290 people out of work —170,000 more than a year ago— and four consecutive months of net job destruction. The figure did not arrive in isolation: it landed on the same day the IPSA closed its best week since June, breaking past 10,700 points, and the dollar retreated below $1,040 after having posted the previous day its second-highest level on record. The contradiction between a rising stock market and a deteriorating labor market precisely captures the economic moment Chile is living through.

The INE's data triggered an immediate political reaction. Finance Minister Jorge Quiroz, Labor Minister Tomás Rau, and the Economy Minister publicly acknowledged the "gravity" of the situation, though the government sought to soften the blow by noting that, in quarter-on-quarter terms, 76,000 jobs were created —the largest gain since January 2025— and that female participation reached an all-time record of 53.8%. The opposition, by contrast, directly challenged Quiroz in Congress, pinning the job destruction on the government's economic agenda. Economist David Bravo, former head of the Labor Reactivation Roundtable, was more categorical: he urged the Executive to send a bill to Congress as a matter of urgency, warning that administrative emergency measures are insufficient. At the Annual Industry Meeting, SOFOFA chair Rosario Navarro, in her final act leading the association, proposed to the President a tripartite agreement between businesses, workers, and government, and insisted on shortening permit processing times as an enabling condition for investment to translate into actual employment. Business associations, for their part, circulated a list of 30 measures to create jobs without going through Congress, all of them executable by administrative means.

That the stock market closed higher in this context is not paradoxical: it reflects the fact that third-quarter 2026 corporate earnings have been, on the whole, solid. Aggregate net income of the 31 IPSA companies rose 23.6% year-over-year to $3.59 trillion in the period, with a weighted gain of 26.8%. Five companies concentrated half of those profits, while another five posted losses, a dispersion that illustrates a two-speed economy. The IPSA's advance was also in tune with global markets: on a half-holiday session for Thanksgiving in the United States, the Nasdaq gained 2.3% and the S&P 500 advanced 1.6%.

In currency markets, the Chilean peso recovered ground. The dollar fell $6.45 to $1,034, driven by copper's rebound after signals of support from China to its domestic markets, though the greenback tried to bounce at midday when the unemployment news prompted defensive buying and a pickup in U.S. sovereign yields. The exchange rate nonetheless remains above the $1,000 threshold it crossed on November 18, and not far from the all-time high of $1,051 posted on July 14, 2022.

Two decisions by the Antitrust Court (TDLC) also marked the day. The TDLC ordered Cencosud and Walmart to jointly pay US$67.3 million —$69.940 billion— in consumer indemnifications for collusion in the fresh chicken market between 2008 and 2011, a case that had been sanctioned in the infraction proceedings in 2019 and upheld by the Supreme Court in 2020.
