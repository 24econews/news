# Chile: Economic Pulse — 2026-11-23

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-11-23*

Chilean copper has never been as exposed to a single climatic factor as it is today, and the warning issued this week by consultancy CRU makes it hard to ignore: in an extreme El Niño scenario, Chile could lose up to 225,000 tonnes of copper output in 2027, a figure equivalent to roughly two percent of global production and one that, if realized, would further strain an already structurally deficient global copper market. CRU also estimates that the storms of July and August this year have already stripped 65,000 tonnes from supply, turning the climate phenomenon into a first-order operational variable for miners, their financiers, and the governments that depend on royalties from the red metal.

The climate threat arrives at a moment when Chile is accumulating contradictory signals about its ability to attract and retain productive capital. On one hand, the Singaporean trader that this week took a one hundred percent stake in a mining company in Atacama to develop a copper and gold project confirms that foreign interest in the resources of the north remains very much alive. On the other, the litigation pitting Empresas Copec against Axxa Chemicals — a dispute involving a claim of more than 10 billion pesos over chemical supply contracts for mining, and in which Axxa has sealed a new alliance that allows Saudi Aramco to expand its penetration among miners that buy diesel — reveals the frictions that emerge when long-term businesses tied to large-scale mining reorganize themselves. That two decade-long partners end up in arbitration and in court is, in itself, an indicator of the degree of competition now taking hold in the mining supply chain.

Against that backdrop, the Asian tour of President José Antonio Kast — which included meetings with Xi Jinping at a moment when the Chinese leader was simultaneously starring in a historic summit with Donald Trump and Vladimir Putin — takes on a concrete commercial dimension. Chile needs China, its largest copper buyer, to maintain robust demand precisely as domestic supply risks are rising. The deepening of trade ties agreed during the trip is, in that sense, both a diplomatic bet and an economic insurance policy.

The week also brought news that illustrates the other face of the country's development strategy: that of technological Chile. Paula Bellizia, Amazon Web Services' vice president for Latin America, confirmed that the four-billion-dollar investment committed by AWS over fifteen years is backed by a government that sees Chile as a regional technological hub. Cencosud, Ripley and Latam Airlines already operate with the company in the region, suggesting that the digital infrastructure of major Chilean corporations is migrating to the cloud at a pace that just three years ago was difficult to anticipate. Along the same lines, the startup Ainwater closed a funding round with a Paraguayan fund and the investment vehicle of Karina von Baer to expand into Brazil, where it will apply artificial intelligence to the monitoring of water treatment plants. These are small but cumulative signals of an entrepreneurial ecosystem that, as Sebastián Acevedo of ChileGlobal Ventures acknowledges, "keeps its foundations intact," even if those raising capital have suffered in recent quarters.

On the fiscal front, the government rolled out one of the less-publicized articles of the Reconstruction Law: the voluntary retirement incentive for public employees, with six thousand slots opened and projected savings of 120 million dollars. It is a measure aimed squarely at consolidating current spending in a year in which the Finance Ministry faces the pressure of a tight budget and in which, according to the weekly summary from Diario Financiero, there is a risk of negative GDP in 2026 and upward pressure on the dollar that complicates the cost equation for importers and companies indebted in foreign currency.

The interest of Brisa Group — the Portuguese giant that manages more than 1,500 kilometers of highways — in entering the Chilean concessions system, with meetings already held before the MOP, the Concessions Directorate and InvestChile, reinforces the perception that road infrastructure remains an attractive asset for international operators. It is a relevant data point at a time when Manuel José Balbontín, vice president of Vinci Compass, declared from Madrid that Chile "is taking measures that, taken together, are pro-growth and pro-investment," though with the caveat that the country must demonstrate over the next two or three decades that it respects and preserves its institutional framework.

Finally, the green hydrogen industry closed the week with a harsh assessment. Mario Marchese, former director of HNH Energy, attributed the sector's retreat to the fall in international demand, high infrastructure costs and oversized projects, acknowledging that the industry "sinned through enthusiasm." The pivot toward the domestic market, he warns, will require state support and regulatory changes that are not currently on the agenda. It is a chapter that is closing slowly, but one that compromises part of the export diversification expectations that Chile had built around its advantages in solar and wind energy.

What comes next will shape the trajectory of several of these vectors at once. The market will be watching closely the evolution of the exchange rate and any signal from the Central Bank on monetary policy in response to the inflationary pressure implied by a stronger dollar. On the mining front, climate models looking ahead to 2027 will be scrutinized with an intensity that, until recently, was unthinkable in the boardrooms of the major copper producers. And on the commercial front, the concrete results of Kast's Asian tour — measured in verifiable investment commitments — will be the acid test of whether the government's economic diplomacy is translating bilateral meetings into real capital flows.
