# Chile: The Economic Pulse — 2026-11-11

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-11-11*

The indefinite trucker strike that has paralyzed routes in at least nine regions of the country for two days has crystallized, in just a few hours, nearly all the tensions defining Chile's current economic moment: a government without fiscal space to absorb external shocks, an economy that markets no longer expect to grow in 2026, and a logistics chain whose fragility is laid bare precisely as the fresh fruit export season begins its countdown.

The Central Bank's Economic Expectations Survey released this week delivered the day's harshest signal: the median of surveyed economists now projects 0% GDP growth for 2026, a third consecutive downward revision from the 1.3% anticipated in July. The possibility of a contraction—with a floor of -0.3%—is no longer a tail scenario. It is the lower bound of consensus. The 2027 projection holds at 3%, but that figure coexists with 11-month expected inflation of 3.5%, above the Central Bank's target, which narrows monetary policy's room to stimulate demand. Former Finance Minister Rodrigo Briones, speaking at a Fynsa event, was even more cautious: he projected 2.8% growth for next year and warned that the reconstruction law is insufficient to achieve trend GDP growth of 4%.

It is against this backdrop of fragility that one must read President José Antonio Kast's decision to submit, under immediate discussion, a bill to cap fuel price increases at CLP 20 per liter for 90 days. The initiative responds directly to the strike called by the Confederación de Camioneros Fuerza del Norte and the Confederación Centro-Sur, whose indefinite walkout has blocked port access in San Antonio and Valparaíso and partially cut off Route 5 and Route 68. Talks at the Ministry of Transport ended without agreement; leaders of the No+TAG movement confirmed that mobilizations will continue until they secure concrete commitments on fuels, the specific tax, and tolls. The Executive's legislative response, to be voted on Monday the 16th in the Chamber floor, aims to buy time but does not resolve the underlying equation: the Fuel Price Stabilization Mechanism (Mepco) was deliberately activated in March to pass the international oil price increase through to consumers, a decision the government took to protect public finances and that is now coming due politically. Brent crude, which closed around US$92, has fallen from recent highs on signs of possible diplomatic relief in the Middle East, but still shows a year-to-date gain of roughly 40% since the conflict erupted in March.

The impact on the agri-industrial chain is immediate. The industry association Frutas de Chile warned that the strike threatens the country's reputation as a reliable supplier in markets where it competes with other origins, noting that this is the most critical moment of the year. The Sociedad Nacional de Agricultura rejected the blockades but acknowledged the cost problem. Lo Valledor is assessing the impact on the availability of fresh produce. British importer Worldwide Fruit, which expects to receive around 7,000 tons of Chilean avocados in the 2026-2027 season, illustrates precisely how much is at stake: disruptions in export logistics at the start of the season can affect long-term contracts with international buyers who have alternatives.

The fuel dispute overlaps with a budget debate that has yet to close. The Chamber rejected the Interior Ministry's allocation—the second consecutive day of tension in the discussion of the 2027 Budget Law—with legislators pointing to cuts in Senapred and the Cholito Law. The Finance allocation was approved in general terms, though amid sharp questioning of Minister Jorge Quiroz over staffing reductions at the SII, Customs, and the Treasury. In parallel, the CUT, Confech, and other organizations of the Frente Común announced mobilizations for November 11, 18, and 27, centered precisely on the budget cuts. The government is simultaneously facing a truckers' strike over prices, a parliamentary opposition rejecting its budget allocations, and a social movement calling marches: three distinct fronts with a common root cause, which is the perception that fiscal adjustment is being distributed regressively.

In mining, the outlook is no more reassuring. Copper production fell 8.5% year-to-date through September according to Cochilco, with the ninth month of the year posting a 15% decline to 385,100 tons. César Pérez Novoa of BTG Pactual warned that weather, inputs, and the labor market could jeopardize copper production in 2027. The Federación de Trabajadores del Cobre, meanwhile, launched a harsh critique of Codelco board chair Bernardo Fontaine, accusing him of carrying out a smear campaign to pave the way for privatization of the state miner. The controversy comes at a delicate moment: Barrick suffered a fresh setback when the Second Environmental Court suspended for 30 days the resolution approving its El Alto exploration project, in the same Pascua Lama area, following claims from communities arguing that the area is subject to a definitive and final closure ordered by the Supreme Court.

Against this accumulation of adverse signals, some relevant counterweights are emerging. GasAndes sealed firm contracts of up to 35 years with shippers in Argentina, awarding between 50% and 100% of its available capacity in its first open season since the pipeline was built, amid the production boom in Vaca Muerta. It is an energy milestone with long-term implications for the country's supply security. DP World agreed to extend its concession at the Port of San Antonio through 2041 in exchange for a US$227 million investment that will add 300,000 TEU of annual capacity, reinforcing the logistics infrastructure of the country's main cargo hub at a moment when that chain is under stress. Chilean AFPs saw their losses on interest rate swaps narrow after the rebound in U.S. Treasury bonds, a technical reprieve for a sector that had built up large positions betting on a yield decline that never materialized. And Banco BICE raised more than US$108 million in perpetual bonds at a 3.93% rate, a signal that the local capital market retains depth even in an environment of uncertainty.

What lies ahead is a week of high institutional intensity. Monday's vote on the fuels bill is the first test: if it fails to secure enough support or if truckers reject the measure as insufficient, the conflict could escalate to passenger transport sectors that have already threatened to join in. The 2027 Budget debate will continue with new allocations, and the release of the October Imacec—whose median forecast points to zero change—could confirm that the economy has entered a zone of real, not just statistical, stagnation. In that scenario, pressure on the Central Bank to act and on the Executive to revise its fiscal strategy will become difficult to resist.
