# Chile: The Economic Pulse — 2026-11-18

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-11-18*

The Central Bank on Tuesday put at more than US$14 billion the combined losses —both realized and unrealized— accumulated by the AFP multi-funds through September on their positions in US interest rate derivatives, a figure that far exceeds previous estimates and that turned the session into one of the densest of the year in terms of signals about the country's financial health.

Pensions Superintendent Joaquín Cortez appeared before the Chamber of Deputies to break down the anatomy of those losses: US$10 billion corresponded to the decline in the value of swaps still outstanding as of September, plus US$4.175 billion in losses already incurred on closed contracts. That sum of US$14.236 billion was reduced to US$10.796 billion by October as rates partially eased and the regulator's new rules began to take effect. Even so, incurred losses —those that can no longer be reversed— climbed to US$6.993 billion as of November 5, the date on which the implementation of the new regulation was completed. For Fund E, the system's most conservative portfolio and the one at the heart of the public controversy, the total loss stood at US$1.476 billion at the start of November, of which US$1.162 billion is already unrecoverable.

The Central Bank, in its semiannual Financial Stability Report released the same day, placed the episode in a systemic frame. Chile's derivatives market, which as of December 2025 reached a notional amount equivalent to 5.5 times GDP, is exceptionally deep for an emerging economy —in April 2025 the volume traded in interest rate derivatives equaled 4.6 times GDP, compared with one time in comparable emerging economies— and pension funds are among its most important participants. Central Bank President Rosanna Costa was even more direct in her appearance before the Senate Finance Committee: with long-term potential growth around 2%, Chile operates with margins so narrow that external shocks make it "very possible" that the economy will drift toward negative figures. She stopped short of uttering the word recession, but she did not rule it out either.

The FSR's diagnosis does not end with the AFPs. The report identified as the main external risk a possible reassessment of expected returns on artificial intelligence investments, whose growing debt-financed funding could trigger an abrupt correction in global markets with contagion effects on Chile via sovereign spreads, currency depreciation and capital outflows. The warning arrived on the same day that Wall Street closed at lows not seen since July —the S&P 500 in the red with all eyes on Nvidia's earnings— and as a fresh wave of digital outages hit the US electronic intelligence agency and Apple services, feeding concerns about the fragility of global tech infrastructure.

Against that backdrop, the dollar remained the most visible thermometer of the tension. After brushing against $1,000 in the two previous sessions, the currency closed around $993, pulling back from that psychological threshold in a session marked by US retail sales that beat expectations and kept alive the possibility that the Federal Reserve will raise its rate again in December. Analysts consulted by Diario Financiero noted that the $1,000 barrier is revealing more about the structural weakness of the peso —with copper offering no support— than about genuine dollar strength. For the IPSA, the threat of a dollar sustained above that level splits the index: exporters with dollar revenues such as CMPC, Copec and SQM-B would be net beneficiaries, while retail, beverages and Entel would face greater cost pressures.

SQM, which trades both on the Santiago Stock Exchange and the NYSE, illustrated with its own numbers the other side of the moment: the lithium miner's earnings reached US$1.569 billion in the first nine months of 2026, a 288% year-on-year increase driven by solid demand for the metal key to the energy transition. The result confirms that Chile continues to capture value from the lithium boom, although export concentration in a single commodity —also visible in President Kast's tour of Shenzhen for the APEC CEO Summit, where he highlighted that bilateral trade with China topped US$60 billion last year— raises questions about the long-term diversification that the president himself sought to address by bringing, for the first time, a delegation of technology companies to ChileWeek.

Fiscal policy added its own share of uncertainty. The 2027 Budget debate in the Senate kicked off with the approval of ten line items, but immediately got tangled up in the dispute over the Autonomous Pension Protection Fund (FAPP): the Finance Ministry argues that the US$160 million contribution is not due in 2027 but in 2028, contradicting a ruling by the Comptroller General that sided with the fund. The opposition is already threatening a constitutional impeachment of Minister Quiroz, which adds a first-order political variable to a budget negotiation that is already complex. In parallel, the Universal Daycare bill advanced in the Chamber's Finance Committee —with urgent processing and a floor vote scheduled for Wednesday— adding spending pressures that the Finance Ministry will have to square in a context of more expensive global funding.

A note of relief came from the electricity industry: the National Energy Commission confirmed an average 4.4% cut in electricity tariffs starting in January 2027, slightly above the preliminary projection, thanks to the expiration of supply contracts signed in 2008 and 2010 at prices near US$100 per MWh and their replacement by contracts awarded between 2022 and 2025 at an average of US$67 per MWh. The disinflationary effect comes on top of the fuel price freeze approved by Congress, which economists estimate could shave up to 0.2 percentage points off CPI and bring annual inflation below 5% after October's 4.5% reading.

What the market will be watching in the coming days is threefold: Nvidia's earnings and their impact on global risk appetite; the speed with which the Senate untangles the FAPP knot without derailing budget approval; and the dollar's behavior around the $1,000 threshold, which depending on what the Fed does in December could stop being a ceiling and turn into a floor.
