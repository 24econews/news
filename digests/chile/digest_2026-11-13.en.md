# Chile: The Economic Pulse — 2026-11-13

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-11-13*

President Kast's government closed a deal with the main trucking associations on Thursday that ends three days of road blockades, but did so at a fiscal cost that could reach US$900 million and that implies freezing fuel prices through March 31, 2027 — a concession that Finance Minister Jorge Quiroz himself had ideologically resisted until Wednesday. It is the central story of the day: a right-wing government that came to power promising fiscal discipline and market pricing, yielding to pressure from blocked highways on the very same terms as its predecessors.

The final terms of the agreement, announced by Interior bi-minister Claudio Alvarado alongside Quiroz, contemplate reversing the $42-per-liter hike on diesel and the $39 hike on gasoline, adding an additional $100 cut to the former, and setting a price ceiling through the end of March next year. For diesel in the Metropolitan Region, that implies a maximum price of $1,452 per liter, a drop of roughly 9% from the current level. The bill, reintroduced this morning in the Chamber of Deputies after being withdrawn on Wednesday, must be dispatched by Tuesday under the commitment of Senate President Paulina Núñez. Not all mobilized groups stood down: the No+Tag movement rejected the agreement, alleging unequal treatment, and announced it will maintain its actions in Santiago.

The backdrop to the crisis is revealing. Chile, according to GlobalPetrolPrice data cited by La Tercera, has the fifth-lowest diesel price in the OECD, nearly 10% below the global average. The hike that triggered the strike was not arbitrary: it reflects the impact of the armed conflict between the United States and Iran that began in February, which pushed global oil prices higher and lifted local diesel by $675 since then. That a country with comparatively subsidized prices came to the brink of shortage — with 70% of routes monitored by the Cámara Nacional de Comercio moving at speeds under 15 kilometers per hour, cold chains compromised, and more than 30% of trucks dispatched since Monday failing to reach their destination — illustrates how fragile national logistics are in the face of any disruption.

The pressure on public finances is not arriving alone. In Congress, the 2027 Budget debate is piling up setbacks: the Chamber on Thursday rejected the Mining allocation — in a 59-59 tied vote resolved against — amid a debate dominated by the future of Codelco and the possibility of opening the state-owned copper producer to private capital. The Segegob allocation also fell, with votes from the opposition and from the libertarian ruling bloc, in a rejection that mixes fiscal arguments with criticism of the government's communications performance. That Congress is simultaneously racing the clock on both the fuels law and the Budget, in sessions that stretch past midnight, gives the measure of the institutional tension of the moment.

While the executive was negotiating with the truckers, markets were responding to their own signals. The dollar rose more than $12 at the close and again brushed $997, driven by remarks from Donald Trump that pushed back the prospect of a nuclear deal with Iran, which sent U.S. sovereign yields up more than 10 basis points and strengthened the global dollar to its highest levels since April. The dollar index touched 103 points, while copper futures fell sharply on the London Metal Exchange, trading below US$6.21 a pound. With the Fed pricing in a 25-basis-point hike in December and another in January, and the Banco Central de Chile with no room to move its 4.50% rate, the spread compresses and the peso finds no support.

In that adverse context, the IPSA managed to close above 10,600 points thanks to a singular catalyst: Entel jumped nearly 11% after confirming, via a material disclosure signed by its CEO Antonio Büchi, that it had received a non-binding offer from Millicom International Cellular for the entirety of its subsidiaries Entel Perú S.A. and Americatel Perú S.A. The transaction, described as being in advanced negotiations, could be valued between US$1.54 billion and US$1.848 billion, according to market estimates based on a multiple of five to six times the US$308 million EBITDA that the Peruvian subsidiary posted in 2025. That unit contributes more than 35% of Entel's consolidated revenues, operates 10.5 million mobile lines, and lifted its earnings 45% in the first nine months of 2026. For Millicom — which already absorbed Movistar Chile this year — the acquisition would complete its footprint across 13 Latin American markets and consolidate its position as the second-largest regional operator in scale after América Móvil.

The session held other heavyweight corporate developments. ENAP's board accepted in an extraordinary session the resignation of Julio Friedmann as CEO, just four months after his ratification, in an exit the executive himself described as a personal decision. The move is striking given that under his management the state-owned firm posted US$1.112 billion in earnings between January and September 2026, an all-time high. Friedmann will remain in his role until February 5, 2027. In parallel, Chilean renewables firm Verano Energy confirmed the entry of Brookfield as a strategic partner, in a deal that will drive US$1.6 billion in investments over the next five years to develop 2.5 GW of solar and battery storage capacity, mainly in Chile and Colombia. And in the liquefied gas market, four players — funds Patria and Vinci Compass, U.S. infrastructure firm Stonepeak, and a consortium between the Von Appen Burose family and asset manager CMB Prime — submitted binding offers for the Gasmar terminal, a sign that international appetite for energy infrastructure assets in Chile has not waned despite regulatory uncertainty.

What lies ahead merits attention on several simultaneous fronts.
