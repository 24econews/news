# Argentina: The Economic Pulse — 2026-11-24

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-11-24*

Jensen Huang's return to Nvidia triggered on Monday a global rebound of a magnitude not seen in recent memory, one that reached all the way to Buenos Aires — even though the local market remained closed for National Sovereignty Day — and handed Argentine assets the most badly needed respite in months, just as they had been piling up weeks of accelerated deterioration.

The Nasdaq climbed 6.5% and Nvidia shares surged 31.6%, snapping a seven-session losing streak, after President Donald Trump publicly pressured the chip giant's board to reinstate Huang following his resignation over personal scandals. Against that backdrop of tech euphoria, Argentine ADRs listed in New York rallied across the board: Globant advanced 9.5%, Satellogic 7.1%, and Grupo Galicia and Cresud each gained 5.5%. Country risk, which had brushed 1,070 basis points on Friday — its highest level since October 2025 — pulled back 30 basis points to close near 1,035, with an intraday low of 1,015.

That the relief is welcome does not mean it resolves anything. The week ending Friday, November 20 laid bare the structural fragility of Argentina's position with a brutality that the Financial Times was quick to document: in just four months, the sovereign yield spread went from 402 basis points — the eight-year low set in July — to over 1,070, a 166% escalation that the London paper explicitly attributed to the sliding popularity of President Javier Milei, doubts over his 2027 re-election, and uncertainty surrounding the debt payment schedule. Milei's approval, which hovered around 40% in January, has dropped to roughly 30%, according to the article. The implicit re-election odds, estimated from the long end of the Globales curve, fell from 90-92% in mid-July to around 40%, according to local analysts: essentially a coin toss.

Finance Secretary Federico Furiase insisted throughout the week that the deterioration reflects the global backdrop — rising rates, a tech sell-off, instability in emerging markets — and that Argentina does not need external financing because its financial program for this year and next is already covered. "Argentina seems to be coming from the future," he went so far as to say, comparing the local fiscal surplus with the deficits piled up by developed economies. But more critical economists, such as Alberto Ades — whom Milei himself invited to a Cabinet meeting in October — point out that "the problem lies more in the debt than in the dollar," and that the market is not questioning the 2027 maturities but rather those of 2028, 2029 and beyond, which are the ones exposed to a potential change of administration.

The most unsettling signal of the week was not country risk but the FX gap. The contado con liquidación nearly touched 1,800 pesos, the blue-chip dollar hit 1,675, and the wholesale rate closed at 1,580, leaving a 13.6% gap between the official exchange rate and the CCL — the widest since April 11, 2025, the final day of currency controls for individuals. The Argentine peso was the second worst-performing emerging-market currency over the past 30 days, trailing only the Brazilian real, according to GMA Capital. Measured at the CCL, the peso lost 8.5% over that period, compared to the 3.5% official depreciation. Brazil, where Congress is debating Lula's fiscal adjustment plan with skepticism, compounds the picture: if the real stays weak, Argentina's real multilateral exchange rate appreciates, hurting regional exports that depend on the Brazilian market — which absorbs 35% of foreign sales from regional economies including wine, garlic, olive oil, and tobacco.

As markets digested that storm, INDEC will release Tuesday the September EMAE, the data point that will determine whether Argentina has officially entered a technical recession. Consulting firms are already anticipating that it has: Equilibra estimates monthly seasonally adjusted growth of just 0.5% in September, insufficient to offset the 2.8% drop in July and the marginal 0.1% recovery in August. For the third quarter to avoid coming in below the second — which already showed a 0.6% contraction — September would have needed to post a 8.5% gain, a figure that exceeds by more than three times the largest monthly advance recorded throughout 2026. The government is ruling out any change of course: more fiscal and monetary tightening, with no consumption stimulus that could put pressure on the dollar or on inflation. The room for orthodoxy, however, narrows as the elections draw closer and household finances fail to improve.

Sector-level data reinforce that diagnosis. The metalworking industry used just 41.8% of installed capacity in October, the lowest reading for that month since 2016. The year-to-date figure shows a 5% year-on-year decline. In the footwear sector, Arluchi announced the closure of its Chivilcoy plant and the relocation of production to Brazil. Since Milei took office, the universe of registered firms has shrunk by 32,290, with the sole exception of Neuquén, where the dynamism of Vaca Muerta added a net 59 companies. Against that backdrop, agroindustry exported USD 47.802 billion in the first ten months of the year, up 14.2% from the same period in 2025, although the real multilateral exchange rate is already retreating from the previous annual average.

The week's most strategically ambitious development came from the energy sector: the government confirmed the approval of the Argentina LNG project under the RIGI framework. The initiative — led by YPF, Italy's Eni, and the UAE's XRG — contemplates investments of USD 50.750 billion through 2050, with USD 25 billion slated for 2027-2030. Minister Caputo described it as "the largest investment in Argentine history." The final investment decision, according to YPF president Horacio Marín, is slated for November 30. Tesla also entered the local market with five versions of its Models 3 and Y, at an event attended by President Milei and in which Elon Musk participated via video call from an airplane — a gesture that mixes economic openness with the narrative of courting global investors that the government has turned into a political instrument.

What to watch closely in the coming days: the September EMAE, which could confirm the technical recession and add financial pressure; the trajectory of the Brazilian real and the decision by Brasília's Congress on Lula's fiscal adjustment, whose approval or rejection will move the multilateral exchange rate and directly affect Argentine export competitiveness; the dynamics of the CCL and the FX gap, which the market tracks as a thermometer of pre-election confidence; and November 28, the date on which the International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea will rule on the precautionary measures requested by Argentina to halt the Sea Lion oil project in the Malvinas, a diplomatic front with the potential for real operational impact, given that two contractors have already withdrawn from the project.
