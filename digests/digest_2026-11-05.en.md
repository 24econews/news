# Argentina: The Economic Pulse — 2026-11-05

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-11-05*

Argentina's country risk closed Wednesday at 852 basis points, its highest level in more than a year, on a session that lays bare the central tension defining the country's economic moment: macroeconomic fundamentals are improving on paper, but investors are increasingly pricing in the political risk of 2027. What makes this divergence newsworthy today is not simply that country risk keeps climbing — it has been doing so for weeks — but that it did so against a strong tailwind from Wall Street. The Dow Jones rose nearly 3% following the Republican Party's defeat in the US midterm elections, US Treasury yields eased, and yet Argentine sovereign bonds averaged a 1.1% decline, energy sector stocks collapsed by as much as 8.5% in New York — Vista Energy led the losses, YPF fell 7.1% to USD 41.61, its lowest since April — and the Merval index retreated 2.4% in pesos. Argentina traded in the opposite direction from the world.

The underlying explanation is political. Economist Fernando Marull put it precisely: of the roughly 400 basis points by which the sovereign spread has widened since mid-year, half reflects global factors — geopolitical tensions, high rates in developed markets, the uncertainty surrounding the Middle East conflict that drove oil prices higher — and the other half is purely Argentine, a repricing of the odds of Javier Milei's reelection. According to that analysis, four months ago markets assigned close to a 90% probability to the continuity of the program; today that figure has fallen to around 50%. The "Lula effect," as several local analysts call it, operates as a mirror: the Brazilian runoff that anointed the PT leader generated weeks of punishment for the São Paulo market, and investors in Buenos Aires fear that the October 2027 presidential election will replicate that pattern of prolonged uncertainty with assets under pressure. The Brazilian real, which reached 5.50 per dollar before recovering slightly to 5.46, serves as a regional thermometer of that nervousness.

The paradox is that the Democratic victory in Washington, initially read as positive for global markets by implying a brake on the Trump administration's fiscal deficit and a decline in long-term rates, was processed in Buenos Aires as an additional problem. A Congress in opposition hands limits Trump's room for maneuver to deploy discretionary financial support such as the swap the US Treasury arranged last year. The Export-Import Bank ratified on Wednesday, at an Atlantic Council event in Washington, the continuity of its financing framework for the Andes-Atlantic Corridor — up to USD 7 billion through 2027 — and its president John Jovanovic emphasized that "no one voted last night against more jobs for Americans," signaling the bipartisan independence of that institution. But markets priced in the uncertainty before reading the nuances.

Against that backdrop, Minister Luis Caputo this week deployed the largest political offensive toward the productive sector since the start of the administration. At the 32nd Industrial Conference of the Unión Industrial Argentina, he presented a package of four measures: suspension of ARCA attachments on SMEs until May 2027, extension of the installment plan to 18 payments for tax debts due through October 31, the ability to use export credit balances to cancel customs obligations, and a Banco Nación credit line for automobile purchases. Additionally, he opened negotiations with governors to eliminate the Stamp Tax, offering compensation equivalent to 50% of the lost revenue. The reception was one of relief but without euphoria: UIA president Martín Rappallini warned that the industry has accumulated nine consecutive months of decline, that nearly 95,000 industrial jobs have been lost, and that macroeconomic stabilization "is fundamental, but it is only the beginning." The UIA simultaneously presented its own project, the so-called Federal Industrial Fiscal Pact or RICI, which calls for a tax reduction similar in scale to RIGI but oriented toward the manufacturing sector, with 30 years of fiscal stability.

Within that context sits the ambivalent signal from credit. Consumer loans fell for the sixth consecutive month in October — credit cards contracted 2.1% in real monthly terms, personal loans 0.1% — while mortgage loans grew 2.3%, the only category with momentum. Dollar loans, which had grown steadily for nine months driven by exporters, showed a striking deceleration. Caputo rejected at the FIEL conference the notion that the solution is monetary expansion, arguing that banks are not lending due to uncertainty rather than lack of liquidity. Meanwhile, the Government withdrew from the Chamber of Deputies the entirety of the Financial Competitiveness Law bill after an article emerged proposing to tax CEDEAR investments under the Income Tax — in an episode that illustrates the costs of legislative improvisation at moments of fragile confidence — though it confirmed that the text will be corrected and resubmitted.

The BCRA bought only USD 9 million on Wednesday, after two sessions without intervening, in a week that adds multiple signals of FX slowdown. Since January it has accumulated purchases of USD 15.973 billion, exceeding by more than 50% the annual target of USD 10 billion agreed with the IMF, but the pace is noticeably decelerating: it has intervened in only three of the last eight sessions. The wholesale dollar closed at 1,546 pesos, with the official exchange rate still more than 26% away from the ceiling of the FX band, set at nearly 1,989 pesos for the end of November. The Government also intervened in the peso bond market, with unusual purchases that pushed volume above 3 trillion pesos in a single session — a record on BYMA — depressing yields on long CER bonds in a move that the market interpreted as preparation for the November auctions, a month of heavy maturities.

The heterogeneity of the real economy is another thread running through the session. Vaca Muerta continues to operate in a parallel universe: the province of La Pampa launched a productive integration plan with 15-year tax incentives; the Neuquén Industrial Park expects to generate 25,000 jobs; and Pampa Energía reported that its production at Rincón de Aranda reached 26,000 barrels per day, an all-time high, on track for 45,000 barrels once the USD 2.7 billion Vaca Muerta Sur project is activated. At the other extreme, Metalfor, a Córdoba-based agricultural machinery manufacturer with debts of 52 billion pesos to 23 banks, filed for reorganization proceedings, another signal of the crisis affecting the sector. Workers at Pauny, another tractor factory in Las Varillas, occupied the plant demanding two months of unpaid wages. Auto production fell 14.4% year-on-year in October, the seventh consecutive decline, and capital goods imports have accumulated a 13.9% contraction in volume in the first nine months of the year — an unmistakable signal that private investment in equipment remains absent.

From Madrid, where Santander hosted its Latin American banking conference, came voices of external support that do not always find correlation in prices. Kristalina Georgieva, managing director of the IMF, cited Argentina alongside Greece, Portugal and Ireland as examples of successful fiscal discipline, with the phrase "populism must be beheaded." Ana Botín, chair of Santander — which has 5 million clients in Argentina — stated that "the trend is clearly positive" and emphasized the importance of "consistency" in policies. That institutional backing contrasts with the market punishment registered by Argentine assets, a gap that Marull himself summarized: if the economy is stagnant, the risk of policy change rises, and if that risk rises, the sovereign spread rises with it.

What to watch closely in the coming weeks is threefold: the evolution of country risk around 850 points as a function of new domestic political signals — particularly any definition on candidacies or coalitions for 2027; the speed of implementation of SME relief measures and their effect on credit data and fourth-quarter industrial activity; and the outcome of November's peso debt auctions, which will test the Treasury's ability to roll over maturities in a context of greater distrust. The Belgrano Cargas negotiation — extended to December 21 after Grupo México's withdrawal — and the YPF litigation before the US Supreme Court, where 15 states filed amicus curiae in favor of the plaintiffs, add background noise to a picture that already has enough with electoral uncertainty.
