# Uruguay: Economic Pulse — 2026-11-11

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-11-11*

The Mercosur-European Union agreement has been in force for barely six months, but the foreign trade data from that period already reveal a tension that defines Uruguay's economic moment: the country has preferential access to the world's largest market precisely when its domestic economy is showing persistent signs of weakness. That contrast between external openness and internal fragility is the common thread running through Uruguay's current conjuncture.

Activity numbers tell a story of ups and downs that, taken together, point downward. According to data published by El Observador, the economy contracted 1.1% in August versus July, reversing the modest recovery recorded in July. The second quarter posted a 0.8% quarter-on-quarter decline, a figure that complicated the government's annual growth target, and the third quarter marked another 0.2% contraction. The Ceres Leading Index, which anticipates activity one or two quarters ahead, fell again, reinforcing the signals of weakening that this indicator has been issuing intermittently. The IMF projects growth of just 1.3% for 2026, a figure that the Economy Minister himself, Gabriel Oddone, described as subject to downward revision. "There is a fairly high probability that we will revise the growth projected for 2026 downward," Oddone said, as reported by La Diaria — an unusual admission of vulnerability that local fixed-income markets quickly priced in.

Against that backdrop, the contrast with trade openness is particularly striking. Exports to the European Union during the first six months of the Mercosur-EU agreement moved, depending on the sector, from an "incipient" impact to "notable growth." For agribusiness, meat and the dairy industry, the European channel offers a decompression valve at a time when the domestic market is not generating sufficient traction. The latest auction on the Global Dairy Trade platform recorded an average 3% rise in dairy prices, a positive signal for a sector that moves more than USD 3.1 billion annually and encompasses some 550 companies. That price improvement coinciding with the period of greatest trade fluidity with Europe is no accident: the agreement is beginning to reshape the geography of Uruguayan exports.

The government's response to the activity slowdown has been pragmatic and moderate in scope. The Ministry of Economy and Finance announced measures to reduce costs and streamline procedures to facilitate foreign trade, complementing that package with initiatives aimed at stimulating private investment, which, in the Ministry's own words, shows signs of "modest growth." On the fiscal front, the Fiscal Advisory Council analyzed what it called worrying breaches in the 2024 public finances, adding pressure to the maneuvering room of Oddone's economic team. The government also issued regulations setting out the limits of the global minimum tax in line with fiscal stability clauses, a signal to large corporate investors regarding the predictability of the tax framework.

On the financial front, one of the most relevant data points of recent months was a USD 1.697 billion bond placement, which Oddone described as successful and as proof of the country's credibility. Consistent with that strategy, 92% of this year's sovereign debt issuance was placed in local currency, consolidating the de-dollarization of public liabilities that the Central Bank has been promoting for years. The BCU president highlighted that process as one of the most significant structural achievements of his administration. The Financial Stability Committee, for its part, noted that the financial system "is well prepared to absorb hypothetical adverse scenarios," an assessment that took on additional relevance in the context of Paraguay's recent banking crisis. In a move aimed at modernizing the financial architecture, the BCU presented a draft bill to create an open finance system, in line with international standards already operating in more developed markets.

The external environment adds complexity. The trade war between the United States and Canada is generating pressures that, according to El Observador's coverage, are spreading throughout the hemisphere. Uruguay, whose export structure depends on the stability of regional and global flows, is not immune to that turbulence. Senator Tabaré Viera warned that Brazil's economic slowdown will impact Uruguay, a reminder that the vulnerability of the bloc's main trading partner has direct consequences for Montevideo. The government, aware of that risk, is betting on geographic diversification of exports and on the EU agreement as buffers.

What will continue to shape the debate in the coming weeks is the Rendición de Cuentas, which Parliament has begun to address in the Senate amid clashes between the government and the opposition over macroeconomic projections and adjustment timelines. Oddone requested an additional USD 1 billion margin to finance the budget, arguing that projection deviations are normal in any economy. The final vote on that budget document, progress on the competitiveness bill in the Chamber of Deputies, the trajectory of the price index in a context where inflation reached 4.9% year-on-year in October but remains historically low, and the first concrete steps toward OECD membership will be the indicators to watch in the short term.
