# Uruguay: Economic Pulse — 2026-10-15

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-10-15*

The drop in poverty to 14.8% in the first half of 2026 arrives at an awkward moment for President Yamandú Orsi's government: the same period in which multiple signals point to a decelerating economy, analysts are trimming their growth forecasts, and Economy Minister Gabriel Oddone himself has publicly acknowledged there is "a fairly high probability" that this year's projections will need to be revised downward. The tension between an improving social indicator and a weakening economic cycle defines the most revealing moment in Uruguay's current juncture.

The reduction in poverty — which according to El Observador reached 14.8% in the first half — is politically welcome for the Frente Amplio, but it comes amid an underlying controversy: the Ministry of Economy publicly questioned the figures from the Instituto Nacional de Estadística, pointing to "inconsistent poverty and employment results." The methodological dispute between the MEF and the INE is no minor technical matter; it erodes the credibility of the data precisely when the government most needs favorable figures to justify its fiscal roadmap in the Rendición de Cuentas.

That Rendición de Cuentas is, in itself, another open front. Oddone presented a financing request for USD 1 billion that the Coalición Republicana refused to approve, forcing the minister to declare that he will not give up on negotiating. The Consejo Fiscal Autónomo, for its part, warned Parliament about an "overestimation" in the MEF's projections, reinforcing external doubts about the fiscal trajectory. In parallel, the Ministry activated the safeguard clause to raise the debt ceiling, a signal that room to maneuver is narrowing amid anemic growth.

Activity data confirm this diagnosis. The economy grew just 0.8% in the first quarter of 2026 year-on-year, according to the Banco Central, a figure that represents a modest rebound from the virtual stagnation of 2025 — a year in which annual growth reached 1.8%, almost entirely explained by statistical "carryover." The Ceres Leading Index, which posted two consecutive months of mild expansion during spring, has fallen again more recently, reinforcing signs of weakening. Analysts polled by local media have again trimmed their 2026 forecasts, with consultancies like CPA Ferrere estimating growth of just 1%, well below the official target.

July's recovery, described by El Observador as "mild," is not enough to shift the narrative. The diagnosis emerging from the Ceres index — an economy entering 2026 "decelerated and without clear signs of growth" — matches warnings from independent economists who note that Uruguay's macroeconomic stability, acknowledged even by IMF Managing Director Kristalina Georgieva in a recent visit, is not translating into productive dynamism. As BBVA summarized in a recent analysis, Uruguay faces the challenge of transforming stability into growth, a gap that has become structural.

Against this backdrop, the competitiveness debate takes on added weight. The government is preparing changes to the investment regime and is debating in Parliament a competitiveness bill — described by Deputy Andújar as "interesting though improvable" — that seeks to lower costs without deregulating the market. At the same time, the MEF announced measures to stimulate private investment, which is only showing "signs of modest growth." The agricultural sector, facing falling soybean and rice prices and still not having recovered the ground lost to droughts in previous years, is pushing for additional FX measures. The president of the Asociación Rural del Uruguay warned that "we are in a critical situation" regarding the drop in the dollar and its impact on export competitiveness.

Political tension within the ruling party itself adds noise. President Orsi pushed back against Oddone to make clear that economic policy "ultimately always rests in the hands of the president," a signal that frictions between the Presidency and the economic team — though contained — are not insignificant. The debate over the AFAPs, which the government announced it will reform within the framework of the Diálogo Social, is already reviving differences within the Frente Amplio and generating criticism over the lack of attention to the party's "social base."

On the external front, Uruguay continues to be perceived as an anchor of regional stability — its country risk is the lowest in Latin America, and Standard & Poor's raised its investment grade by two notches in recent years — but U.S. pressure on the country to sever trade ties with China, revealed by Minister Oddone himself, places Montevideo before a geopolitical dilemma that could complicate its access to key markets. China is not Uruguay's top goods export destination, according to data cited by El Observador, but the trade relationship with Beijing is strategic for the agricultural sector.

What to watch in the coming weeks: the evolution of negotiations over the Rendición de Cuentas and whether the government can build majorities to pass its fiscal program; second-quarter activity data, which will show whether the rebound seen earlier in the year has continuity or fades; and the outcome of the AFAP debate, which could become the first real test between the MEF's economic pragmatism and the redistributive demands of the Frente Amplio's base.
