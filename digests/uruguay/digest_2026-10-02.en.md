# Uruguay: Economic Pulse — 2026-10-02

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-10-02*

The resolution of the port conflict at Terminal Cuenca del Plata was not a mere labor matter: it arrived at the worst possible moment for an economy that has just posted a 0.8% contraction in the second quarter, and whose shipping lines had already conveyed to the government their "deep concern" and "extreme uncertainty" over the port call diversions that the strike was triggering. The fact that the Presidency had to intervene directly to unlock the agreement between Terminal Cuenca del Plata and its union says a great deal about the fragility of the moment: Uruguay could not afford to have its main port continue operating as a source of operational risk in a quarter when activity had already lost ground.

The second-quarter print is the backdrop coloring the entire current economic discussion. The 0.8% drop — confirmed by the Central Bank and widely cited by local and international outlets including El País, Infobae and Cronista — directly complicates the growth target of the Yamandú Orsi government. The contraction has one clearly identifiable driver — the impact of the drought on agriculture, with soybeans and rice as the protagonists of the sectoral collapse — but the deeper problem is that the economy reached the quarter already without genuine momentum. According to analyses gathered by El Observador, 2025 growth was sustained almost exclusively by "statistical carryover," meaning that 2026 started, in the words of analysts themselves, "zero to zero." The Ceres Leading Index has oscillated between signals of recovery and decline in recent months, without establishing a clear trend, and private analysts have again trimmed their growth projections for 2026, with firms such as CPA Ferrere placing expansion at just 1%, well below official forecasts.

This gap between the Ministry of Economy's projections and those of the private sector is precisely what the Consejo Fiscal Autónomo brought before Parliament, warning of an "overestimation" in the MEF numbers presented in the Rendición de Cuentas. The tension between institutional optimism — Minister Gabriel Oddone has stated that the economy "is slowly beginning a path of recovery" — and external technical pessimism is one of the most relevant debate axes of the moment. Oddone himself has acknowledged that there is "a fairly high probability" of revising 2026 projections downward, an infrequent admission from a sitting minister that reflects the honesty with which the Frente Amplio economic team is calibrating its public communications.

The fiscal picture adds further pressure. The MEF activated the safeguard clause to raise the debt ceiling, a signal that room for maneuver has narrowed. Year-end 2024 already recorded a deficit similar to that of 2019 and an increase in public debt of more than ten percentage points of GDP, an inheritance that Oddone recognized from the start of the transition as "more restrictive than any of us imagined." In that context, Fitch Ratings maintained Uruguay's investment grade — a fundamental anchor for access to international financing — but expressly warned about low growth and rising debt. The rating agency, in line with Standard & Poor's, which had previously raised the Uruguayan rating by two notches, appears to value institutional and fiscal stability more than economic dynamism, a distinction that local analysts themselves sum up with the question that titles an El País article: "Stability or stagnation?"

There are, however, elements that temper the gloomier diagnosis. Employment and private sector real wages are growing above the level of activity, suggesting that the labor market retains notable resilience relative to the economic cycle. Instant transfers grew 90% in a year and already account for seven out of every ten interbank transactions, a sign of financial modernization that the Central Bank is deepening with the draft bill to create an open finance system. Uruguay also climbed in the Index of Economic Freedom to fourth place in Latin America. And on the external front, September exports grew 10%, totaling USD 1.398 billion, while the recent bond placement of USD 1.697 billion received demand that Oddone himself described as successful, including the country's first-ever sovereign issuance in Swiss francs.

The government, for its part, is attempting to tackle the competitiveness problem with concrete measures: reducing costs and procedures for foreign trade, a competitiveness bill under parliamentary consideration, and the promise that fuel prices — under heavy international pressure — will not undergo the abrupt adjustment some had feared. The decision to reduce the IMESI discount on fuel purchases along the border with Argentina also aims to recover part of the consumer traffic being lost to the neighboring country.

What will need to be watched in the coming weeks is whether the mild recovery recorded in July by the economic activity index manages to hold up in August and September, closing the third quarter with a positive number that neutralizes the damage from the second. The evolution of the exchange rate — the dollar has been under downward pressure, which the MEF has identified as a "severe" risk to competitivness — and international prices for agricultural commodities will be decisive. The resolved port conflict removes at least one source of uncertainty, but the greater test remains demonstrating that the macroeconomic stability Uruguay has painstakingly built can finally translate into growth.
