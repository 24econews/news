# Uruguay: The Economic Pulse — 2026-10-16

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-10-16*

The question dominating Uruguay's economic debate this week is not whether the country is growing, but whether it will grow enough to sustain what it already has. That question became more urgent with the convergence of three simultaneous signals: Fitch Ratings confirmed Uruguay's investment grade but issued explicit warnings about low growth and the accumulation of public debt; the Ministry of Economy activated the safeguard clause of the fiscal framework to raise the permitted debt ceiling; and the Autonomous Fiscal Council told Parliament what it described as an "overestimation" in the projections presented by the MEF itself in the Rendición de Cuentas. Three different institutions, one underlying diagnosis.

Fitch's decision to maintain investment grade — which Uruguay preserves with singular consistency relative to neighbors that have lost it or never had it — did not come without caveats. The rating agency acknowledged the country's institutional strength and the credibility of its monetary policy, but noted that the pace of economic expansion is insufficient to absorb the spending pressures accumulating on the state. That balance precisely summarizes Uruguay's structural paradox: stability is secured, but the growth that should emanate from it fails to materialize. The economy grew just 0.8% year-on-year in the first quarter, according to Banco Central data, and 2025 closed with annual expansion of only 1.8%, below all official forecasts. Minister Gabriel Oddone, who traveled to the United Kingdom to strengthen financial ties with that market, publicly acknowledged a "fairly high probability" that growth projections for 2026 will be revised downward. Consultancy CPA Ferrere now projects expansion of just 1% for this year — half of what the government estimated at the beginning of the fiscal year.

The MEF's activation of the fiscal safeguard clause adds tension to the picture. The mechanism, built into Uruguay's fiscal rule for stress situations, allows the government to temporarily exceed the established debt ceiling, but its use sends an unmistakable signal: public accounts are under real, not theoretical, pressure. The Autonomous Fiscal Council was even more direct when appearing before Parliament, warning that the MEF's projections presented in the Rendición de Cuentas overstate revenues or understate expenditures — or both — to a degree that compromises the credibility of the budget framework. The fiscal deficit at year-end 2024 was 4.7% of GDP, similar to 2019, with public debt accumulating more than ten percentage points of increase over that same period.

The exchange rate adds another dimension to the problem. The dollar remains depreciated against the Uruguayan peso, a phenomenon the government has acknowledged as potentially capable of "severely affecting the competitiveness" of the export sector. The MEF took measures to mitigate the impact — including extending the Imesi fuel tax reduction to the border zone up to 80 kilometers from the Argentine border — but the agricultural sector, through the Asociación Rural del Uruguay, warned that the situation is at a "limit." The ARU president stated that the minister "understands," but that measures to date are insufficient. The price gap with Argentina — which by some estimates can reach as high as 25% — pressures border zones and erodes retail trade in cities such as Rivera, Artigas, and Salto.

Against that backdrop of low dynamism, the MEF published new potential growth projections through 2032 that, according to analysts, assume an acceleration the country has not managed to sustain over the past decade. The World Bank approved a $300 million loan to promote private investment and employment, reinforcing the official narrative that Uruguay maintains fluid access to external financing on favorable terms. The investment by Spain's Solaria — which announced a mega-project for data centers worth up to $5.7 billion, with a definitive contractual deadline set for May 2027 — constitutes the longest-horizon bet to shift the country's productive base. If it materializes on the announced terms, it would represent an unprecedented transformation in the composition of foreign direct investment.

On the institutional front, the Banco Central presented a draft bill to create an open finance system, while the Financial Stability Committee indicated that the banking system "is well prepared to absorb hypothetical adverse scenarios." Inflation remains at its lowest levels in seventy years, giving the BCU relative operating room, although the Central Bank's president warned that the "ultra-conservative" profile in the management of public resources carries a real cost for the country.

What to watch closely in the coming weeks: the evolution of the competitiveness bill the government is bringing to Parliament, the formal revision of growth projections for 2026, exchange rate dynamics in relation to Argentina — whose macroeconomic stabilization under the Milei framework is the most consequential exogenous variable for Uruguay — and concrete progress on the definitive contract with Solaria. If this year's Nobel in Economics, shared by Robert Hall and Michael Woodford for their contributions to macroeconomic stabilization, holds any lesson for Montevideo, it is precisely this: stability is a necessary but not sufficient condition. Without a growth impulse, it becomes stagnation.
