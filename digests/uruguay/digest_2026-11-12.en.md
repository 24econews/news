# Uruguay: Economic Pulse — 2026-11-12

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-11-12*

The acceleration of Uruguayan inflation to 4.9% year-on-year in October, reported today by El Observador, arrives at the most uncomfortable possible moment for the Frente Amplio government: just as Economy Minister Gabriel Oddone attempts to sustain the argument that the economy has begun a "recovery path," and as analysts have already trimmed their growth forecasts for 2026. The inflation uptick is not, in itself, a crisis, but it complicates the official narrative of stability and adds pressure on a central bank operating in an environment of weak demand.

The macroeconomic backdrop is hard to disguise. The economy contracted 0.8% in the second quarter and shrank another 0.2% in the third quarter, meaning the year closed with a second-half contraction and annual growth well below projections. Oddone himself acknowledged before the Senate that there is "a fairly high probability" that expansion forecasts for 2026 will be revised downward. Private consensus points in the same direction: 2025 growth was explained almost entirely by the statistical carryover from the previous year, which means 2026 begins, in the words of the economists polled by El Observador, "zero to zero." The Ceres Leading Index, which anticipates activity trends, has flashed weakening signals in its latest readings, though an April data point showed a mild rebound of 0.2% after four consecutive months without growth.

Against this backdrop, the government has opted to tackle the competitiveness problem from the supply side. The Ministry of Economy announced cost-reduction measures and red-tape simplification to facilitate foreign trade — a modest but deliberate signal to the business sector. At the same time, Oddone indicated that the government is evaluating changes to the port tariff system, which he himself described as "absolutely inadequate," potentially benefiting a sector that moves more than USD 3.1 billion annually and groups 550 companies, according to data published by El Observador. The competitiveness bill is advancing in parallel through the Chamber of Deputies, though its scope remains subject to legislative debate.

Where Uruguay can indeed display solid credentials is in debt management. 92% of bond issuance during the year was placed in local currency, consolidating the de-dollarization strategy the central bank has promoted for years. The World Bank approved USD 300 million in financing to promote private investment and employment, and the government closed a USD 1.697 billion bond placement that Oddone presented as confirmation of the country's sovereign credibility. Moody's maintained a stable outlook, the IMF praised the "solid macroeconomic policies" in its Article IV Consultation, and Fitch reaffirmed investment grade, though it warned about low growth and rising debt — challenges no rating agency can ignore. The Financial Stability Committee, for its part, noted that the financial system is "well prepared to absorb hypothetical adverse scenarios."

On the sectoral front, the Solaria megaproject is drawing attention. President Orsi convened the intendants of Canelones, Florida and San José to coordinate the initiative, whose head, Legnani, projects that works could begin next year. In renewables, HIF Uruguay's solar park in Paysandú, geared toward green hydrogen production, entered the public consultation stage — a step forward on the energy transition agenda that complements an already consolidated clean generation matrix. The Mercosur–European Union agreement continues to mature: six months after entering into force, export sectors describe an impact ranging from "incipient" to "notable growth," with particular interest in the agri-food chains that INAC is also monitoring closely.

On the domestic financial front, the central bank ordered the dissolution and liquidation of GBU Sociedad de Bolsa, a sign that capital markets supervision remains active. More troubling is the cross-border crisis at Ueno Bank in Paraguay, whose collapse involves not only Uruguayan workers but also the organization of drug trafficker Sebastián Marset, who allegedly laundered USD 4.1 million through that institution, according to La Diaria. The BCU president emphasized the soundness of the Uruguayan financial system in the face of that regional episode.

For the remainder of the year and heading into 2026, investors will need to track three vectors closely. First, the evolution of inflation and the central bank's response to price pressures in a weak-growth environment — a combination that narrows monetary policy maneuvering room. Second, the debate over reform of the AFAP system, which the government has confirmed it will pursue and which is already generating friction within the Frente Amplio itself. And third, the legislative progress of the competitiveness bill and private-investment stimulus measures, whose effectiveness will determine whether Uruguay can transform what is today, in BBVA's words, an externally recognized "stability" into the sustained growth it still lacks.
