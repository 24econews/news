# Uruguay: The Economic Pulse — 2026-08-17

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-08-17*

Uruguay is testing international debt markets at a moment when Economy Minister Gabriel Oddone himself acknowledges there is "a fairly high probability" of revising 2026 growth projections downward, making the timing of the operation the most revealing angle of the day: the country is seeking external financing just as its economic momentum weakens.

According to El Observador, the government conducted investor soundings to assess conditions for a return to international debt markets, a move that fits within a broader liability management strategy. La Diaria previously reported that the government had already executed a debt operation combining issuance, reopening, and buyback of global bonds. Investor appetite does not appear to be the issue: when the Ministry of Economy and Finance went out to place peso-denominated notes, it received offers for nearly 9.7 billion pesos and awarded more than double the anticipated amount, at a rate below 7%. Demand is there. But the context in which this fundraising is unfolding is, to say the least, complex.

The Uruguayan economy grew just 0.4% in the second quarter of the year, confirming a slowdown that had already begun to take shape. Full-year 2025 closed at 1.8%, below official forecasts, and the first quarter of 2026 showed a rebound of 0.8% or 0.9% — depending on the source and reference period — that economists describe as modest. The CERES Leading Index registered two consecutive months of growth, including a 0.3% rise in May, but these positive signals coexist with a more troubling reality: the economy contracted in October 2025 for the second consecutive month, with industry, construction, and commerce among the hardest-hit sectors.

The central tension of the moment is not between growth and stability, but between fiscal credibility and expansion capacity. The Fiscal Advisory Council warned Parliament about an "overestimation" in MEF projections in the Budget Accountability Report, and three economists consulted by El Observador identified low growth, persistent deficits, and competitiveness problems as the main risks. The opposition will summon Oddone for questioning, and he has defended himself by noting that "projection errors are a routine matter" and that "the economy is up and running," even if the recovery, by his own admission, is "slow." The Senate, with Frente Amplio's votes, backed the economic team's management.

The competitiveness problem occupies a central place in the debate. The president of the Asociación Rural del Uruguay described the situation as being "at the limit" given the falling dollar, and the government responded by reducing the IMESI discount on fuel purchases along the Argentine border. Oddone was explicit in stating that "the competitiveness game is not played through the exchange rate," but at the same time the government acknowledges that Uruguay is perceived as cheap for Argentines and expensive for Brazilians, an asymmetry that puts particular pressure on the agricultural sector. The record wheat harvest, which according to El Observador would contribute some $3.9 billion to the economy, offers an important sectoral counterweight but does not resolve structural bottlenecks.

On the financial front, the Financial Stability Committee assessed that the system "is well prepared to absorb hypothetical adverse scenarios," and the recent two-notch upgrade of the investment-grade rating from Standard & Poor's placed the country at the lowest risk level in Latin America. The Central Bank submitted a draft bill to create an open finance framework, a signal that the regulator is betting on the modernization of the financial system over the medium term. However, peso demand is not picking up at the pace the government expected, which delays the "virtuous cycle" of pesification that forms part of the official strategy.

On the political-economic front, Frente Amplio's proposal to introduce changes to the AFAP regime generated internal tensions within the governing coalition and a private-sector response that forced a postponement of the more disruptive changes. Oddone called it an "exaggeration" to speak of nationalizing the pension funds and ruled out any confiscation, but uncertainty about the future of the individual capitalization system remains a risk variable for institutional investors.

Three things warrant close attention in the coming weeks: the actual terms Uruguay manages to secure in international debt markets, which will serve as a thermometer of external perception of its fiscal trajectory; the evolution of Q2 2026 activity data, which will determine whether the first-quarter rebound was a genuine inflection point or a technical bounce; and the scope of the competitiveness bill Oddone has pledged to send to Parliament, whose regulatory ambition will define whether the government has real tools to tackle the structural problem most frequently cited by businesspeople and economists.
