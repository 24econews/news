# Uruguay: The Economic Pulse — 2026-10-23

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-10-23*

Spanish firm Solaria Energía y Medio Ambiente tumbled nearly 8% on the Madrid exchange this week, deepening a losing streak that began just seven days after it announced its Uruguay investment, and that drop now offers the sharpest lens through which to read Uruguay's economic moment: a country that urgently needs private investment to break out of a low-growth cycle, yet depends on external partners whose financial health is as uncertain as the broader global backdrop.

The backdrop is worrying. Uruguay's economy closed 2025 with momentum that analysts themselves describe as almost exclusively "statistical carryover," meaning 2026 began, in the words of private economists, essentially from a standing start. The IMF trimmed its Uruguay growth projection for 2026 to 1.3%, a figure firms like CPA Ferrere consider out of reach and have revised down to 1%. The CERES Leading Index registered no growth in September, consolidating a loss-of-momentum trend running since the middle of last year. Economy Minister Gabriel Oddone publicly acknowledged "a fairly high probability" of revising official 2026 projections downward — a statement whose soberness is unusual, and one the market absorbed without excessive surprise but with full attention.

Against this backdrop of structured slowdown, the Frente Amplio government is trying to articulate a response that pairs austerity signals with selective stimulus. The Ministerio de Economía y Finanzas moved forward with measures to spur private investment, which so far shows only "signs of modest growth," per the MEF itself. The BCU, meanwhile, submitted a draft bill to create an open finance framework, a reform aimed at improving competition in the financial system and reducing intermediation costs, aligned with regulatory trends already operating in Brazil and the European Union. The BCU president also warned that the "ultraconservative" profile in money management "costs the country dearly," a sign that the monetary authority is pushing for pension funds to take on more productive risk, in line with the transformations the government is promoting in the AFAP regime.

The AFAPs, in fact, concentrate one of the most relevant political tensions of the moment. The government confirmed it will push a "set of transformations" to the pension regime, which has revived differences within the Frente Amplio itself and drawn criticism from sectors that fear the changes will sacrifice the party's "social base" in favor of a technocratic agenda. The opposition, for its part, will summon Oddone to answer for the AFAP changes and for what it calls a "complex situation" in the economy, though the Senate has already backed the economic team's management with ruling-coalition votes.

On the external front, Uruguay is sounding out investors to return to the international debt market, a move reflecting both refinancing needs and confidence that the country's risk differential — the lowest in the region, according to Oddone himself — remains a tradable asset. The minister is also leading a tour of the United Kingdom to "strengthen economic and financial ties," suggesting that diversifying sources of capital and trade sits among the economic team's priorities. That logic finds backing in the export data: the main destination for Uruguayan goods is no longer China, a shift on the podium of Uruguay's trade architecture that merits monitoring.

On the tax front, the DGI formalized the creation of a special unit dedicated to auditing some 3,000 high-net-worth taxpayers, using Big Data tools to detect inconsistencies. The measure is consistent with a government that needs to broaden the revenue base without raising general rates — Oddone was explicit that cutting tax expenditure "is not a relevant strategy" — while simultaneously signaling greater fiscal rigor toward the top of the income pyramid. On the labor front, Fábricas Nacionales de Cerveza signed an agreement with its union that includes relocating workers from the Minas plant to Montevideo, an operational consolidation move that reflects the competitiveness pressures facing industrial producers in an environment of an appreciated exchange rate.

The dollar, indeed, remains the central knot in the competitiveness debate. The MEF announced measures to mitigate the peso's appreciation in the face of a phenomenon that could "severely" affect export competitiveness, including extending the IMESI discount on fuels within a radius of up to 80 kilometers from the Argentine border. The president of the Asociación Rural del Uruguay was blunt: "We are at a breaking point, and the minister understands it." What remains to be seen is whether palliative measures suffice, or whether the discussion of systemic competitiveness — ports, logistics, labor costs, access to credit — ultimately imposes itself on the five-year budget agenda the government is building. That, together with the evolution of the exchange rate in Brazil over the coming weeks, will largely define the quarter ahead.
