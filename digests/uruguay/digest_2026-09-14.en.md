An alarm bell rang through Uruguay's economic debate this week with confirmation that activity contracted for a second consecutive month, reviving doubts about whether the modest first-quarter growth —0.8% year-on-year according to the Banco Central del Uruguay— marked a genuine turning point or merely a statistical mirage fueled by carryover from prior years.

The monthly activity data published by El Observador reveal that industry, construction, and commerce led the deterioration in the most recent period, three sectors that together account for a substantial share of formal urban employment. The pattern is troubling because it is not a one-off correction but a loss of momentum that the leading index compiled by Ceres had already been signaling. Although that same indicator posted a 0.3% rise in May —accumulating two positive months— the more recent activity data suggest the signal was weak and transitory. Ceres sums it up with clinical precision: the economy began 2026 "zero to zero," with no cruising speed, in a year the government had projected as the start of a sustained recovery.

The central tension dominating Uruguay's economic landscape is precisely that: the Ministry of Economy and Finance defends a narrative of gradual reactivation in its Rendición de Cuentas, while independent bodies and the Consejo Fiscal Autónomo itself question its foundations. The Council warned Parliament that the MEF's projections involve a systematic "overestimation," a heavyweight technical critique that adds to those from private consultancies. CPA Ferrere projects growth of just 1% for 2026, well below official estimates, and its economists note that "mathematically we have no chance" of hitting the targets set. ECLAC, for its part, cut its projection for Uruguay to 1.5%, placing the country at the bottom of the regional table, behind nearly all its neighbors —a position that the specialized outlet Todo El Campo bluntly described as "bottom of the class, after Bolivia."

What compounds the diagnosis is that the slowdown in activity finds no offset in the domestic credit channel. Household credit has now contracted for seven consecutive months, and delinquency shows no signs of easing —a combination that describes an engine switched off at the heart of private consumption. The government had bet that disinflation —Uruguay is running its lowest inflation in seventy years, according to a BBC report— would generate a virtuous cycle of renewed peso demand, credit expansion, and consumption dynamism. That virtuous cycle, as El Observador noted, "is taking its time." Inflation is falling without peso savings picking up sufficiently, and banks are not increasing exposure to households whose payment capacity remains under stress.

On the fiscal front, the Rendición de Cuentas presented by Minister Gabriel Oddone projects a "challenging and uncertain" global scenario and requests additional resources to sustain spending. The Consejo Fiscal Autónomo does not question the prudence of the approach but rather the credibility of the macro assumptions underpinning it. Oddone, for his part, has managed to position his stewardship well with international investors: the MEF drew strong demand for a new nominal peso-denominated bond, a sign that capital markets continue to value Uruguay's institutional framework. The country also received backing from Fitch Ratings, which maintained its investment grade —albeit with an explicit warning about low growth and rising debt. In parallel, the government issued regulations for the global minimum tax thresholds aligned with OECD standards, and expects to collect roughly USD 350 million annually from multinational taxation once incorporated into the budget —a non-trivial revenue source that could ease the fiscal constraint without touching domestic rates.
