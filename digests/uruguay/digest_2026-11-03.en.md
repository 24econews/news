# Uruguay: Economic Pulse — 2026-11-03

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-11-03*

The 1.1% drop in economic activity in August compared to July is not just an isolated data point to be corrected — it is the most concrete signal yet that Uruguay faces a structurally weaker 2026 than the Frente Amplio government had anticipated when it took office, and that the tools available to reverse the trend are more limited than officials publicly acknowledge.

The figure, released by the Banco Central del Uruguay and reported by El Observador, confirms what the Ceres Leading Index had been signaling for weeks: the economy lost momentum in the second half of the year, and the statistical carryover that explained much of 2025's growth has already run its course. According to several analyses, the country will enter 2026 effectively at "zero to zero," without any accumulated impulse, which turns any annual growth projection into an exercise in building from the ground up. The World Bank projects an expansion of just 1.2% for 2026, below the Latin American average, while private consultancies such as CPA Ferrere are even more pessimistic, placing growth at around 1%. Economy Minister Gabriel Oddone himself acknowledged before the Senate that there is "a fairly high probability" of revising official projections for the year downward.

What makes this moment particularly difficult for the government is not the August decline itself, but the convergence of three simultaneous pressures. Private investment has yet to take off despite specific stimulus measures announced by the Ministry of Economy and Finance, showing only "modest signs of growth" according to the ministry itself. Employment and real private sector wages are, paradoxically, growing above the pace of activity, squeezing corporate margins and raising questions about labor market sustainability if activity fails to rebound. And structural bottlenecks — competitiveness, bureaucratic costs, port tariffs that Oddone himself described as "absolutely inadequate" — remain unresolved at the pace the private sector demands.

Against this backdrop, the government announced four immediate measures to reduce what it called "bureaucratic excesses," and the competitiveness bill began its passage through the Chamber of Deputies, although legislators and analysts warn that political peace and macroeconomic stability — conditions Uruguay meets amply — are not by themselves enough to reverse what some experts describe as a "paralysis" of potential growth. Uruguay maintains the lowest country risk in the region, as Oddone emphasized at international forums, and the Financial Stability Committee noted that the financial system is "well prepared to absorb hypothetical adverse scenarios." The solidity is there; what is missing is traction.

On the fiscal front, the inheritance received by the Oddone team was described by the minister himself as "not dramatic but hardly auspicious." The Fiscal Advisory Council reviewed shortfalls in 2024 public finances, and the Ministry had to activate the safeguard clause to raise the debt ceiling — a signal of pressure, though not of alarm. In parallel, Uruguay sounded out international investors for a new debt operation in global markets, and the MEF placed peso-denominated Treasury Notes amid oversubscription — a vote of confidence in sovereign credit despite the low-growth backdrop. The economy's pesification strategy, which the BCU has begun tracking with a dedicated index, is advancing gradually.

Two external developments deserve attention. First, the Mercosur-EFTA trade agreement came into force, with Uruguay beginning to operate under new rules with Norway and Iceland — a small bloc by volume, but one with high purchasing power and symbolic relevance for the trade-opening agenda. Second, exporting sectors report results ranging from "incipient impact" to "notable growth" six months into the Mercosur-European Union agreement, an encouraging signal that nevertheless has not been enough to offset weak domestic demand. The economic slowdown in Brazil, Uruguay's main trading partner, is another risk factor flagged in recent days by Senator Tabaré Viera.

The domestic political debate intensified this week with the opposition's interpellation of Minister Oddone in the Senate, focused both on changes to the AFAP regime — a reform that is also generating fractures within the Frente Amplio itself — and on the "complex situation" of the economy. Oddone responded that the economy "is moving forward" and defended projection deviations as routine. The Senate, with FA votes, backed the economic team's management.

What to watch in the coming weeks is threefold: the pace of approval of the competitiveness bill in Parliament and what concessions the government will have to make to secure majorities; the evolution of September and October activity data, which will determine whether August was an outlier or the start of a more pronounced trend; and the outcome of the international debt operation under exploration, which will test real global investor appetite for Uruguayan paper at a moment when the country combines credit solidity with disappointing growth prospects.
