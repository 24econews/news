# Uruguay: Economic Pulse — 2026-11-17

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-11-17*

The debut of the Frente Amplio government sets the tone for the day with an unmistakable signal: Azucena Arbeleche took office for a second time as Minister of Economy and Finance with a pledge of immediate spending cuts, in a context where the outgoing minister himself, Gabriel Oddone, openly acknowledges a high probability of downward revisions to 2026 growth projections. It is an unusual combination — an award-winning manager returning to office with a mandate for austerity, in a country whose economy navigates between institutional stability and a dynamism that refuses to materialize.

Arbeleche, recently named Finance Minister of the Year for the Americas by leading industry publications, arrives at a moment of considerable fiscal pressure. The Fiscal Advisory Council has identified worrying non-compliance in the 2024 public finances, and the debate over the Accountability Bill in the Senate revealed deep disagreements over economic projections and the timelines available for consolidation. Oddone's defense before senators that "projection deviations are a routine occurrence" did not dispel the tension; on the contrary, it made clear that the gap between the outgoing government's initial expectations and the reality of economic activity was wider than the economic team was comfortable admitting.

The new government also inherits a structural tension between the imperative to stimulate private investment — the previous MEF announced measures in that direction in response to signs of only "modest growth" — and the imperative to contain spending that the Ministry of Economy agreed to subject to new controls during election years, adopting proposals from legislator Bordaberry to limit fiscal discretion in those periods. It is a significant concession: anchoring fiscal behavior to more rigid rules precisely when the economy most needs flexibility.

On the macroeconomic outlook, private analysts project growth of just one percent for the current year, consistent with the warnings from Fitch Ratings, which maintained Uruguay's investment grade but explicitly flagged low growth and the upward trend in public debt. Morningstar DBRS reaffirmed the sovereign rating in similar terms, underscoring a challenge the incoming government will have to manage from day one. Tabaré Viera himself has noted that Brazil's economic slowdown will have a direct impact on Uruguay, a reminder that the dynamics of Mercosur's main trading partner are not a peripheral data point but a first-order variable for local activity.

The Central Bank, for its part, adds an uncomfortable diagnosis to the picture: its president warned that the "ultra-conservative" profile in money management "comes at a high cost for the country," a formulation that points directly at the savings and portfolio habits of Uruguayans who systematically underuse the formal financial system. This diagnosis arrives at the same time the BCU unveiled a draft bill to create an open finance system, seeking to align Uruguay with the global open banking trend and improve financial intermediation. The Financial Stability Committee, in parallel, certified that the banking system is well prepared to absorb hypothetical adverse scenarios, which at least clears systemic stability risks from the immediate horizon.

Pension system reform returns to the center of the political debate with confirmation that the government will push changes to the AFAP regime within the framework of the Social Dialogue. The move revives fissures within the Frente Amplio itself, with factions criticizing the lack of attention to the party's "social base," while the Executive created a formal forum for exchange with the pension fund administrators to assess the planned changes before formalizing them in legislation.

On the competitiveness front, the bill seeking to reduce the cost of living advanced in parliamentary debate, with Deputy Andújar describing it as an "interesting" measure "that can be improved." At the same time, a private company argued before legislators that a tram-train system for Montevideo would be three hundred million dollars cheaper than the BRT alternatives under consideration, opening a debate over urban infrastructure with direct fiscal implications. The government also issued regulations for the transport agency and estimates that the sector reform will generate twenty-five hundred direct jobs.

The external horizon for economic policy has two relevant markers. Minister Oddone will lead a mission to the United Kingdom to strengthen economic and financial ties, a signal that the government is keeping open its strategy of diversifying partners beyond the regional axis. And the World Bank approved three hundred million dollars in financing to promote private investment and employment, complementing the bet on data centers as a driver of digital activity — Bloomberg Línea noted that the potential impact of these processing centers on the Uruguayan economy could be considerable, particularly if the negotiations involving operators beyond Google are confirmed.

The central question, however, remains whether the new economic team can combine fiscal credibility with growth momentum in a more adverse external environment. With Brazil decelerating, global protectionism on the rise — a Nobel laureate in economics explicitly urged Uruguay to push back against Trump's policies — and inflation at seventy-year lows generating its own monetary policy dilemmas, Arbeleche returns to a ministry with the same reputation she earned in her first term, but with a markedly narrower margin for maneuver.
