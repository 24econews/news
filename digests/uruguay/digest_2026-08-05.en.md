Kristalina Georgieva's visit to Montevideo condensed into a few hours everything that defines Uruguay at this moment: a solid macroeconomic reputation the world recognizes, but a growth dynamic that not even its defenders can describe as satisfactory. The managing director of the International Monetary Fund praised the country's stability —she went so far as to say she hadn't come earlier "because you are doing things right"— but explicitly called for more courage in the face of a challenging environment. That tension between accumulated international credibility and insufficient domestic traction is the axis that organizes the entire Uruguayan economic conversation today.

The first quarter of 2026 delivered GDP growth of 0.8% over the prior quarter, a figure that several sources also cite as 0.9% depending on the indicator used. It is a real rebound from the virtual stagnation with which 2025 closed —a year in which the economy grew 1.8%, below official forecasts and practically without organic momentum, according to analysts at Exante who describe that result as "carryover" growth— but insufficient to speak of a recovery. The World Bank has already trimmed its 2026 projection to 1.6%, the BCU projects below the MEF, and several private analysts are consolidating estimates below 1% annually if the external environment does not improve. Minister Gabriel Oddone, who has publicly acknowledged a "fairly high probability" of revising projections downward, defends at the same time that the economy is on a "recovery path." The difference between the two statements is not contradictory: it is simply a question of how fast that recovery is expected to advance.

What complicates the picture is the regional context. The situation in Argentina and Brazil simultaneously challenges Uruguayan competitiveness. A depreciated Argentine peso and the effects of Javier Milei's policies are generating pressure in border departments, where the government recently decided to reduce the IMESI discount for fuel purchases: a tactical measure that reflects how much room has been eroded. The president of the Asociación Rural del Uruguay described the situation as "at the limit." Meanwhile, Brazil —the country's main trading partner— is going through its own cycle of uncertainty, with a real under pressure that erodes relative prices for Uruguayan exporters.

Against this backdrop, the government presented international investors with its terms for a new sovereign debt issuance in the capital markets. According to El Observador, Uruguay sounded out investors to return to the international market, an operation that has already materialized with the placement of bonds worth USD 1.3 billion in pesos and dollars, as reported by La Mañana. The MEF also doubled the issuance of peso-denominated Notes after receiving demand of nearly 9.7 billion pesos. The country currently holds the lowest sovereign risk in Latin America, a position that allows it to finance itself on favorable terms but which also contains a trap: the spread between the cost of credit and the pace of growth must continue to narrow for the fiscal trajectory to be sustainable.

The domestic fiscal dispute has its own epicenter in the Rendición de Cuentas, whose parliamentary negotiation is deadlocked. Oddone is asking for roughly USD 1 billion in additional spending, the opposition within the governing coalition is not voting the document, and the Consejo Fiscal Autónomo formally warned Parliament of an "overestimation" in the MEF's projections. The minister defended the figures by arguing that "projection errors are a common thing," but institutional criticism from an independent body carries different political weight than criticism from the opposition. Separately, the government confirmed it will push transformations to the AFAP regime within the social dialogue, a politically delicate process that Oddone called an "exaggeration" when described as nationalization, and on which the Frente Amplio is already showing internal divisions.

Among the growth engines the government has identified to underpin its projection are private investments in green hydrogen, data centers, and a new pulp mill. These are medium-term, capital-intensive bets that will not generate domestic demand in the short run. Meanwhile, Ceres' leading activity index showed mixed signals —increases in April and May followed by declines— and household disposable income, while no longer falling after seven consecutive months of deterioration, has not recovered enough ground to sustain a robust consumption cycle. Public perception of the economic situation, according to pollster Cifra, "has been deteriorating," adding political pressure to the debate over real wages.

What remains to be watched in the coming weeks is whether Uruguay manages to close the parliamentary negotiation of the Rendición de Cuentas with some form of political backing, how the real exchange rate evolves in a context in which the BCU has already acknowledged a reduction in exchange-rate lag but the export sector continues to demand more, and whether the major private investments announced —particularly in pulp and clean energy— advance toward final investment decisions capable of mobilizing domestic demand. Georgieva's visit left a precise message: stability is a necessary condition, not a sufficient one.
