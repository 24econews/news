# Uruguay: The Economic Pulse — 2026-08-13

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-08-13*

Uruguay is heading back into international debt markets today at a moment when the gap between what citizens feel and what formal indicators predict has never been more pronounced — and that tension defines the entire economic reading of the country.

President Yamandú Orsi's government confirmed that the Ministry of Economy and Finance is sounding out institutional investors for a new sovereign bond issuance in international markets, an operation that comes as Minister Gabriel Oddone leads a tour through the United Kingdom aimed at strengthening financial ties. The timing is technically favorable: Uruguay has achieved the lowest country risk in Latin America, Standard & Poor's raised its rating by two notches, and IMF Managing Director Kristalina Georgieva publicly praised the solidity of the Uruguayan economy, though with the recurring caveat that stability alone is not enough. The global context adds urgency: Oddone has warned of a potential "negative shock" in energy prices stemming from the escalation in the Middle East, which makes locking in financing on favorable terms before markets reprice that risk a matter of clear management logic.

The problem is that the macroeconomic picture underpinning that debt operation coexists with a considerably murkier domestic reality. Pollster Cifra reports that the economic climate as perceived by Uruguayans "has been steadily deteriorating," and that half the population considers the country's economic situation to be poor. That perception is not arbitrary: the economy grew just 1.8% in 2025, below both official and IMF forecasts, and consultancy CPA Ferrere projects that growth in 2026 will not exceed 1%, well short of the government's target. Oddone himself acknowledged that there is "a fairly high probability" that growth projections for this year will be revised downward. The Ceres Leading Index, which posted two months of modest growth in the middle of last year, has fallen again and reinforces the signs of weakening. At the same time, industry, construction, and commerce all registered consecutive declines, and the World Bank described Uruguay as a "superstar" that has lost ground.

The contradiction emerging from this picture — enthusiastic international investors, uncertain citizens and businesses — reflects a classic divergence in economies that maintain solid fiscal and financial fundamentals but fail to translate them into growth sufficient to visibly lift living standards. The Financial Stability Committee noted that the banking system is "well prepared to absorb adverse scenarios," but that institutional strength does not resolve the competitiveness problem that business leaders themselves identify as their main concern for 2026, according to a recent survey. Uruguay remains expensive in dollar terms, demand for pesos is not picking up at the pace the government had hoped, and the virtuous circle that the pesification policy sought to activate continues to prove elusive.

Against that backdrop, the government is betting on a specific set of large-scale private investments to hit its growth projections: green hydrogen, data centers — with Google as an anchor and other companies under evaluation, according to Undersecretary Martín Vallcorba — and a new pulp mill. If these projects materialize on the anticipated timelines, they have the capacity to move GDP in a statistically significant way, but their impact on employment and disposable household income is more diffuse and longer-term. Knowledge Economy service exports posted record growth, pointing to the existence of dynamic sectors, but the underlying workforce base is still narrow. Agriculture, for its part, offers a positive note: wheat production, at record levels, could generate roughly USD 3.9 billion in income for the economy, a meaningful buffer on the external front.

On the fiscal front, the Accountability Bill remains the political epicenter. Oddone faces the Coalición Republicana's refusal to vote for the project, demands from Cabildo Abierto that, in the minister's own words, require "a separate law," and pressure from the opposition, which will summon him to answer for changes to the AFAP system. Oddone dismissed the notion that this amounts to "nationalization," but the debate is open and creates noise at a moment when the government needs signs of cohesion to underpin the international debt operation. The government will also include the global minimum tax on multinationals in the budget law, expecting to collect USD 350 million, a figure that would help ease a fiscal situation that the minister himself described at the start of his tenure as "more restrictive than any of us had imagined."

What to watch in the coming weeks is the concrete investor response to the MEF's sounding — the pricing and maturity terms Uruguay manages to secure in the markets will say a great deal about how much its credibility premium is really worth in a more volatile global environment — the evolution of the exchange rate in a country that remains structurally expensive in dollars, and whether domestic political pressure over the Accountability Bill ends up weakening the fiscal signal the government needs to send for that debt operation to prove successful.
