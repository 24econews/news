The answer offered by today's data is an uncomfortable one for President Yamandú Orsi's government.

The Neltume Ports proposal, from the Chilean subsidiary of shipping group Ultramar, is the day's most consequential corporate news: the company has formally submitted to the Uruguayan executive a plan to build a new container terminal at the capital's port, with an investment that would nearly double the scale of any recent port infrastructure project in the country. If it materializes, it would have direct implications for Montevideo's position as a regional logistics hub, at a moment when Buenos Aires and southern Brazilian ports are aggressively competing for the same cargo traffic. The initiative arrives, however, in a macroeconomic context that complicates investor optimism: the Uruguayan economy posted its second consecutive monthly contraction, with industry, construction and commerce among the hardest-hit sectors, according to data compiled by El Observador. First-quarter growth came in at just 0.8% year-on-year—a figure that consultancy CPA Ferrere, among others, describes as insufficient to meet government targets—and the Ceres leading index has oscillated between contradictory signals, with a 0.3% recovery in May followed by a fresh decline that reinforces doubts about the momentum of activity.

The most striking tension emerging from today's coverage is the widening gap between the perceptions of economic agents and those of ordinary citizens. While most companies foresee economic stability for 2026 and investors responded with strong interest to the issuance of a new sovereign bond in nominal pesos—an unambiguous signal of confidence in fiscal institutions and in the de-dollarization process—household pessimism persists with stubborn resilience. According to Cifra, close to half of Uruguayans consider the country's economic situation to be poor, and three in ten perceive themselves as poor. This divorce between business sentiment and popular malaise is not trivial: in a political cycle that has only just begun, it feeds redistributive pressures that Economy Minister Gabriel Oddone will have to manage carefully.

Oddone, who has explicitly positioned Uruguay "closer to Europe than to the United States" in terms of economic management, faces a diagnosis he himself acknowledged upon taking office: a tighter-than-expected fiscal situation, household credit in decline for seven straight months with delinquency rates that refuse to ease, and demand for pesos that has yet to rebound with the strength the central bank needs to consolidate its de-dollarization strategy. Food deflation is now palpable, which has halted the decline in mass consumption and generated some contained euphoria within the economic team. And in the agricultural sector, wheat production posted record levels, with an estimated contribution of $3.9 billion to the economy—a figure that could be the main driver of GDP in the second half, provided international grain prices cooperate.
