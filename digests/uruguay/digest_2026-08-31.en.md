# Uruguay: The Economic Pulse — 2026-08-31

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-08-31*

Uruguay's economy is navigating a tension few countries in the region experience in quite this way: consumer credit has been contracting for seven consecutive months and delinquency rates refuse to ease, even as technology services exports have just hit an all-time high and lamb meat shipments abroad jumped 34% in the first half of the year. It is the snapshot of a two-speed economy, where sectors plugged into the global market thrive with vigor while the domestic market remains stalled, unable to find traction.

Recent activity data confirms this divide. The economy expanded just 0.8% in the first quarter versus the previous quarter, a modest figure that, according to the Banco Central, reflects a country "evolving below its potential growth." In the following months, activity fell for a second consecutive month, with industry, construction and commerce among the hardest-hit sectors. The CERES Leading Index sent mixed signals: it pointed to an incipient recovery, yet the broader set of available indicators painted a picture of fragility hard to ignore. Analysts consolidated cuts to their 2026 growth projections, with firms like CPA Ferrere placing GDP expansion at a mere 1%, well below what the government of President Yamandú Orsi had anticipated.

The Ministry of Economy, led by Gabriel Oddone, publicly acknowledged that there is "a fairly high probability" of revising the year's growth projection downward. The tone of the minister, who has become the most visible face of the Frente Amplio's economic policy since taking office in March, was one of technical honesty: he warned that the recovery is slow, that the fiscal situation is more restrictive than anticipated and that the global environment is "challenging and uncertain." His words resonated with particular force when he compared the risk of fiscal indiscipline to the Argentine case, a reference he repeated in multiple public appearances and which was picked up by regional outlets such as Infobae, Perfil and Los Andes. In the coming days, Oddone will lead a mission to the United Kingdom to "strengthen economic and financial ties," a signal that the government is seeking to diversify its external anchors amid pressure from Washington—according to the minister himself—for Uruguay to scale back its commercial ties with China.

Against this backdrop, the Ministry of Economy's policy moves aim to improve competitiveness without expanding spending. The Senate approved a bill along these lines that, according to its sponsors, will lower the cost of living and streamline foreign trade procedures. The government also issued regulations for the thresholds of the global minimum tax on multinationals, from which it expects to collect USD 350 million, and will incorporate this framework into the five-year budget bill. In parallel, the Banco Central unveiled a draft proposal to create an open finance system, an initiative aimed at modernizing the country's financial infrastructure and one that dovetails with the regulatory agenda several emerging markets are pushing in response to the rise of fintechs.

The agricultural sector displays an equally telling duality. In the first half, 117 agro-industrial products hit export records, sales of agricultural machinery rebounded in the second quarter and lamb meat achieved that 34% jump in foreign-currency earnings. Yet soybeans and rice posted setbacks that weighed on agricultural GDP in the first quarter. The medium-term key could lie offshore: YPF, in partnership with Italy's ENI, both listed on international markets, formalized their entry into oil exploration in Uruguayan waters—a bet that, if it pans out, could reshape the country's energy and export matrix in the next decade. The government, which already highlighted in an official document the advances in renewable energy, sees this initiative as a logical extension of its wager on productive transformation.

The Knowledge Economy is perhaps the most striking data point of the period. Exports of technology and high value-added services posted record growth, and the Undersecretary of Economy noted that projects from major global tech corporations—beyond Google, whose presence in Uruguay is already well known—are under evaluation. This dynamic turns the country into a regional case study and explains why IMF Managing Director Kristalina Georgieva praised Uruguay's macroeconomic stability during her recent visit, although she also called for "boosting growth" and left without offering a blank check. The Financial Stability Committee, for its part, noted that the banking system is "well prepared to absorb hypothetical adverse scenarios," providing a cushion against potential external shocks.

What remains to be resolved is the harder question: how to move from stability to growth. August inflation is coming in around 1.6%, the lowest in seventy years, a signal of monetary strength but one that also raises questions about domestic demand. Household credit contracting for seven consecutive months is not a sign that families are choosing to save more; it is a sign that the consumption engine is switched off. Consumer confidence has fallen and half of Uruguayans consider the country's economic situation to be poor, according to consultancy Cifra. In the coming months, attention will focus on whether the five-year budget strikes a credible balance between fiscal discipline and private investment stimulus, and on whether the debate around the AFAPs—the individually funded pension system the government has pledged to reform—generates uncertainty or a boost to domestic savings. Oddone's London tour, meanwhile, could presage a fresh sovereign debt operation in international markets, where Uruguay remains one of the region's most highly regarded issuers.
