# Uruguay: The Economic Pulse — 2026-11-05

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-11-05*

August's monthly drop in economic activity—a 1.1% decline versus July, according to data released today by El Observador—is no isolated data point: it crystallizes a narrative of sustained deceleration that the new Frente Amplio administration inherits with urgency, and that Economy Minister Gabriel Oddone himself can no longer dress up with managerial optimism.

The August figure is the most unsettling of the recent cycle precisely because it arrives after the second quarter already posted a 0.8% contraction—breaking eight consecutive quarters of growth—and after the third quarter closed with an additional 0.2% decline. 2025, which the Luis Lacalle Pou government handed over with barely 1.8% annual expansion, well below the 2.6% projected, produced what analysts technically call a near-zero "statistical carryover": 2026 begins, in the words of economists cited by El Observador, practically "nil-nil." Oddone himself acknowledged with unusual candor that there is "a fairly high probability" that the government will revise downward the growth target for 2026, originally set at a modest 1%.

What makes the current situation more disquieting is that the weakening is not an exclusively Uruguayan phenomenon but responds in part to regional forces beyond Montevideo's control. El Observador and La Diaria concur in noting that Uruguay, Argentina and Brazil are all simultaneously showing poor economic performance. Senator Tabaré Viera put it bluntly: "Brazil's economic slowdown will hit Uruguay." The neighboring giant is the country's main trading partner, and its cooling is inevitably transmitted through trade, tourism and investment. Argentina, for its part, adds the element of cross-border exchange-rate distortion: the government had to reduce the IMESI discount for fuel purchases along the Argentine border, a sign that price asymmetries between the two countries remain a source of consumption leakage and a concrete fiscal challenge.

The Ceres Leading Index, a barometer anticipating activity, fell once again, reinforcing signs of weakening and adding temporal depth to what some still wanted to read as one-off fluctuations. Business confidence, according to a recent survey, also posted a fresh decline, with a simultaneous deterioration in the assessment of the government's performance. This is the context in which Oddone defended before the Senate that "the economy is moving forward" and that forecast deviations "are a routine occurrence."

The economic team's response is articulated on several fronts. The Economy Ministry announced cost-reduction measures and red-tape simplification to facilitate foreign trade, while evaluating a reform of the port tariff system, which Oddone described as "absolutely inadequate." In parallel, the government issued regulations setting the limits of the global minimum tax in line with fiscal stability clauses, a nod to international investors who are already eyeing Uruguay as a destination for technology capital: Undersecretary Vallcorba confirmed that, in addition to Google, other projects from major tech firms are under evaluation. The World Bank recently approved $300 million in financing aimed at promoting private investment and employment, a signal of external backing the government will leverage in its narrative.

On the trade front, the entry into force of the Mercosur-EFTA agreement with Norway and Iceland opens up a high-purchasing-power market that export sectors already describe as an opportunity, although its impact on activity figures will necessarily be gradual. More immediate is the debate over the Mercosur-European Union agreement: six months into its implementation, export sectors describe an impact ranging from "incipient" to "notable growth" depending on the sector, suggesting that the benefits have yet to become widespread.

On the institutional front, the Economy Ministry took the first formal step toward a roadmap for applying for OECD membership, an objective Oddone himself links to Uruguay's ambition of reaching an "A" credit rating in the "not-too-distant future." Fitch Ratings kept the country's investment grade rating, though it warned about low growth and rising debt, a combination that narrows the fiscal room for maneuver.

The pension front adds political complexity: the government confirmed it will promote changes to the AFAP regime within the framework of the Social Dialogue, while 112 Frente Amplio economists signed a declaration against the social security plebiscite being pushed by labor sectors—an internal fracture that reveals tensions between the economic team's technocratic pragmatism and the social base of the governing coalition.

What the market and analysts should watch in the coming months is threefold: the speed at which measures to stimulate private investment materialize, the evolution of activity in Brazil as the main external variable, and the government's ability to sustain fiscal discipline while facing redistributive pressures from within its own electoral base. If September's activity data confirms the August trend, the pressure to revise 2026 projections downward will cease to be a probability Oddone cautiously acknowledges and will become an arithmetic necessity.
