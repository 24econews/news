# Uruguay: The Economic Pulse — 2026-09-07

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-09-07*

The tension defining Uruguay's economic moment isn't between two sectors or two policies, but between two simultaneous readings of the same reality: business owners and investors who maintain reasonably optimistic expectations, and a citizenry that perceives its economic situation as deteriorating or stagnant. That gap—documented today by El Observador—is not a sociological curiosity; it's a data point with direct political and economic consequences for a Frente Amplio government barely months into its term and already facing its first turbulence.

The context surrounding that fracture is complex. New data suggest the Uruguayan economy stalled in the second quarter of the year, with some private estimates pointing to a contraction of as much as 0.7% during that period, according to uy press. Industry, construction, and commerce posted consecutive declines. The Ceres leading index, which had anticipated some improvement in April and May with gains of 0.2% and 0.3% respectively, has fallen again in more recent readings, reinforcing the signals of weakening domestic demand. Against this backdrop, the BBC picked up an unusual paradox this week: Uruguay is registering its lowest inflation in seventy years, a first-order macroeconomic achievement that nonetheless generates its own challenges by compressing corporate margins and complicating relative price adjustments.

The Ministry of Economy, led by Gabriel Oddone, is navigating this slippery terrain. In the Rendición de Cuentas currently being debated in Parliament, the Consejo Fiscal Autónomo formally warned lawmakers about an "overestimation" in the growth projections presented by the MEF, an institutional alarm bell that Oddone himself moved to defuse by arguing that deviations in projections "are commonplace" in any fiscal planning exercise. The opposition, led by the Partido Nacional, has already announced it will summon the minister to Parliament in general committee to explain, among other things, the projected changes to the AFAP regime—the individual capitalization pension fund system—whose possible transformation is perhaps the most politically sensitive issue of the moment. The government confirmed it will push for "a set of transformations" in that system, though Oddone was quick to rule out any "nationalization" and dismissed any form of confiscation of savings.

In parallel, the government is attempting to articulate a growth agenda. Oddone announced the drafting of a bill to improve competitiveness, with measures that would include a reduction in red tape and tax relief for the formal sector. The Unión Industrial Argentina—whose Uruguayan counterpart shares similar concerns—warned this week that the tax burden on Uruguay's formal economy is, by its calculations, "the highest in the world," a claim the government disputes but which reflects the productive sector's unease. Competitiveness is, according to business surveys cited by El Observador, the top concern of companies looking ahead to 2026, ranking even above demand or the exchange rate.

The external front offers some relief. Wheat, which had a record harvest, could contribute close to USD 3.9 billion to the economy, a figure that partially mitigates the weakness in the domestic market. CAF—Latin America's development bank—announced it will double its portfolio in Uruguay to USD 2 billion and will convene the region's finance ministers in Montevideo, a meaningful gesture of institutional confidence at a moment when the country needs signals of international backing. Uruguay has already booked USD 27 million in direct benefits since the entry into force of the Mercosur-European Union agreement, according to uy press, though the full impact of that treaty is still marginal.

On the financial policy front, the Banco Central presented a draft bill to create an open finance system, an initiative aligned with global open banking trends that seeks to broaden competition in the payments system and lower intermediation costs. The Comité de Estabilidad Financiera, for its part, indicated that the banking system "is well prepared to absorb hypothetical adverse scenarios," a message of preventive calm in a regional context where financial volatility has not disappeared. Uruguay's country risk remains the lowest in Latin America, an asset the Orsi government has inherited and, for now, is preserving.

What to watch in the coming weeks is the actual second-quarter outcome when the Banco Central publishes official GDP figures, the degree of parliamentary support the Rendición de Cuentas garners with its upwardly revised spending projections, and the reception—both in the private sector and among the opposition—of the still-undefined competitiveness bill. Uruguay's economy grew 0.8% in the first quarter versus the previous quarter, driven by consumption and exports, but if the second quarter confirms the contraction that partial indicators anticipate, the government's narrative that the economy is "on the move" will become notably harder to sustain.
