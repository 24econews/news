# Uruguay: The Economic Pulse — 2026-09-17

The 0.8% contraction in gross domestic product during the second quarter, released this week by the Banco Central del Uruguay, is not in itself the most important story of the day. What is truly revealing is the constellation of factors surrounding it: a government that acknowledges the setback as "anticipated" but faces 2026 growth projections that analysts keep trimming, a World Bank arriving with $300 million in financing alongside a stark diagnosis of the country's structural limitations, and a port dispute that threatens to erode competitiveness precisely as the government tries to persuade the world that Uruguay is an attractive place to do business.

The second-quarter contraction is largely a story about agriculture, which absorbed the blow of the drought, with the sector shrinking by roughly 22% according to estimates compiled by AgroLatam. Beyond the farm gate, the picture was more nuanced: finance, mining and select services held up reasonably well, and Economy Minister Gabriel Oddone insisted the result "does not surprise us," reaffirming the official 1.6% growth forecast for the year as a whole. The problem is that this narrative of gradual recovery collides with the reality of private analysts, who once again trimmed their 2026 estimates to around 1%, well below the government's target. CPA Ferrere, among others, projects a year of subdued momentum reflecting an economy whose macroeconomic stability — widely praised by the IMF and international rating agencies — is not translating into sustained expansion.

Against that backdrop came the World Bank's approval of a $300 million loan geared toward promoting private investment and employment. The financing arrives with a diagnosis the institution itself did not soften: growth, PISA test results, the labor market and energy policy rank among the country's leading challenges. The World Bank's vice president noted that "competitiveness is moving from diagnosis to a concrete reform agenda," a diplomatic formulation that captures precisely the gap between the Uruguay applauded at international forums and the one that local experts themselves, without hedging, call paralyzed. In La Diaria, economists warned that "political peace and the promotion of economic stability are not enough" to reverse that stagnation.

The government of Yamandú Orsi has several tools on the table. The Ministry of Economy reorganized Comap to focus investment incentives on micro, small and medium-sized enterprises; a competitiveness bill is advancing in the lower house; and the MEF has announced measures to cushion exporters from the appreciation of the peso, a phenomenon the president of the Asociación Rural del Uruguay described as "a breaking point." The BCU president, for his part, criticized what he called an "ultra-conservative" approach to monetary management, arguing that such conservatism "comes at a high cost to the country." The debate over the AFAPs also remains open: after meeting with sector representatives, Oddone agreed to set up a 60-day dialogue table, while the Frente Amplio works through internal differences over the scope of pension-system reform.

On the port front, the situation escalated. The union at the Terminal de Contenedores del Puerto de Montevideo launched an open-ended strike, forcing operations to run on minimum services. Katoen Natie, the Belgian operator that controls the terminal, asked the government to declare the service essential, while the PIT-CNT criticized the government decree as "excessive." The dispute comes at a particularly delicate moment: Neltume Ports, a company linked to Chile's Ultramar group, submitted a $900 million investment proposal to the government for a new container terminal in Montevideo, an initiative the government did not celebrate publicly given the potential conflict with Katoen Natie and existing contractual commitments. In parallel, the shareholders of Montecon irrevocably withdrew the arbitration they had initiated against Uruguay before ICSID, closing a contentious chapter that had weighed on the country's investor image.

In the energy sector, Italy's ENI joined the offshore block where the first exploratory well in Uruguayan waters will be drilled, a signal that international interest in South Atlantic resources remains alive despite Minister Oddone's warnings about the risk of a "negative shock" to energy prices stemming from the conflict in the Middle East. On the financial front, the dollar traded on Wednesday around the levels reported by BROU, with the peso's appreciating pressure keeping exporters on edge. The Ceres leading index posted a 0.3% gain in May, marking two consecutive months of growth, though the institution itself cautioned that the August data "reinforced signs of fading momentum."

The coming weeks will be decisive on several simultaneous fronts: the resolution of the port dispute in Montevideo, whose impact on logistics chains is already being estimated; the progress of the competitiveness bill in Parliament; the trajectory of the exchange rate and its effects on agriculture; and the eventual release of new activity data that will help gauge whether the third quarter confirms the recovery the government anticipates or whether Uruguay's economy is still searching, as one analyst quoted by Montevideo Portal put it, for "a genuine growth thesis."
