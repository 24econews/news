Growth in 2025 — a meager 1.8%, well short of the 2.6% projected — was largely the product of a statistical carryover effect inherited from the previous year, meaning 2026 starts without that cushion. Private analysts have once again trimmed their expansion forecasts for this year, and Oddone himself has publicly conceded that there is "a fairly high probability" of revising downward the official growth target of 1%, a figure that was already modest compared with the country's historical potential.

The activity data that have accumulated paint a picture of fragility that sits uneasily with the official narrative of gradual recovery. Against that backdrop, business confidence has also deteriorated: surveys show a fresh drop in the business climate, though most firms still foresee stability for 2026 and continue to cite competitiveness — not demand — as their main concern.

That word, competitiveness, best captures the central tension in Uruguay's economy at this moment. The nominal exchange rate has remained relatively stable — the dollar hovers around levels where the Central Bank has not intervened since 2021, a stance Oddone himself has pointed to as part of the macroeconomic stability recipe — but that very stability, combined with inflation that eased to multi-decade lows before edging back up to 4.9% year-on-year in October, has contributed to making local costs in dollars more expensive.
