# Uruguay: Economic Pulse — 2026-10-20

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-10-20*

The mega-investment by Solaria, the Spanish renewable energy company, in data center infrastructure in Uruguay —a project valued at up to $5.7 billion and with capacity of up to 500 megawatts— is crystallizing today with greater precision than the market had anticipated, and represents the most relevant angle of Uruguay's economic landscape: a country that offers macroeconomic stability in a turbulent global environment, but which has yet to resolve its main structural dilemma, that of low growth.

Solaria disclosed in Spain the details of its agreement with the Uruguayan government, and according to El Observador, authorities set May 2027 as the deadline for signing the definitive contract. The company, whose shares trade on the Spanish stock exchange, has found in Uruguay a combination that is difficult to find in Latin America: electricity supply based on renewables, institutional stability, and an investment regime with fiscal stability clauses. The Yamandú Orsi government, through the ministry led by Gabriel Oddone, has presented this project as the spearhead of a productive repositioning strategy that the country urgently needs. The investment, if fully realized, would represent one of the largest flows of foreign private capital in the country's recent history, and would position Uruguay as a regional node for digital infrastructure at a moment when global demand for data centers shows no signs of slowing.

The context in which this project arrives, however, is not that of an economy in full swing. Analysts have again trimmed their growth projections for 2026: consultancies such as CPA Ferrere estimate that GDP will grow barely 1% this year, well below the official target, and Minister Oddone himself has publicly acknowledged that "there is a fairly high probability" that government estimates will be revised downward. Consultancy Exante notes that the economy saw a rebound in early 2026 relative to the stagnation of 2025, but that this momentum is fragile. The Central Bank indicated that activity "is evolving below its potential growth," a description that the Ministry of Economy and Finance (MEF) has corroborated with its own potential growth projections through 2032, recently published with figures that do not invite optimism.

Fitch Ratings maintained Uruguay's investment grade —in a decision the market received without surprise given the country's track record of fiscal discipline— but explicitly warned about the sluggish growth and the rise in public debt. The agency places its finger on the same wound that local analysts point to: Uruguay has stability, but stability without growth is, at best, a necessary but insufficient platform. Country risk remains the lowest in the region, as Oddone himself has highlighted in international forums, a real asset but one that does not generate jobs nor close the gap with more dynamic economies.

On the trade front, data on the destination of Uruguayan exports show changes at the top of the trading partner podium, with China losing prominence as the main buyer of goods in favor of other destinations, according to El Observador. This rebalancing has implications for the exposure of Uruguayan agriculture to Chinese demand, particularly relevant given that agriculture remains one of the most volatile engines of the economy. The sector recorded a sharp drop in recent periods —with soy and rice as the main drivers of the retreat— although July data showed a slight recovery in aggregate economic activity.

The Frente Amplio government also faces the pressure of a moving economic legislative agenda: the competitiveness bill has already begun its consideration in the Chamber of Deputies, with signs that it could be improved during the parliamentary process. Simultaneously, the MEF is working with the Central Bank on modifications to the FA's debt restructuring bill, while the government confirmed it will promote transformations in the AFAP regime —which has generated tensions within the ruling coalition itself. The reduction of the IMESI discount for fuel purchases on the border with Argentina, a measure that directly affects the competitiveness of retail trade in border zones, adds another layer to a menu of economic policy decisions that the economic team is trying to articulate without compromising fiscal balance.

The Ministry of Economy will also soon lead a tour of the United Kingdom, aimed at strengthening financial ties. This type of economic diplomacy takes on greater relevance when the country needs to diversify its sources of financing and project confidence before European institutional investors. Uruguay recently issued debt in Swiss francs —a historic first— and continues to develop its agenda of bonds linked to climate indicators, consolidating a narrative of a responsible and predictable country in international markets.

What is worth watching in the coming weeks is manifold: the progress of the competitiveness bill in Parliament and the changes it may undergo; the evolution of the exchange rate and the impact on export competitiveness, a variable that the agricultural sector monitors with growing alarm; the second-quarter 2026 data that will confirm or refute the thesis of an incipient rebound; and, above all, any concrete signal regarding the definitive agreement with Solaria, whose signing in 2027 would mark a before-and-after in the composition of private investment in Uruguay.
