# Uruguay: Economic Pulse — 2026-11-16

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-11-16*

The tension between two simultaneous readings of the Uruguayan economy — one that celebrates its institutional stability before the world, and another that registers a contraction over two consecutive quarters — is the most revealing feature of the moment the country is going through.

While President Yamandú Orsi was in Madrid presenting the mega-investment by Spanish firm Solaria in renewable energy, calling it "a strong bet and therefore, history," domestic data told a more uncomfortable story. The Uruguayan economy contracted 0.8% in the second quarter and 0.2% in the third, which technically amounts to two consecutive quarters of contraction. First-quarter growth — a mere 0.8% — proved insufficient to sustain annual momentum, and analysts polled by various sources now project growth of around 1% for 2026, with risks skewed to the downside. Economy Minister Gabriel Oddone himself acknowledged that there is "a fairly high probability" that growth projections for next year will need to be revised downward.

The IMF's diagnosis, published as part of the 2025 Article IV Consultation, captures that duality precisely. The IMF highlighted the country's "sound macroeconomic policies" and its resilience to external shocks — a judgment Oddone was quick to endorse — but warned of mounting "spending pressures" and recommended a review of health and education outlays. Standard & Poor's, for its part, maintained the country's investment grade rating and projects 1.1% growth for 2026, while Fitch Ratings confirmed its rating but flagged weak dynamism and the gradual rise in public debt. For a country that makes macroeconomic stability its main pitch to international investors, the combination of praise for the institutional framework with warnings about spending sustainability is a signal the new government cannot ignore.

The Ceres Leading Index has swung between signs of weakening and mild recoveries throughout the year, reflecting an economy that has been unable to find a sustained trajectory. According to that same think tank, the economy continues to grow but at a decelerating pace. Growth in 2025 is almost entirely explained by "statistical carryover" from the previous year, which means that 2026 starts, in the analysts' own words, "zero to zero." Inflation expectations ticked up slightly and point to a year-end close of around 5.1%, keeping pressure on the BCU regarding its rate policy. The exchange rate, according to BROU, remains at relatively stable levels, and analysts anticipate a dollar without major surprises heading into year-end.

Faced with this low-growth landscape, the Frente Amplio government has opted for a combination of private investment stimulus and public spending restraint that is not easy to sustain politically. The Economy Ministry announced measures to reduce costs and red tape in foreign trade, seeking competitiveness gains without additional tax intervention. The Competitiveness and Cost of Living Reduction bill advanced in the Senate, although its handling in the Chamber of Deputies is generating friction. At the same time, the government issued regulations setting out the limits of the global minimum tax in line with fiscal stability clauses — a nod to foreign investors operating under free-trade zone regimes or investment promotion contracts. Private investment, according to the ministry itself, shows "signs of modest growth," but the word "modest" encapsulates the gap between programmatic ambitions and the reality of the cycle.

Minister Oddone's tour of the United Kingdom to "strengthen economic and financial ties," together with Orsi's presence in Madrid closing the Solaria deal and calling on Spanish businesses to consider Uruguay as a regional base for Mercosur, traces an active economic diplomacy at a moment when the Nobel Prize in Economics laureate publicly warned that the country must "fight Trump's protectionism." The successful bond placement of $1.697 billion, highlighted by Oddone himself as a signal of credibility, confirms that international markets remain willing to finance Uruguay on reasonable terms, although that window is not permanent.

In the real economy, the wheat harvest kicked off with a planted area 10% larger than the previous season, a bright spot for the agricultural sector in a year of mixed results. HIF Uruguay's project to install a solar park in Paysandú aimed at green hydrogen production entered the public consultation stage, consolidating Uruguay as a benchmark destination for clean energy. The withdrawal of the ICSID arbitration by ATCO, the second-largest shareholder of Montecon, closes a legal risk front for the State on port matters.

What to watch closely in the coming weeks is the parliamentary debate over the Budget Accountability Report, where the government's growth projections are being challenged by the opposition and some coalition partners. The reform of the AFAP regime, confirmed by the executive within the framework of the Social Dialogue, will have direct implications for the domestic capital market. And the trajectory of the Ceres Leading Index in the coming months will be the first concrete signal of whether the economy can stabilize before the 2026 budget cycle begins.
