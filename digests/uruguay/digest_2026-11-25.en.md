# PART 1 — MAIN NARRATIVE

Economy and Finance Minister Gabriel Oddone issued an unusual challenge to the analyst community on Tuesday: explain why Uruguay remains insulated from the currency volatility shaking its neighbors. The question is not rhetorical. In a regional environment where the Brazilian real and the Argentine peso continue under significant pressure, the Uruguayan peso has held relatively steady — a phenomenon that the Ministry itself acknowledges as difficult to attribute to any single factor. That it is the head of the Economy Ministry who publicly poses the question reveals both the perceived strength of Uruguay's position and the discomfort of governing amid an uncertainty that is not yet fully understood.

Part of the answer may lie in debt strategy. In recent weeks, the BCU and the Ministry of Economy and Finance have tested the 6% benchmark rate through eight local debt auctions, a sequence designed to anchor yield expectations in the domestic market and signal monetary discipline to investors closely monitoring rate dynamics across the region. That bet is complemented by the international positioning Oddone also articulated this week, when he highlighted Uruguay's drive toward OECD accession and its aspiration to become a "protagonist on the global agenda" — a narrative of institutional credibility that the government is deliberately deploying as a shield against external shocks.

But if Uruguay's macro picture offers a measure of calm, the domestic consumption front is sending less reassuring signals. Consumer confidence fell again and remains in moderately pessimistic territory, according to data released Tuesday. It is an indicator that does not move in a straight line, but its persistence in negative territory suggests that Uruguayan households still do not perceive the adjustment cycle to be over. In that context, the combination of a relatively stable peso with depressed demand expectations creates a tension that authorities will need to manage carefully if domestic growth is to keep pace with the country's financial strength.

The regional dimension adds another layer of complexity. Brazil has become conspicuously cheaper — a direct effect of the real's depreciation — and that puts direct pressure on Uruguayan tourism. According to El Observador, Argentine tourists, who historically gravitated to Uruguayan beaches during the austral summer, could now "fly over" to Brazilian destinations, drawn by a price-to-quality ratio that a weak real makes hard for Uruguay to match. It is a concrete threat to a sector that represents a meaningful source of foreign currency during the high season, and it arrives at a moment when the BCB and the US Federal Reserve are maintaining monetary policy paths that continue to compress the room for maneuver of the continent's smaller emerging economies.

Itaú's 2027 projections, published Wednesday, confirm that this low-growth regional environment is not transitory. The bank anticipates modest expansion for Uruguay, Brazil and Argentina next year, in a scenario where the tailwinds that powered the post-pandemic recovery have dissipated and where global trade uncertainty — with tariffs as the dominant variable — continues to weigh on export expectations. For Uruguay, heavily dependent on regional demand and commodity prices, that is a framework that limits room for acceleration.

Against that backdrop, one data point stands out as illustrating Uruguay's reliance on discrete, high-intensity economic events: according to calculations published by El Observador, a Copa Libertadores final held on Uruguayan soil could contribute up to US$26 million to the local economy, while a top-tier international concert would generate roughly US$10.6 million in direct and indirect impact. The figures underscore how much a single event can mean for an economy the size of Uruguay's — and also how fragile a growth strategy that depends on their occurrence turns out to be.

What to watch in the coming weeks is the trajectory of the real bilateral exchange rate with Brazil, which will determine how much the tourism competitiveness gap widens or narrows before the summer season begins. Equally relevant will be the outcome of upcoming local debt auctions, which will test whether the market continues to validate the rate path set by the BCU. And on the external front, any move by the Federal Reserve or the BCB that alters capital flows to the region could ultimately put to the test the explanation that Oddone is still waiting to hear.
