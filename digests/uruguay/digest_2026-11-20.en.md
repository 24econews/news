# Uruguay: The Economic Pulse — 2026-11-20

Uruguay has become, somewhat awkwardly, the favorite talking point of Argentine Economy Minister Luis Caputo as he seeks to justify the patience his stabilization program demands. By publicly noting that it took Uruguay more than a decade to achieve single-digit inflation, Caputo not only implicitly acknowledged the scale of the challenge Buenos Aires faces, but also shed light on a paradox that now dominates the economic debate in Montevideo: the country that serves as the regional beacon of macroeconomic discipline is trapped in a low-growth cycle that unsettles analysts and the government itself.

The irony is hard to miss. While top-tier international media—from the BBC to Bloomberg—devote coverage to explaining why Uruguay is posting its lowest inflation in 70 years and what unusual challenges that reality poses, the Ceres Leading Index has fallen once again, reinforcing signals of weakening economic activity that have been accumulating since last year. The economy grew just 1.8% in 2025 according to BCU data, well below the 2.6% originally projected, and did so almost entirely on statistical carryover: the second half was contractionary, with declines of 0.8% and 0.2% in the second and third quarters, respectively. The start of 2026, in the words of several analysts consulted by El Observador, is essentially from a standing start.

What makes this situation especially complex is that it is not an isolated shock but rather the confluence of three simultaneous disturbances the government has had to absorb: turbulence in global markets tied to the new US trade policy cycle, weakness among regional partners—Argentina and Brazil are posting equally poor performances, as El Observador itself notes when describing regional stagnation—and pressures on competitiveness stemming from the so-called "country cost," a theme the Academia Nacional de Economía chose as the centerpiece of its 2026 prize under the title "Country Cost: An Agenda for Change Toward a More Competitive Uruguay." Against that triple backdrop, the Ministry of Economy announced concrete measures to reduce costs and streamline procedures to facilitate foreign trade, a signal that the Orsi administration recognizes that the competitiveness equation can no longer be deferred.

The Rendición de Cuentas submitted by the Executive projects a recovery in a global scenario that the document itself describes as "challenging and uncertain," reflecting the caution with which the authorities manage their own expectations. Standard & Poor's, which has maintained the country's credit rating, forecasts growth of just 1.1% for 2026, while Fitch Ratings, though keeping the investment grade, explicitly warns of low growth and a gradual rise in debt. The BCU, meanwhile, held its benchmark interest rate at 6% against an inflation backdrop that remains under control, a signal that monetary policy has no room—nor any immediate need—to act as a stimulus lever. Private-sector analysts, in turn, have again trimmed their 2026 growth projections, in a downward revision that has already been repeated several times in recent months.

Against that cautious backdrop, two pieces of news stand out from the gray tone of the macro cycle. The World Bank approved USD 300 million in financing to promote private investment and employment, an operation that signals institutional confidence in the solidity of Uruguay's framework. And Neltume Ports, a firm with a presence in South American ports, is proposing a USD 900 million container terminal in Montevideo, with the goal of positioning the port as a regional logistics hub. Both initiatives point to the same diagnosis the government and economists acknowledge: macroeconomic stability is consolidated, but translating it into sustained growth requires investment in infrastructure and a structural reduction in the cost of doing business.

Added to this is the growing attention on data centers, a sector that, according to Bloomberg Línea, could have a significant impact on the Uruguayan economy given the country's energy profile and institutional stability. The question left open in this week's debate—"stability or stagnation?", as El País framed it—accurately captures the tension that will define the year: Uruguay holds credentials its neighbors envy, but activity figures do not yet reflect the dividend that reputation should generate.

What will bear close watching in the coming weeks is whether the incipient rebound at the start of 2026—documented by consultancy Exante and confirmed by the BCU in the first quarter—manages to hold through the second half of the year, or whether weak external demand and regional headwinds once again stall the momentum. The potential approval of the Neltume Ports project and the government's negotiations with prospective investors in digital infrastructure will be key bellwethers. So too will be the BPS election process, where the business sector is already pushing for reforms in medical certifications and a reduction in state costs, a sign that the competitiveness agenda is far from exhausted by the measures announced this week by the Ministry of Economy.
