# Uruguay: Economic Pulse — 2026-09-18

The contraction of Uruguay's GDP in the second quarter of 2025, confirmed by the Central Bank at a 0.8% decline from the previous period, arrives at the worst possible moment: just as President Yamandú Orsi's government was presenting its Budget Accountability Report to Parliament and the World Bank was approving a $300 million loan to stimulate private investment. The tension between a relatively upbeat external diagnosis and deteriorating domestic data clearly defines Uruguay's current economic landscape.

The quarterly drop did not surprise the economic team. Minister Gabriel Oddone acknowledged that the contraction "was anticipated" and attributed it primarily to the impact of the drought on agriculture — a sector that dragged down the broader economy following the collapse in soybean and rice output — reaffirming the annual growth projection of 1.6% for 2025. However, data from El Observador paints a more structurally worrying picture: the economy is moving "at two speeds," with finance, agriculture and mining as the sole engines of the 4.4% growth recorded in the first part of the year, while industry and commerce remain sluggish. Household credit has now posted seven consecutive months of decline and delinquency rates are not easing, signaling a domestic consumer under pressure and weak internal demand that fails to complement the export sectors. Oddone himself was more candid in his medium-term projections, admitting to La Diaria that there is "a fairly high probability" that the growth forecast for 2026 will need to be revised downward, in line with the systematic downgrades issued by private analysts. CPA Ferrere, for example, is already projecting an advance of barely 1% next year, well below the official target.

It is in this context that the World Bank financing takes on a more political than technical dimension. The $300 million approved is explicitly earmarked for promoting private investment and employment, two variables that the multilateral itself identifies as Uruguay's key shortfalls. In a report cited by El Observador, the World Bank describes the country as a "superstar that stopped being one," noting that potential growth has been eroded by high costs, low productivity and insufficient private investment. The organization adds PISA test results and energy challenges to that list, shaping a diagnosis the government partly shares but confronts with limited instruments.

The Budget Accountability Report submitted to Parliament reflects this tension between ambition and constraint. Oddone proposes reactivating the economy in a global scenario he himself described as "challenging and uncertain" — with the war in the Middle East generating what he called a "negative shock" on energy prices — and without resorting to new taxes or a significant spending increase. The budget incorporates the global minimum tax on multinationals, with estimated revenues of $350 million, and the government has regulated its limits in accordance with fiscal stability clauses. Simultaneously, the Ministry of Economy is designing changes to the investment regime and advancing a competitiveness bill whose review has begun in the Chamber of Deputies. The official philosophy is to lower costs for businesses without pursuing deep deregulation, a stance that experts consulted by El Observador consider insufficient to reverse what they call the "Uruguayan paralysis": political peace and macroeconomic stability, they note, are no longer differentiating advantages but expected minimums.

On the currency front, the dollar continues to face downward pressure, a situation the Ministry of Economy acknowledges could "severely affect competitiveness." The MEF announced palliative measures — including a reduction in the IMESI fuel tax discount along the Argentine border and an evaluation of extending it along the litoral region — and the president of ARU warned that the agricultural sector is "in critical condition." The peso's strength against the dollar, in a context of inflation that both the BBC and the IMF highlight as the lowest in 70 years, generates the paradox of a country praised for its monetary stability while simultaneously watching its competitive position erode.

Adding to this are new frictions in the port sector, where the TCP union has launched an indefinite strike that has kept the container terminal operating with minimum services, and the controversy generated by Neltume Ports' announcement of a $900 million project for a new terminal in Montevideo that could clash with the interests of Katoen Natie. In the offshore sector, Italy's ENI joined the block where the first exploratory well will be drilled, teaming up with YPF in a bet that could change the country's energy equation over the medium term.

What lies ahead is a dense agenda. The competitiveness bill must advance through Parliament amid an intense fiscal debate, with the opposition questioning changes to the Fonasa refund and coalition partners demanding more forceful support measures. The Central Bank has also presented a preliminary bill to create an open finance system, while the government has announced reforms to the AFAP regime and a new employment law. The indicator to watch most closely in the coming weeks will be the August economic activity data — which, according to the Ceres think tank, has already "reinforced signals of a loss of momentum" — and the government's ability to hold its year-end projection without revising it downward yet again.
