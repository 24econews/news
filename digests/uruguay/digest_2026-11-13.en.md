# Uruguay: The Economic Pulse — 2026-11-13

Uruguay's economy closed 2025 on a sour note that complicates the new administration's opening act: GDP posted consecutive quarterly contractions in the second and third quarters of the year — of 0.8% and 0.2% respectively, according to data published by El Observador — turning annual growth into an almost purely statistical phenomenon, sustained by the positive carryover from the first quarter rather than by genuine dynamism. The more troubling reading is not the recent past but what lies ahead: analysts have again trimmed their projections for 2026, and market consensus suggests the year is starting, in the words of economists cited by El Observador, "zero-zero," with no accumulated momentum to serve as a cushion.

The central tension defining Uruguay's economic moment is this: the country enjoys an enviable macroeconomic reputation in the region — Standard & Poor's maintains its credit rating with a stable outlook and projects 1.1% growth for 2026, Moody's also assigns a stable outlook, and the IMF praised the "solid macroeconomic policies" in its latest Article IV Consultation — but that stability is not translating into expansion. Fitch Ratings, which held its investment grade rating, was perhaps the most blunt, explicitly warning about low growth and rising debt as structural risks. The president of the Banco Central, according to La Diaria, was equally emphatic: the "ultra-conservative" profile in monetary management "is costing the country dearly," and Uruguay is not fully leveraging its stability to grow.

Against this backdrop, the Ceres Leading Index has swung between contradictory signals over recent months, reflecting the absence of a clear trend. After four consecutive readings with no growth, it posted a 0.2% rise in April before falling again, reinforcing what the index itself calls signs of weakening. Business confidence matches that somber tone: only one in four executives expects their company's profitability to improve next year, according to a survey reported by El Observador — a figure that starkly illustrates the mood in the private sector.

The new government of Yamandú Orsi — with Gabriel Oddone at the helm of the Ministry of Economy and Finance, a post to which he was appointed on the strength of his standing as an independent economist — has inherited this scenario with no room for complacency. Oddone, who in his first weeks leading the MEF ruled out stagnation and reaffirmed the fiscal course, now faces a parliamentary interpellation and a growing public debate over whether the available tools are sufficient. His stance has been consistent: there will be no additional tax changes, the government does not need more resources, and the 1% wealth tax — a rallying cry for sectors of the Frente Amplio — "is not applied anywhere in the world." Fiscal discipline, in short, remains the anchor. But the lingering question is whether that anchor is also holding back growth.

On the trade policy and competitiveness front, the MEF announced measures to reduce costs and streamline procedures to facilitate foreign trade, in parallel with legislative progress on a competitiveness bill aimed at easing burdens on SMEs and making goods cheaper for lower-income households. The Mercosur-European Union agreement, whose first six months in force have already generated data on Uruguayan exports to the European bloc, represents one of the few structurally positive stories, though its full effects will take years to materialize. On the energy and transition front, the HIF Uruguay solar park in Paysandú — linked to the green hydrogen project — entered its public consultation phase, a sign that the long-term investment agenda continues to advance even against a backdrop of weak demand.

The Financial Stability Committee delivered a reassuring message: Uruguay's financial system "is well prepared to absorb hypothetical adverse scenarios," an assessment that took on added weight following the collapse of Ueno Bank and the crisis at Grupo Vázquez in Paraguay, whose fallout also reached Uruguayan workers. The BCU president highlighted the soundness of the local system in the face of that regional episode, reinforcing the narrative of institutional resilience that Uruguay projects abroad.

What to watch closely in the coming weeks is both varied and urgent. Parliament's handling of the competitiveness bill will determine whether the government can offer the private sector concrete signs of relief before the deterioration in business sentiment crystallizes into investment decisions. Minister Oddone's tour of the United Kingdom to "strengthen economic and financial ties" will indicate how much external political capital the new government is seeking to accumulate. And the first-quarter 2026 data — which, according to some economists, already show a slight pickup from the 2025 stagnation — will be the first test of whether the downcycle has bottomed out, or whether the year that is starting "zero-zero" risks ending in negative territory.
