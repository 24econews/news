Uruguay tested international markets this week in what amounts to the clearest signal yet that the Ministry of Economy and Finance is looking to anchor its financing strategy before the global environment turns more complicated. According to El Observador, the government is sounding out foreign investors about a possible new sovereign debt issuance — a move that takes on added weight when read alongside the latest activity data: the economy grew just 0.8% in the first quarter of 2026 versus the fourth quarter of 2025, and the Ceres leading index has been sending mixed signals about the direction of the cycle for months.

The backdrop against which Uruguay is seeking external funding is not straightforward. Minister Gabriel Oddone publicly acknowledged that there is "a fairly high probability" of revising growth projections downward for 2026, further conceding that external shocks — among them, the impact of the war in the Middle East on energy prices — represent a "negative shock" to the economy. That the government is testing investor appetite at precisely this moment says something about how it reads the available timeline: act before the window closes, capitalizing on the fact that Uruguay still holds the lowest country risk in Latin America and a credit rating that Fitch places among the strongest in the region.

The macro picture surrounding this financial operation is marked by a tension that isn't new but has sharpened: Uruguay grew in 2025 largely on statistical carryover, and 2026 began, in the words of several analysts consulted by El Observador, "flat as a pancake." The Ceres leading index showed gains of 0.2% in April and 0.3% in May — two consecutive months of expansion, yes, but of a magnitude that hardly inspires enthusiasm — while the economy contracted 0.2% in the third quarter of last year and registered a decline in October. The World Bank cut its growth projection for Uruguay to 1.6% this year and 1.7% next year, figures Oddone himself has flagged as likely to be revised lower.

Competitiveness is the exposed nerve. The rural sector has been warning for months that the exchange rate has left it "at the breaking point": the Uruguayan peso remains, according to the Big Mac index, the most overvalued currency in the world in relative terms, which puts agro-industrial exporters in a structurally difficult position against regional competitors. At the same time, the wheat harvest is expected to bring in roughly USD 3.9 billion to the economy — a record figure that cushions the blow — and the government is betting that three large private-sector projects — green hydrogen, data centers, and pulp — will be the engines sustaining activity in the second half of the year and into 2027. These are long-term bets in sectors with genuine global demand, but their impact on this year's GDP is necessarily limited.

On the fiscal front, Oddone was explicit at the Council of Ministers: there will be no expansion of spending beyond what is contemplated in the 2027 budget. The Accountability Report, which just cleared Parliament, included resource reallocations without opening new funding sources, reflecting a narrow margin. The government is also working on a competitiveness bill — with declared inspiration from similar reforms in the region — and on changes to the investment regime, although the MEF was clear that reducing tax expenditure "is not a relevant strategy" at this juncture.
