The irrevocable withdrawal of the international arbitration that Montecon's shareholders had initiated against Uruguay before ICSID closes today one of the most politically sensitive investment disputes in the country in recent years, precisely as the Frente Amplio government needs to project institutional stability against a macroeconomic backdrop that is accumulating troubling signals.

The withdrawal—explicitly characterized as irrevocable—brings to a close a dispute that had emerged from the port conflict surrounding Montevideo's container terminal and that had drawn the Uruguayan state before the World Bank's arbitration tribunal. The case's resolution removes a contingent liability from the fiscal balance sheet at a moment when the Consejo Fiscal Autónomo has just warned Parliament that the Ministry of Economy and Finance has engaged in an "overestimation" of the revenue projections included in the Rendición de Cuentas. If the independent body is correct, the government's room for maneuver is even narrower than official figures acknowledge, and the space for any extraordinary outlay—such as would have been required by an adverse ICSID award—is practically nonexistent.

The fiscal context takes on greater significance in light of the activity data released in recent days. The Uruguayan economy grew a mere 0.4% in the second quarter, a figure that several analysts consider disappointing even relative to modest prior expectations; some tracking models even point to a quarter-on-quarter contraction. Ceres's Leading Index, which anticipates activity's trajectory several months ahead, has fallen once again, reinforcing the signs of weakening that had already been accumulating since late 2025. The institute notes that the economy enters 2026 decelerated and without clear signs of recovery—a description that contrasts with the cautiously optimistic tone that Economy Minister Gabriel Oddone has maintained in his recent public appearances.

The contradiction between public perception and the mood of the organized private sector is perhaps the most revealing data point of the moment. According to El Observador, the pessimism of the general population clashes with the optimism expressed by businesspeople and investors, most of whom project economic stability for 2026 and cite competitiveness—not growth itself—as their main concern. That divergence is not trivial: it suggests that the cycle is asymmetrically affecting household incomes and corporate margins, or else that popular perception is capturing a distributional reality that aggregate indicators do not adequately reflect.

Against that backdrop, the government has opted for a strategy of gradual signals toward the private sector. The MEF has announced measures to stimulate private investment, which is showing signs of "modest growth" according to La Diaria, and Minister Oddone has confirmed that a bill will be introduced to improve competitiveness, with an emphasis on reducing red tape and targeted tax adjustments, but stopping short of broad deregulation. The consultation of Argentine economist Federico Sturzenegger in drafting that bill—reported by El Economista—has generated political noise, given the ideological profile of the man who was the architect of Milei's deregulation reforms. The government has been quick to clarify that no programmatic link exists between the two agendas.

The agribusiness sector delivers today's most positive note. That export inflow would arrive at a moment when the dollar's position within the exchange rate band remains a subject of debate: MEF sources noted that the greenback could approach the band's floor in the coming months, which would ease import pressures but would aggravate the agricultural sector's concerns about external competitiveness, a tension that the president of the Asociación Rural del Uruguay has already described as a "breaking point."

On the financial front, the Financial Stability Committee has communicated that Uruguay's banking system is "well prepared to absorb hypothetical adverse scenarios," a routine declaration that nonetheless takes on greater weight given the context of slower growth and the reforms underway to the AFAP regime. The government confirmed in the framework of the Diálogo Social that it will push for transformations to the pension fund system, the precise scope of which has yet to be publicly detailed.

On sovereign risk, Uruguay retains its position as the region's issuer with the lowest spread, ahead of Chile, Paraguay and Peru—a strength that the IMF itself has recognized in recent visits. That same institution, however, has urged the country to push its growth rate with greater determination, while DBRS Morningstar has warned that economic performance remains "mediocre" by regional standards.

What will bear watching in the coming weeks is whether the competitiveness bill announced by Oddone can advance through Parliament with sufficient speed to shift expectations, and whether third-quarter data confirms or reverses the deceleration trend flagged by both the BCU and Ceres's Leading Index. Also in focus will be the construction sector's response to the collateral effects of the reduction in the workweek—which according to El Observador has generated a domino effect with negative consequences for the sector's activity—as well as the government's ability to keep the fiscal deficit around the 4.1% of GDP at which 2025 closed, in a year when the MEF's revenue projections are being questioned from within the institutional fiscal-control framework itself.
