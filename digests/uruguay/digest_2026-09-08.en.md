# Uruguay: The Economic Pulse — 2026-09-08

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-09-08*

Uruguay finds itself at the center of an economic tension rarely articulated as clearly as in today's news flow: the country boasts the strongest financial credentials in Latin America, yet its real economy cannot translate that strength into genuine growth, and the gap between citizen perception and the business community's outlook has become politically unsustainable.

The most revealing data point of the day is not macroeconomic but political-perceptual. According to El Observador, half of Uruguayans consider the country's economic situation to be poor, while businesspeople and investors maintain a marked optimism. This "economic divide"—as the outlet calls it—is not merely a government communication problem: it is the symptom of a two-speed economy, where the financial sector and export-oriented firms capture the benefits of stability, while sectors representing 44% of private employment continue to contract or stagnate.

The underlying activity picture confirms the worst suspicions. New data suggest that the Uruguayan economy stalled in the second quarter, with estimates pointing to a contraction of up to 0.7%, according to uy press, contrasting with the 0.8% growth recorded in the first quarter. The Ceres Leading Index, which had posted two consecutive months of expansion, fell again, reinforcing signs of weakening. The Central Bank had already flagged that the economy "is evolving below its potential growth," and Economy Minister Gabriel Oddone himself acknowledged before Parliament that 2025 growth was explained almost entirely by "statistical carryover," leaving 2026 to start "zero to zero." ECLAC trimmed its growth projection for Uruguay to just 1.5% for 2026, in line with private consultancies such as CPA Ferrere, which projects only 1%, well below the government's expectations.

Against this backdrop, the Ministry of Economy and Finance's decision to sound out international investors for a new sovereign debt issuance carries a dual reading. On one hand, it reflects Uruguay's enviable market position: the country has the lowest country risk in Latin America, its investment grade was upgraded two notches by Standard & Poor's, and the MEF received "strong interest" from investors for new peso-denominated notes, doubling the peso Note issuance after receiving bids for nearly $9.7 billion. Oddone will also head a mission to the United Kingdom to strengthen financial ties. On the other hand, the very need for external financing is a reminder that the fiscal deficit closed the previous period at 4.7% of GDP, and that the outgoing administration left a public debt that grew by more than ten percentage points of GDP during its tenure. That Uruguay can tap international markets on favorable terms is a real achievement; that it must do so while growth decelerates raises questions about fiscal sustainability.

The Frente Amplio's agenda adds political tension to the economic picture. The government confirmed it will push for "a set of transformations" in the AFAP regime, prompting an opposition interpellation of Minister Oddone and a response from the Partido Nacional, which will summon the minister to Parliament in general committee format. Oddone dismissed the notion that this amounts to "nationalization," calling it an "exaggeration," and assured there will be "no confiscation." The AFAPs, for their part, welcomed the "progress achieved" in negotiations with the Executive. The debate revives fractures within the Frente Amplio itself, with internal criticism over neglecting the party's "social base."

On the competitiveness front, which businesses identify as their main concern for 2026 according to the El Observador survey, the government announced a bill to enhance it and the MEF took concrete steps: reducing red tape and taxes. The Unión Industrial Arrocera warned, meanwhile, that the fiscal pressure on the formal economy is "the highest in the world," an assertion that resonates in a context where the government also confirmed it will include the global minimum tax on multinationals in the budget bill, with expected revenue of $350 million. The World Bank was more direct: Uruguay was a "superstar" that has lost ground, and it sees further cooling of its economy.

A genuinely positive note comes from the agro-industrial sector, where a record wheat harvest could contribute some $3.9 billion to the economy. And services exports from the Knowledge Economy posted record growth, signaling that structural transformation is advancing—though at a pace insufficient to offset stagnation in traditional sectors.

What to watch in the coming weeks is the execution of the sovereign issuance in international markets and the terms Uruguay is able to negotiate, which will serve as a thermometer of real external confidence. The parliamentary debate over the AFAPs will determine whether the government can advance a sensitive reform without losing political cohesion. And second-quarter data, once officially released, will tell whether the cooling that leading indicators now anticipate was transitory or marks the beginning of a trend that will force the MEF to revise its budget downward before the year ends.
