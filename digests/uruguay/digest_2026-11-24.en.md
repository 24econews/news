# Uruguay: Economic Pulse — 2026-11-24

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-11-24*

Uruguay's economy enters 2026 carrying a burden the government itself concedes it had not anticipated: last year's growth was driven almost exclusively by the statistical carryover from prior quarters, meaning the country begins the new fiscal year from a practically neutral base. According to analysts cited by El Observador, the economy "grew in 2025 almost entirely by carryover" and 2026 "starts zero to zero" — a formulation that captures with unusual precision the central dilemma facing the new economic team led by Minister Gabriel Oddone.

The diagnosis rests on a sequence of data that hardly allows for an optimistic reading. After a first quarter of 2025 that posted gains of just 0.8%, the economy contracted 0.8% in the second quarter and then shed another 0.2% in the third, according to BCU data. In August, monthly activity fell 1.1% versus July, breaking a modest recovery registered that month relative to the prior period. Together, these figures paint a picture of persistent stagnation that has already triggered political fallout: more than half of Uruguayans identify the economy as the country's main problem, according to a poll reported by El Observador, and the opposition — the Coalición Republicana — has publicly called for a "reaction" from the government on economic, fiscal and drinking-water fronts.

Minister Oddone has not dodged the reality. In the Rendición de Cuentas presented before Parliament, he argued that projection deviations "are a common occurrence" and insisted the economy is on a "recovery path," albeit a slow one. He also made clear there will be no new taxes — flatly ruling out the 1% wealth levy pushed by sectors within the Frente Amplio, which he described as something that "is not applied anywhere in the world" — and that the government is not seeking additional revenue. The strategy, instead, runs through modifying the fiscal rule to embed it in the five-year budget, stimulating private investment — which the Ministry of Economy described as showing signs of "modest growth" — and redesigning the AFAP regime, a reform the government confirmed within the framework of the Social Dialogue and for which it has already established a formal channel of engagement with the pension fund administrators.

The tension between fiscal consolidation and economic dynamism runs through the entire agenda. The Fiscal Advisory Council reviewed 2024 public finance slippages it described as "concerning," while the Ministry of Economy itself activated the safeguard clause to raise the debt ceiling, a signal that fiscal margins are tighter than expected. Fitch Ratings held its investment grade but explicitly warned about low growth and rising debt — a combination the agencies are watching with increasing attention across the region. Standard & Poor's, by contrast, upgraded Uruguay's investment grade by two notches in an earlier decision the government hailed as recognition of the country's institutional strength.

Against that backdrop, the decision to issue a global bond for €1.25 billion — the first debt placement in that currency in two decades — is both a demonstration of privileged access to international markets and a signal of the financing needs the administration faces. Oddone's tour of the United Kingdom to "strengthen economic and financial ties" points in the same direction: Uruguay is seeking to diversify its investor base and consolidate its reputation in European markets while navigating a global environment the government describes as "challenging and uncertain."

Two structural elements warrant sustained attention. The first is the growing weight of data centers in the national economy: according to Bloomberg Línea, the potential impact of this sector is significant, and the government itself is in talks with players such as Google while evaluating other large-scale tech projects. The second is the legislative process around the competitiveness law, which began its passage in the Chamber of Deputies with qualified support — the opposition warned it will not vote on the competition-defense changes "as they stand" — and whose final approval will partly determine the government's capacity to attract investment in the sectors most exposed to regional competition.

The external front adds another layer of complexity. President Orsi acknowledged that the economic situation will force the government to "ease off the accelerator in some cases," while the Nobel laureate in Economics cited by La Diaria directly warned Uruguay that it will have to "fight against Trump's protectionism." With analysts already trimming their 2026 growth forecasts and the World Bank approving $300 million in financing to promote private investment and employment, the coming weeks will be marked by parliamentary negotiation of the Rendición de Cuentas — where Oddone insisted he "will not give up on negotiating" — and by the release of fourth-quarter activity data, which will determine whether the 2025 stagnation extends or whether the economy finally sets foot on a path of genuine growth.
