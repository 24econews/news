# Argentina: The Economic Pulse — 2026-10-13

The defining data point of today's session is not the IMF downgrade itself —which was anticipated— but rather the speed at which signs of simultaneous strain are piling up across every front: growth that the Fund cut from 3.5% to 2.1% for 2026, September inflation that INDEC releases today and that the market pegs at around 1.9%, a global sovereign bond crisis that is punishing Argentina more than almost any other emerging market, and an Economy Minister negotiating in Bangkok for a USD 869 million disbursement while back in Buenos Aires bankruptcies mount, delinquency hits record highs, and an open debate rages within the Cabinet itself over whether the real exchange rate is sustainable. The Argentina of twin surpluses and historic export volumes coexists, today with striking tension, with a domestic economy that cannot get off its knees.

The International Monetary Fund released on Monday, as part of its Annual Meetings in Bangkok, its October World Economic Outlook, and the number for Argentina was unambiguous: 2.1% growth for 2026, down from the 3.5% the institution had ratified in July. The 1.4 percentage point cut in just three months is no marginal technical adjustment; it is the second downward revision this year, after the World Bank had already trimmed its projection from 3.6% to 2.1% in the first week of October. At the same time, the IMF raised its average annual inflation estimate for Argentina to 32.5%, from the 30.4% forecast six months ago. President Javier Milei himself previewed the cut hours before its release, in remarks to Radio Mitre, dismissing the notion that two consecutive quarters of GDP contraction amount to a technical recession: "Falling 10% is not the same as falling less than 1%," he argued. Economist Miguel Ángel Broda, however, was more severe: by his analysis, Argentina's country risk climbed 173 basis points between July and early October —surpassed only by Ukraine, a country at war, among the fifteen nations he tracked— and financing for 2027 has become "more demanding" in a context where the 10-year US Treasury yield has reached 5.24% annualized, its highest level since 2003.

That external backdrop is the real stage for Luis Caputo's visit to Bangkok, where the minister is seeking to close the third review of the program with the IMF and unlock a still-pending USD 869 million disbursement. The technical evaluation, according to sources within the institution, has not yet concluded with a staff-level agreement. The Argentine delegation, which includes BCRA president Santiago Bausili and vice president Vladimir Werning, arrives at a moment when the global sovereign bond crisis has deepened. France hit a country risk of 160 basis points —the highest since 2002 for a European issuer— and energy tensions are escalating: Brent crude surpassed USD 104 on Monday, driven by attacks on Ukrainian infrastructure, instability in the Strait of Hormuz, and the agreement between Washington and Moscow for the United States to source Russian diesel. For Argentina, which runs an energy surplus thanks to Vaca Muerta, expensive oil is simultaneously a tailwind for exports and pressure on domestic inflation: gasoline has risen 23% in dollar terms year-to-date.

The most revealing tension of the day, however, is not in Bangkok but in the exchange rate analysis Milei brought to the Cabinet itself. The BCRA's Multilateral Real Exchange Rate Index stood in September 24% below its historical average. Former Goldman Sachs chief economist Alberto Ades —who addressed the Cabinet last week in an episode that generated rumors of a Caputo replacement, swiftly denied by both men and by Milei— laid out that lag in stark terms. Equilibra's chief economist, Lorena Giorgio, was even more direct: the currency band scheme carries "the serious risk of a 25% jump in the exchange rate without the BCRA being able to intervene to halt a run." An appreciated peso cheapens imports and squeezes industrial margins, but benefits energy, mining, and agriculture. It is that duality that explains why the agro-export sector has shipped nearly 90 million tons over the first nine months of 2026 —surpassing the all-time annual record set in 2021— while the textile industry suffers its worst crisis in four decades, according to the CEO of Cocot Dufour, manufacturing employment fell 5.2% in the first quarter of the year, and 32,290 companies have vanished from the formal registry since Milei took office.

Delinquency is today the variable that best illustrates that fracture. The non-performing loan portfolio at banks climbed to 7.7% from 3.2% a year earlier. In non-bank lending, 26.5% is in arrears, versus 16% twelve months ago. Among young people up to age 25, default rates on retail chain financing reach 63%, according to the Debt Map compiled by the Centro de Estudios para la Ciudad. Half of the borrowers who refinanced between April and August remain in the same delinquency category. That credit deterioration acts as a structural brake on the recovery of domestic consumption the government needs before the 2027 elections: retailers face their most challenging Mother's Day in years, real wages in registered private employment strung together three consecutive months of decline over the July–September period, and the monetary base contracted in real terms, with fewer pesos in circulation than a year ago.

Against that gloomy domestic backdrop, the Argentina of natural resources continues to build a radically different long-term narrative. In Catamarca, four lithium plants are inaugurating facilities this month with a combined investment of USD 2 billion. The province of Neuquén posted its all-time monthly revenue record in September —ARS 746.396 billion, equivalent to USD 484.7 million— thanks to royalties from Vaca Muerta. The government made official in the Official Gazette the inclusion of Los Toldos II Este, the Tecpetrol project from the Techint Group, in the RIGI: USD 6.4 billion of investment, with a target output of 70,000 barrels per day. A mission of thirty Texas-based companies will arrive in Neuquén on October 18 under the auspices of the Argentine-Texan Chamber of Commerce and the Atlantic Council. Miguel Galuccio, CEO of Vista Energy —the company in which Peter Thiel acquired a 1% stake for USD 76 million in August— argued in Silicon Valley that Argentina can turn itself into a data center platform by leveraging Vaca Muerta gas, given that "transporting data costs between 90% and 99% less than transporting gas or electricity."

What the market is watching this afternoon is the September inflation figure INDEC will publish at 4 p.m. The private consensus puts it at 1.9%, an acceleration of 0.2 points from the 1.7% registered in August. If the number tops 2%, it will reinforce doubts about the pace of the disinflation process; if it comes in below, the government will have an argument to defend the exchange rate anchor as working. In parallel, the evolution of country risk —which closed last week at 589 basis points after dropping nearly 60 units from its peak of 655— will remain the thermometer of the program's credibility. BBVA Research anticipated that 2026 will close with "the lowest primary surplus of the Milei era," at around 1.3% of GDP. Sustaining that result in 2027, an election year with heavy debt maturities and international market access still blocked by the level of sovereign risk, is the test the economic team must pass in the coming months.
