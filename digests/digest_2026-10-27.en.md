# Argentina: Economic Pulse — 2026-10-27

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-10-27*

Lula da Silva's victory in Sunday's Brazilian runoff triggered on Monday the largest external shock Argentine assets have endured so far this year, and Tuesday's session confirms that the episode was no passing jolt but rather the catalyst for a vulnerability that had already been building. The country risk index compiled by J.P. Morgan jumped 57 points in a single session to 765 basis points, its highest level since late October 2025, and has now climbed 75% from the 403-point low recorded in July. The telling detail is not the number itself but its regional comparison: while Argentina's country risk rose more than 20% on the month, Peru's climbed 12% and Colombia's 9%, and Brazil's — paradoxically, the epicenter of the political earthquake — barely moved. That differential, documented by Fundación Capital, is the moment's most unsettling signal: the market is punishing Argentina for domestic reasons, and Brazil was simply the trigger.

The transmission channel was immediate and multi-pronged. The Bovespa plunged more than 16% at Monday's open — tripping the circuit breaker for the first time since March 2020 — and the real depreciated as much as 12% in futures markets before moderating its decline to 8.2% at the close, settling at 5.40 reais per dollar. In Buenos Aires, the effect was more contained but no less significant: the wholesale dollar climbed to $1,541, Global bonds fell 2.1% on average, and the MSCI Merval shed 3% to 99,227 points. The contado con liquidación advanced to $1,679, widening the gap with the official exchange rate to around 9%. The Central Bank, which had bought dollars in 194 of the 199 sessions between January and the previous Friday, opted to stay on the sidelines: a neutral balance, no net purchases, a pause the market read as a sign of caution amid the storm. According to La Nación, market sources suggest the National Treasury intervened with its own sales to contain the rise in the wholesale dollar, thereby sparing the BCRA from having to sell reserves — which would only have been possible had the exchange rate breached the top of the currency band, set at $1,946.73.

The hardest-hit company was Mercado Libre, whose ADRs on Wall Street fell as much as 15% at Monday's open before recovering some ground to close down 7.8%. The explanation is straightforward: Brazil accounts for 52.6% of consolidated net revenue at the company founded by Marcos Galperin, with $15.201 billion in 2025. The real's depreciation is a double-edged risk: it reduces the dollar value of revenue generated in Brazilian currency even if sales volumes remain stable. Behind Mercado Libre on the decliners' list were Banco Supervielle (-9.4%), BBVA Argentina (-8%), and Grupo Financiero Galicia (-6.8%).

What makes the juncture especially uncomfortable for Minister Luis Caputo's economic team is that the external shock lands on an economy that was already showing signs of internal weakness before the Brazilian runoff. The seasonally adjusted EMAE for August grew just 0.1% following July's 2.9% decline — the sharpest monthly drop since April 2020 — and the trend-cycle indicator posted five consecutive months at zero after Indec revised the series. Morgan Stanley cut its 2026 growth forecast to 1.3%, below the market consensus of 1.5%, and considers a technical recession the most likely scenario. The official September reading, which Indec will publish on November 24, could formally confirm it. Manufacturing has accumulated a 2.7% decline in the first eight months of the year, metallurgical capacity utilization in September touched its lowest floor for that month since 2016, and formal private employment has strung together 14 consecutive months of contraction: 266,000 jobs lost since November 2023, according to UBA's Instituto Interdisciplinario de Economía Política, leaving formal employment at May 2014 levels. A Bumeran report notes that 87% of workers consider their salary insufficient to cover basic needs, and 74% say their purchasing power has worsened in recent months.

Against that backdrop, the government displays a paradox that the Financial Times summarized this week under the headline "Milei's economic reforms under pressure": public accounts show a primary surplus of 1.2% of GDP in the first nine months of the year, September exports topped USD 10 billion for the first time in 13 years, and the BCRA has accumulated reserve purchases of more than USD 15.8 billion in 2026, tripling its annual target with the IMF. Vaca Muerta contributed 698,500 barrels per day in September, a record equivalent to 71.7% of national output, and the energy sector was the main engine behind the export record. Yet that dynamism is failing to pull the domestic market along, and country risk — which should fall given solid fiscal fundamentals — has climbed 75% since July. Fundación Capital puts it bluntly: the deterioration reflects domestic factors, not regional ones, tied to the cooling of activity and doubts about the sustainability of the financing program in 2027.

In that context, the government is advancing on multiple regulatory fronts. It formalized the new Cold Zone Regime via Decree 1266/2026, which limits the 50% gas price subsidy to Patagonia, Malargüe, and the Puna, excluding the 94 municipalities that had benefited from post-2021 expansions. The Energy Secretariat published Resolution 299/2026 with the operational mechanics. It also inaugurated the upgrade of the Cordillerano-Patagónico System, a USD 40 million project executed by Camuzzi that connects 25 localities in Chubut, Río Negro, and Neuquén to the Gasoducto General San Martín, enabling 12,500 new service hookups and linking the system to Vaca Muerta for the first time. On the fiscal side, ARCA regulated Inocencia Fiscal II through General Resolution 5916/2026, eliminating income and asset ceilings for enrollment in the simplified Income Tax regime, although specialists warn that pending definitions from the UIF — due by November 2 — are needed before taxpayers can mobilize mattress dollars without triggering anti-money-laundering reports. The fintech sector, meanwhile, projects USD 2.2 billion in investment in 2027 and 28% real revenue growth, according to the Cámara Argentina Fintech.

What the market is monitoring from here is the triangle of tensions that will define the coming months: the evolution of country risk and its impact on the Treasury's capacity to issue dollar debt in the local market — a central piece of Caputo's financing program that is now in question — the depth of the technical recession that the September reading could certify on November 24, and currency dynamics in the regional neighborhood after Bolsonarismo's defeat in Brazil. The BCRA reduced its short position in dollar futures contracts to just USD 42 million as of September 30, effectively zero, which restores room to deploy that instrument as a shock absorber against potential currency pressures in the run-up to the 2027 election. The question is whether the USD 75 billion "bazooka" that Milei touted in Paris will be enough if country risk remains at levels that shut off access to the voluntary debt market.

## Corporate Watch

### Mercado Libre (NASDAQ: MELI)
ADRs of the company led by Marcos Galperin closed Monday down 7.8% on Wall Street following Lula da Silva's reelection, given that Brazil accounts for 52.6% of its consolidated global revenue; the stock fell as much as 15% at the open before recovering part of the ground, trading around USD 1,644 at the session's close.

### Moolec Science (NASDAQ: MOOL)
Shareholders approved the voluntary liquidation of the biotech company with 98.4% of the vote at an extraordinary meeting held on October 20, appointing liquidators from Interpath (Cayman) Limited to conduct the process under Cayman Islands law; the decision closes the cycle of a company once valued at more than USD 1 billion that lost control of Bioceres Crop Solutions (NASDAQ: BIOX) in June 2025.

### Stellantis (NYSE: STLA / Euronext: STLAM)
The Argentine subsidiary confirmed that the Ferreyra, Córdoba plant — where the Fiat Cronos, Fiat Titano, and Ram Dakota are produced — will halt production for seven weeks starting December 1, three weeks longer than the customary holiday shutdown, as a result of weaker-than-expected demand in both the local market and Brazil; the Palomar plant, meanwhile, has been operating on a single shift since May for the same reason.
