# Bolivia: The Economic Pulse — 2026-09-21

*Analysis generated with AI | 2026-09-21*

Bolivia is living through its first full day without a diesel subsidy, and the most immediate data point emerging from the pumps —one that to some extent defies the worst predictions— is that the kilometer-long queues that for months paralyzed truckers and weighed on agribusiness have begun to dissipate. The fuel price has definitively shifted to Bs 17.95 per liter, up from previously subsidized levels, in what constitutes the most drastic structural reform executed by President Rodrigo Paz's government since he took office. The market signal, finally honest, appears to be doing its job: the smuggling that the old price differential invited is losing its rationale, and supply is beginning to normalize. But the relief is provisional, and the political and economic cost of the transition is only just starting to accumulate.

The fiscal dimension of the measure is undeniable. The government has enacted Law 1765, which formalizes the $1.9 billion contract with the International Monetary Fund, approved by a two-thirds majority in both the Chamber of Deputies and the Senate —legislative backing that is not trivial in the Bolivian context. President Paz publicly thanked the Legislature for the approval and stated that the credit will help stabilize the economy, while the Ministry of Economy clarified that the funds will be earmarked to bolster Net International Reserves rather than to finance payrolls or increase basic service tariffs. Disbursements will be quarterly and conditional on meeting adjustment targets, turning the agreement into a mechanism of continuous accountability vis-à-vis a multilateral body. The government also raised, by decree, to Bs 6 billion the fund available for fuel purchases throughout the year, a signal that eliminating the subsidy does not mean walking away from the market but rather recalibrating how and to whom resources are transferred.

In parallel, the Ministry of Economy reported that Bolivia posted a fiscal surplus in the first five months of 2026 —including a surplus of roughly Bs 2.3 billion in January— reversing a deficit trend that became a defining feature of the previous Luis Arce administration's model. The reduction in central-government spending is around Bs 8 billion, according to statements from the minister himself. Country risk, which climbed above 1,000 basis points during the most acute phase of the crisis, has fallen below 500 basis points, placing Bolivia below Argentina and Ecuador on that indicator —in relative terms, a sign of recovering confidence from international markets.

Even so, the elimination of the diesel subsidy simultaneously activates multiple pressures the government cannot ignore. The Central Obrera Boliviana has convened a national plenary to reject what it has dubbed the "dieselazo." The coca growers of Trópico de Cochabamba declared themselves in a state of emergency and announced protests starting Monday. The Catholic Church, through the Archbishop of La Paz Percy Galván, called for a "propositive dialogue" and offered itself as mediator, a gesture that reveals the magnitude of the social tension. Public Works Minister Mauricio Zamora met with truckers both in La Paz —at YPFB's facilities— and in Santa Cruz, where he proposed accelerating the conversion of the fleet to Vehicular Natural Gas as a relief valve for the sector. Zamora himself acknowledged that the transition will place pressure on transport, though he defended the reform's inevitability with a phrase that captures the underlying consensus among analysts: "lifting the diesel subsidy would have fallen to whichever government was in power."

YPFB was emphatic in ruling out changes to gasoline prices —"for now it's only diesel," the government reiterated— and asked the population to avoid speculation. The government instructed mayors to monitor prices and insisted that diesel is not "a pretext for profiteering." For now, urban passenger transport fares have not changed, though the accumulated pressure on the sector's operating costs makes that stability look fragile if international fuel prices do not retreat. The National Chamber of Commerce, for its part, endorsed the single diesel price and demanded guarantees of supply and transparency, while more than 1,300 license plates were blocked nationwide for engaging in repetitive fuel loading —a figure that illustrates the scale of the smuggling and speculation that the old system fed.

The government also issued regulations exempting tariffs and VAT on goods destined for production, a measure aimed at cushioning the impact of the diesel price hike on domestic industry. Still, the business sector identified five factors aggravating the crisis: exchange-rate uncertainty, foreign-currency scarcity, falling tax revenues, contraction of productive credit, and weakness of domestic demand. The dollar's volatility continues to put companies in a bind when calculating costs, and business chambers view the exchange-rate flexibilization adopted as insufficient. The digital economy is growing, driven precisely by the need to access dollars, and cryptocurrencies are gaining ground as a real financial alternative —symptomatic of an informal dollarization that the formal banking system has yet to reabsorb. The fuel crisis and the reduction in flights, in turn, are making travel more expensive and complicated, with Uyuni tourism reporting a reduction of up to half its transport capacity.

On the institutional front, the government swore in Christian Morales Burgos as new Minister of Economy and Public Finance following the removal of José Gabriel Espinoza, who was censured by the Legislative Assembly and ultimately withdrew the injunction with which he had tried to reverse his ouster. Morales takes office with a mandate to consolidate macroeconomic stability and accelerate reforms, in a context where Bolivia has just issued one billion dollars in sovereign bonds with demand five times higher than anticipated —a sign that international markets are willing to bet on the country if the reform agenda holds.

What to watch closely in the coming hours is the organized social response: if the COB plenary escalates into mobilizations and if the coca growers make good on blockades, the government will face the acid test of its capacity to sustain the adjustment without yielding to street pressure. Zamora's meeting with truckers in Santa Cruz and the continuation of the dialogue at YPFB will be the first barometer. On the fiscal side, the first quarterly IMF disbursement and the trajectory of international reserves will determine whether macroeconomic stabilization gains enough traction to offset the inevitable inflationary uptick that the government itself has already acknowledged as a consequence of the diesel price hike.
