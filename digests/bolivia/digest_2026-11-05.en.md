# Bolivia: The Economic Pulse — 2026-11-05

*Analysis generated with AI | 2026-11-05*

The 2027 General State Budget that Rodrigo Paz's government presented this week contains an admission that no Bolivian administration has ever formalized so bluntly in an official document: the economy will contract 1.5% next year, the official dollar is projected at Bs 12, and the fuel subsidy will remain in place despite the agreement signed with the International Monetary Fund. That tension — between the commitments undertaken before the IMF and the political realities that prevent their fulfillment — defines Bolivia's economic moment more sharply than any isolated data point.

The magnitude of the deterioration is already difficult to disguise. According to data compiled by El Deber, the Bolivian economy fell 3.27% in the first six months of the year, with nine of eleven sectors contracting during the second quarter. The Central Bank projects a 3.6% drop for all of 2026, a figure that converges with IMF estimates placing the contraction at around 3%, and with those of the World Bank, which forecasts a 3.2% collapse this year before a 4% rebound in 2027. ECLAC, for its part, has classified Bolivia as the slowest-growing economy in all of South America. Cochabamba, the country's third economic engine according to the Atlas Económico, projects its own 4.15% decline with barely 14.6% formal employment among its working-age population.

What makes the picture uniquely complicated is not merely the recession itself, but the combination of factors feeding it simultaneously. Natural gas, the backbone of the Bolivian fiscal model for two decades, is in terminal decline: a vice minister publicly acknowledged that, according to forecasts, the country has barely two years of exportable production left, while YPFB negotiates regional gas integration with Argentina and Brazil in what is, in practice, preparation for imports. Imported gas would cost nearly triple what industry pays today under the subsidized price, according to experts consulted by El Deber, and the delay in expanding the Argentine pipeline threatens to postpone even that emergency exit. President Paz tried to frame this transformation in voluntarist terms — "we had gas, we're going to have gas again" — but the pivot from net exporter to net importer represents an unprecedented structural break in the country's contemporary economic history.

Against that backdrop, the 2027 budget looks internally contradictory. The government has budgeted Bs 11 billion for fuel subsidies, maintaining a policy that the IMF agreement itself required revising. Economy Minister Christian Morales — recently appointed following the parliamentary censure of his predecessor José Gabriel Espinoza and a brief interim period under Óscar Mario Justiniano — tried to justify the omission of the fiscal distribution formula known as "50-50," without offering a response that dispelled doubts about the program's coherence. Regional governors, for their part, are demanding a fiscal pact that grants them greater autonomy and investment, rejecting the government's proposed 1% budget revenue-sharing arrangement, which according to official calculations would amount to Bs 550 million split among nine departments.

The institutional turbulence surrounding the ministerial change is not a minor detail. The Legislative Assembly censured Espinoza, the vice president filed a criminal complaint against him, and President Paz himself admitted to Los Tiempos that he inherited a "devastated" economy that makes him "ashamed." In that context, Morales's appointment with the mandate to "consolidate stability and accelerate transformations" — according to the official communiqué from the Ministry of Economy and Public Finance — sounds more like an imperative than a description of a reality already underway.

There are, nonetheless, signals the government uses to argue that the adjustment is in motion. Country risk has dropped to 364 basis points, below Argentina and Ecuador according to the Ministry. Bolivia posted a fiscal surplus in the first five months of 2026. The Central Bank advanced the return of USD 333 million to savers, CAF sealed a strategic alliance worth USD 3.1 billion, and the World Bank approved an additional USD 200 million for social protection. Gold exports grew 171.5% — driven in part by a decree requiring 80% of generated foreign currency to enter the financial system — though El Deber emphasizes that Bolivia is selling more minerals while its economy remains in recession, illustrating a structural paradox: mining is growing but failing to drag the rest of the sectors along with it.

The imminent closure of between eight and ten state-owned enterprises, announced by Minister Morales for a "very short" timeframe, and the central-government spending cuts anticipated at around Bs 8 billion, suggest the fiscal adjustment is real. But the shutdown of Manaco — the historic shoe factory that operated for 86 years and accumulated losses of Bs 130 million, leaving 600 workers in a state of emergency — illustrates the human and symbolic cost of that state divestment process. Private enterprise shows no signs of being ready to absorb that vacuum: the Cainco business chamber has put ten urgent measures on the table and is announcing a forum to debate how to "rebuild without rehearsing," while Cochabamba entrepreneurs warn that the local economy is registering "elevated stress" and the informal sector continues to expand.

What bears watching in the coming weeks is, first, whether the new mining code — which eases contracts but excludes tax reform — manages to attract foreign investment at the speed the government needs to offset the gas decline; second, whether the IMF agreement survives the budget as presented or whether Morales will have to renegotiate conditions before year-end; and third, whether social blockades — which have already caused gas supply interruptions in La Paz and El Alto — resurface with enough force to erode the fragile fiscal stability the government is trying to sell to international markets from Madrid, where it presented its Investment Law bill to potential creditors at "Bolivia Day."
