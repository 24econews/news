The Legislative Assembly's censure of Economy Minister José Gabriel Espinoza and his immediate replacement by Christian Morales Burgos have captured the attention of markets and analysts today — not so much for the reshuffle itself, but for what it reveals about the political fragility surrounding the most ambitious economic adjustment Bolivia has attempted in decades.

President Rodrigo Paz, who took office pledging to correct the imbalances inherited from the Luis Arce administration, now faces a brutal paradox: the reforms demanded by the International Monetary Fund to keep the financing program active — and which the government itself has explicitly linked to Supreme Decree 5676, as El Deber revealed — are generating political and social resistance that threatens to derail them before they can produce results. The government publicly acknowledged that the decree adjusting diesel prices is an IMF condition, which turns any retreat into a devastating signal for international creditors and for the credibility of the stabilization program.

Pressures are piling up on multiple fronts. The Santa Cruz agricultural sector has issued an ultimatum to the government to repeal the decree before Monday, threatening to convene an Asamblea de la Cruceñidad, the most powerful civic pressure mechanism in eastern Bolivia. The Cámara Agropecuaria del Oriente flatly rejects the adjustment, while the president of Cainco warns that the existence of two diesel prices — the previously subsidized one and the new Bs 18 rate — inevitably fuels the black market, an argument shared by industrialists and economists alike. The road blockades, which according to FEPC have already surpassed in cumulative damage all of 2025, are pushing food prices higher, paralyzing transport and adding inflationary pressure at a moment when Bolivia expects to close 2026 with inflation of up to 17 percent.

Confronted with that storm, the government opted for a signal of continuity dressed up as change. Paz first named Óscar Mario Justiniano as interim minister and then swore in Christian Morales Burgos — until then Vice Minister of the Treasury and a technocratic figure — with explicit instructions to continue negotiations with the IMF and the World Bank and to "change course" on the economy, a formulation that sounds contradictory but reflects the need to preserve the external financial anchor while trying to reduce the domestic political cost. Morales announced he will evaluate adjustments to the diesel decree, though he ruled out its repeal, suggesting the government will attempt a middle-ground solution — perhaps differentiating prices by sector or setting up compensation mechanisms — to relieve pressure without breaching the fund's conditions.
