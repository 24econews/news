The closure of Manaco — one of Bolivia's most recognized footwear brands, with 600 employees declared in emergency after accumulating losses of 130 million bolivianos — crystallizes with cruel precision everything the macroeconomic data has been describing in the abstract for months: an economy contracting in nine of eleven productive sectors, which the Central Bank projects will fall 3.6% this year, and which ECLAC already identifies as the slowest-growing economy in all of South America in 2025. Manaco's bankruptcy is not an isolated industrial accident; it is the human face of a recession that President Rodrigo Paz himself has described as a "ravaged" economy, one he says makes him feel "ashamed."

What makes the current moment especially tense is not just the depth of the contraction, but the distance between diagnosis and action. The 2027 General State Budget, presented by the government in recent weeks, projects an additional 1.5% GDP contraction next year, sets the official exchange rate at 12 bolivianos per dollar — when the market rate is already hovering around that level — and maintains fuel subsidies worth 11 billion bolivianos, according to Los Tiempos. This last decision is the most problematic in terms of external credibility: Bolivia signed an agreement with the IMF that contemplated the gradual elimination of subsidies starting in 2027, and the budget now presented casts doubt on that commitment, though the government is careful not to guarantee the continuity of the subsidy throughout the entire term. Economy Minister Christian Morales — recently appointed by Paz following the legislative censure of his predecessor José Gabriel Espinoza — inherited this Gordian knot as the central mission of his tenure.

The tension between IMF requirements and domestic politics is the axis around which all Bolivian economic discussion revolves. The Fund projects a 3% contraction for 2025, while the World Bank anticipates a 3.2% drop in 2026 before a 4% rebound in 2027 that many analysts consider optimistic. Bolivia has fallen 37 places in the global economic freedom index according to El Deber, and Fitch has expressed doubts about the sustainability of international reserve accumulation. The country that once exported natural gas to Brazil and Argentina is now preparing to import it, and experts warn that imported gas will cost nearly triple for industry. The government authorized by decree the importation of gas for its own consumption and electricity generation, while residents of La Paz and El Alto block roads denouncing irregular fuel distribution. The decline in gas production not only threatens export revenues but domestic electricity generation, putting at risk the viability of entire industrial sectors.
