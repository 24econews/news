# Bolivia: The Economic Pulse — 2026-11-12

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-11-12*

The first anniversary of Rodrigo Paz's government could not have arrived at a more awkward moment: a flurry of data, projections, and multilateral institutions have converged this week to paint the bleakest picture of the Bolivian economy in decades, and the executive's response — dismissal of a minister, a declared change of course, a diplomatic offensive in Europe — suggests that the administration itself recognizes the magnitude of the problem, even if it is still debating how to confront it.

The most revealing figure comes from El Deber: the Bolivian economy contracted 1.63% in 2025 and grew by a nearly imperceptible 0.03% in the final quarter. The Central Bank projects a 3.6% drop for the current year, while the IMF — whose publication on Bolivia the previous government reportedly tried to block, according to local media — estimates a contraction of between 3% and 3.3%, the only one in all of South America. The World Bank goes even further: it anticipates a 3.2% decline in 2026, though it envisions a 4% rebound in 2027 if reforms move forward. Cochabamba, the country's third most populous department, is projecting its own 4.15% contraction, with formal employment barely reaching 14.6% of its working-age population. ECLAC confirms what the numbers already hint at: Bolivia will be South America's slowest-growing economy in 2025.

It is against this backdrop that President Paz publicly acknowledged having inherited a "devastated" economy — his own words, as reported by Los Tiempos — and that he is "ashamed" of the state of public finances. The phrasing carries political weight, but it is also a management signal: Paz dismissed his Economy Minister Gabriel Espinoza, whose censure had been approved by the Legislative Assembly and who was also facing a criminal complaint from the vice president. In his place, Christian Morales Burgos took office with an explicit mandate to "change course" and "consolidate stability." The ministerial reshuffle — the second at Economy in the government's first year — adds uncertainty to an environment already suffering from dollar shortages, an exchange-rate gap, and private-sector distrust.

On the exchange rate, the new minister's signal is one of openness: the Ministry of Economy has declared a return to "a single, floating exchange rate, accessible to all," acknowledging that coexisting with two, three, or four exchange rates is unsustainable. The Central Bank also reinstated dollar deposits of up to USD 50,000 and announced that, starting July 15, it will begin returning foreign-currency savings on a one-year timeline. The minister expects the dollar to stabilize below 11 bolivianos in the coming days, although the 2027 budget already contemplates an exchange rate of 12 bolivianos — an implicit recognition of further depreciation — maintains the fuel subsidy, and projects another economic contraction of 1.5%.

External financing is, for now, the main cushion sustaining the stabilization narrative. The IDB approved USD 750 million to support the process, the World Bank contributed an additional USD 200 million for social protection, CAF sealed a strategic alliance worth USD 3.1 billion, and President Paz announced EUR 250 million in German financing from Madrid. In the Spanish capital, Bolivia also presented its draft Investment Law and previewed reforms in mining and energy. Paz's meeting with the chairman of Repsol — under the banner "Energy to rebuild Bolivia" — is the most concrete sign that gas, a sector in sustained decline, remains the centerpiece of the reindustrialization strategy. Country risk stands at 364 basis points, below Argentina and Ecuador according to the Ministry of Economy, although Moody's rating remains at Caa2, firmly in high-risk speculative territory.

The private sector is in no mood to wait. The Santa Cruz Chamber of Industry, Commerce, Services and Tourism (Cainco) put ten decisions on the table to confront the crisis, insisting that attracting investment requires predictable legal frameworks and legal certainty. Cochabamba business leaders, for their part, are calling for immediate reactivation measures. Harvard Growth Lab, in an analysis cited by El Deber, proposes a "structural pivot" that goes well beyond the fiscal adjustments currently underway. Bolivia fell 37 spots in the global economic freedom index and now ranks 147th, a deterioration analysts link to the weight of the state in the economy — which controls 38% of GDP as a result of nationalizations — and to the growing impact of the illicit economy. The closure of Manaco, after 86 years of shoe production, was the week's most eloquent industrial symbol: high prices, plunging sales, an industry in retreat.

Underlying fiscal tensions complicate any short-term optimism. Departmental governors are demanding a fiscal pact, greater investment, and autonomy. The presidential spokesperson suggested that with an additional 1% in revenue-sharing, Santa Cruz could eliminate its deficit — a statement that reignited the debate over resource distribution. Meanwhile, the new mining code, which loosens contract rules but excludes tax reform, faces opposition from mining cooperatives, and state-owned mining is dragging multimillion-dollar losses across four operations. Mining royalties, on the other hand, rose 103% in the administration's first year, largely driven by gold: La Paz registered USD 1.616 billion in gold output and sales in 2026.

What to watch closely in the coming weeks: how quickly new minister Morales Burgos finalizes a technical agreement with the IMF — whose formal backing of the Bolivian economic program has already been announced by the Ministry of Economy — the effective implementation of the dollar deposit returns starting July 15, the legislative progress of the tax-relief bill, and whether the meeting with Repsol translates into concrete investment commitments in gas. With the World Bank projecting a 4% rebound in 2027, the window of opportunity exists, but it hinges on reforms that Bolivia has spent years promising and postponing.
