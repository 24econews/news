# Bolivia: The Economic Pulse — 2026-08-21

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-08-21*

The parliamentary censure of Economy Minister José Gabriel Espinoza by the Plurinational Legislative Assembly — and the additional criminal complaint filed by the Vice President — is not merely a political skirmish: it is the crystallization of a tension running through Bolivia's entire economic agenda at this moment, pitting a government that has begun substantive reforms against a coalition of interests that would rather not pay their cost.

The Assembly approved the censure of Espinoza after rejecting his justification for missing an interpellation — the minister cited a trip to Santa Cruz and Brazil — and the Vice President went further still by filing a criminal complaint against him. Parliament is demanding that Espinoza answer 29 questions organized into four thematic blocks, turning the interpellation into a political trial by other means. The paradox is striking: the very minister that Bloomberg Línea recently interviewed to state that Bolivia's economy will see positive growth in 2026, and that country risk has fallen from 2,000 to 407 basis points, now faces the risk of being ousted by the same institution that should be ratifying the reforms behind that improvement.

That country risk figure, which according to El Deber currently stands at around 407 basis points and places Bolivia below Argentina and Ecuador per eju.tv, is perhaps the most eloquent indicator of what is at stake. The decline has been sharp from the crisis-era highs, and reflects the first tangible effects of an adjustment that includes the liberalization of the exchange rate — the boliviano is now trading at Bs 11.52 per dollar for the third consecutive day, according to El Deber — the return of dollar deposits to the financial system, the normalization of remittances, a $1 billion sovereign bond issuance, and the approval of a $1.9 billion IMF credit. The Ministry of Economy and Public Finance also announced that Bolivia reversed the fiscal trend and posted a surplus in the first five months of 2026, a signal that contrasts with years of sustained structural deficits under the MAS administration.

The epicenter of social discontent is diesel. Supreme Decree 5676 eliminated the subsidy for large consumers and set a price of Bs 18 per liter — against the previously subsidized level — with a quote recalculated daily. The measure has unleashed a chain reaction: the Cámara Agropecuaria del Oriente declared a state of emergency, Fencomin demands the decree's repeal, transporters and miners reject the adjustment, and the construction sector warns that the increase opens a hole in existing contracts and threatens to paralyze works. Agricultural producers are proposing alternatives such as mobile fuel dispensers with digital systems to deliver fuel directly to the field, while the Departmental Government and civic groups seek regional solutions. The CAO was explicit in its rejection, and business leaders grouped in Cainco demanded accelerated solutions to avert a larger crisis.

The Government maintains that the diesel hike "should not affect" the household basket and that the measure is a long-postponed price "reckoning." YPFB explained technically why the subsidy was unsustainable, and Fundación Jubileo justified it as fiscally necessary. Industrialists, in a significant pivot, backed the removal of the subsidy and asked that it not return, arguing that the blockades — which have accumulated $2.7 billion in losses according to Los Tiempos and 14 dead over 50 days of roadblocks — are more destructive than any price adjustment. The Government activated $24.6 million to support 17,500 producer families and announced funds to rebuild the working capital of small producers hit by the blockades.

In parallel to these fronts, the legislative debate has grown denser. Three bills seek to place a constitutional reform focused on economics and justice on the agenda, and four proposals are competing to define the new Investment Law, which the Government presents as an umbrella for long-term structural reforms. Minister Justiniano proposed a "Productive Triangle" to reactivate key sectors. The Banco Central de Bolivia, however, is projecting a GDP contraction of 3.6% for 2026 according to Los Tiempos — a figure that contrasts with official optimism — while the IMF estimates growth below 2.3% for next year and Fundación Jubileo maintains that the economy has been shrinking for three years. Inflation, according to FEPC, fell to 2.79% nationally, although Bolivia expects to close 2026 with inflation of up to 17% per eju.tv, suggesting that the adjustment process still has a long way to run.

What defines this week, and what markets and investors should watch closely, is whether the Paz government manages to keep Espinoza in his post, because his departure would destabilize the reform signal that has begun to be priced in by rating agencies and international markets. S&P has upgraded Bolivia's rating, and the IDB has committed up to €4.1 billion for the reactivation. CAF and Bolivia sealed a $3.1 billion strategic alliance. The question concentrating the political and economic risk of the coming weeks is whether the Legislature — dominated by forces that do not share the adjustment agenda — will allow those reforms to take hold, or whether the censure of the minister becomes the first step of a rollback that markets have already begun to discount.
