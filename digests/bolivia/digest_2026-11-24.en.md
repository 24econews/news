# Bolivia: The Economic Pulse — 2026-11-24

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-11-24*

Bolivia's 2027 state budget, unveiled this week, projects an economic contraction of 1.5% — an extraordinary admission of weakness that flatly contradicts the official stabilization narrative that President Rodrigo Paz's government has sustained throughout its first year in office. It is the first time a Bolivian budget explicitly incorporates a GDP decline as its baseline scenario, and the figure crystallizes with brutal clarity the real state of an economy caught between mutually cancelling forces.

The International Monetary Fund goes even further: it projects a 3% contraction for the current year, according to Los Tiempos. The contrast with the indicators the Ministry of Economy and Public Finance has highlighted in its first-year assessment is striking. The portfolio, now under Christian Morales Burgos — appointed just over a month ago following the parliamentary censure and subsequent removal of Gabriel Espinoza — has underscored the reduction of inflation to 7.08%, the recovery of international reserves, the achievement of a fiscal surplus in the first five months of 2026, and the improvement in Moody's sovereign rating, which lifted Bolivia to Caa2 with a positive outlook. Country risk remains below that of Argentina and Ecuador, according to the ministry itself. Deals have also been closed for USD 3.1 billion with CAF, a USD 1.9 billion IMF loan, and World Bank approval of another USD 500 million to support structural adjustment, alongside an international markets issuance of USD 1 billion that drew demand five times greater than supply. Bolivia, in other words, has regained access to external financing. But that access has not yet translated into growth.

The sector that best illustrates the gap between the recovery narrative and productive reality is hydrocarbons. According to data compiled by El Deber, hydrocarbons registered a 13.4% drop in activity — the worst sectoral performance in the country and the clearest signal that the historic engine of the Bolivian economic model is faltering. The Ministry of Hydrocarbons anticipates the imminent start of operations at Domo Oso-X3, an exploratory bet the government presents as a lever for reactivation, but which faces active opposition from environmental groups weighing recourse to international bodies to halt it, according to Opinión Bolivia. In parallel, the government has filed criminal complaints against former YPFB executives over anomalies in the importation of crude that allegedly failed to meet quality standards — an accusation that, according to Los Tiempos and El Deber, points at the state oil company's former president and adds another front of institutional turbulence at a key enterprise that also recently admitted its IT systems had been breached.

The economic contradiction flagged by several analysts cited in the Bolivian press is that of an economy trying to press the accelerator on public spending and investment while simultaneously applying austerity brakes. The Ministry of Economy has instructed all public institutions to implement savings policies, while the executive issues reactivation decrees that several sectors reject. The Chamber of Industry and Commerce (Cainco) and the Eastern Agricultural Chamber (CAO) have demanded accelerated solutions to what they describe as five factors aggravating the crisis. Business leaders warn that the combination of low output and residual inflationary pressure is pushing the country toward a stagflation scenario, El Deber reports. Debate over a constitutional reform to enable President Paz's economic agenda has gained momentum in recent days, with a first constitutional adjustment agreement beginning to take shape in the Legislative Assembly.

The drought hitting the altiplano is adding pressure from the rural sector: families have begun selling off livestock and are losing crops amid water shortages, threatening the food supply chain. ANAPO, the oilseed producers' association, is in turn demanding that state-owned Emapa prioritize purchasing Bolivian wheat to supply bakers with flour rather than resorting to imports. The debate over fuel subsidies, particularly gasoline and LPG, remains open and politically explosive: the Minister of Hydrocarbons confirmed that the gasoline subsidy will be lifted but refused to set a date, while Jorge Quiroga warned that the government's mixed signals would unleash a wave of speculation, and peasant organizations have announced they will not permit an end to subsidies. Civic groups have called mass mobilizations in protest of the crisis and fuel shortages.

On the bilateral trade front, the Chile-Bolivia Binational Business Council this week proposed modernizing the Economic Complementation Agreement with Santiago, an initiative that takes on added geopolitical weight given Bolivia's lack of sea access. The exchange rate, now under a flexible regime after the abandonment of fifteen years of fixed parity, is trading around ten bolivianos per dollar, with the 2027 budget projecting a reference of Bs 12. The government maintains that 99.3% of credit in the financial system is denominated in bolivianos and that households will not be materially affected by currency fluctuations.

What will bear close watching in the coming weeks is whether or not operations begin at Domo Oso-X3 — and the environmentalists' legal response — the outcome of the fuel subsidy debate, the speed at which the external financing secured translates into real activity, and whether the constitutional reform underway advances far enough to clear the legal framework the government deems necessary to attract private investment at scale. With the IMF projecting a contraction three times deeper than that foreseen in the official budget, the distance between positive financial signals and the real economy is the principal risk Bolivia must manage before the year closes.
