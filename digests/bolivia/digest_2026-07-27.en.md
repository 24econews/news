# Bolivia: The Economic Pulse — 2026-07-27

The flexible dollar hit Bs 11 this week, a psychological threshold that now commands the full attention of Bolivia's economy and lays bare, with unusual clarity, the gap between official rhetoric and market reality. While Economy Minister José Gabriel Espinoza insists that the exchange rate will stabilize "in the coming days" below that level and that the banking system holds sufficient foreign currency, business chambers report that suppliers are already refusing the boliviano as a means of payment, and analysts are debating whether the real ceiling will be Bs 12, as businessman Samuel Doria Medina has suggested, or whether pressure could push it higher. Monday's session opened at Bs 9.73 for the official reference rate, but the parallel market and the so-called "flexible" dollar traded on divergent paths, making it clear that the currency unification promised by President Rodrigo Paz's government—after abandoning fifteen years of a fixed peg—has failed, at least so far, to anchor expectations.

The tension is structural, not cyclical. Bolivia's external debt hit an all-time record of $14.358 billion in June, according to Banco Central de Bolivia figures cited by El Deber and Los Tiempos, and the country is now sending abroad more resources than it receives in net disbursements—a dynamic known in international finance as negative resource transfer. Fitch Ratings maintains its "negative" outlook on the Bolivian economy, a drag that will raise the cost of any future refinancing and narrows the government's room to attract private capital. On top of that comes a projected contraction of between 0.7% and 1% for the current year, driven in part by the fallout from half a century of road blockades that, according to data compiled by Los Tiempos, left at least 14 dead and economic losses estimated at $2.7 billion. Cochabamba, the country's third most important department, projects a regional economic contraction of 4.15% and a formal employment rate that will barely reach 14.6% of its working-age population.

The diesel crisis adds a layer of immediate urgency to that medium-term deterioration. Fuel shortages have paralyzed 80% of interdepartmental bus services, threaten 800,000 tons of soybeans in Santa Cruz—the backbone of the country's agro-industrial exports—and are chaining poultry producers, dairy farmers, banana growers and manufacturers to a spiral of rising costs. Yacimientos Petrolíferos Fiscales Bolivianos began unloading 48 million liters of imported diesel at the Chilean port of Arica, but the government acknowledged that operational difficulties at that terminal partly explain the delay. The irony has not gone unnoticed: Bolivia, which for years portrayed itself as a net hydrocarbons exporter, now depends on emergency imports through a foreign port to keep its domestic economy moving. The prospect of importing gas at international prices—ten times higher than the subsidized domestic price, according to estimates compiled by Los Tiempos—starkly illustrates the long-term cost of the exhaustion of the extractivist model that underpinned the boom of the last decade.

The International Monetary Fund mission resumed its meetings with private-sector executives this week in parallel with its dialogue with the government, a signal that Washington wants to gauge the temperature of the productive sector before closing any formal agreement with La Paz. President Paz frames that IMF deal as a lever to draw "clear signals" of investment, and the government confirmed to El Deber that an in-principle understanding with the multilateral is already in place. Even so, the business community is pressing for more forceful measures: the Cámara de Industrias is proposing a recovery fund and a family stipend, the Instituto Boliviano de Comercio Exterior is putting forward five specific measures to reverse the foreign-currency shortage, and business chambers from across the country held an extraordinary summit in Cochabamba to build a common national agenda ahead of the next administration. The presidential election, whose proximity has investment sitting on the sidelines, turns economic management into the central argument of the political race: voters, according to polls compiled by El Deber, are judging candidates above all on their ability to run the economy.

Against that backdrop of urgency, the Banco Central de Bolivia approved rules allowing banks to use cryptocurrencies to cope with the shortage of physical dollars—a pivot that would have been unthinkable two years ago and that reveals just how much the coordinates of Bolivia's financial system have shifted. Banco Bisa announced the opening of an office in Panama, cementing its regional expansion. The government launched the second phase of returning dollar deposits starting July 15, on a one-year schedule, and enabled withdrawals of up to $3,000 from the financial system, while also freeing up the use of cards for international purchases at the reference exchange rate. These are necessary steps, but 90% of micro and small businesses are already reporting closures, and informal employment rose by a full percentage point in the first half alone, according to the Instituto Nacional de Estadística.

What to watch closely in the coming weeks is, first, the speed at which the flexible exchange rate converges—or fails to converge—with the level promised by the government; and second, the content and terms of the IMF deal, whose formal announcement could act as a catalyst or, if the conditionalities prove tougher than expected, as a trigger for renewed social unrest. President Paz's meeting with the business community, the trajectory of diesel supply and the Senate debate on the reformulated General State Budget—where Doria Medina is proposing to incorporate the 50/50 revenue-sharing mechanism—will round out the picture of an economy going through, in the words of Bolivian economists themselves, the most severe crisis in the last 67 years.
