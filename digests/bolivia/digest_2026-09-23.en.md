# Bolivia: The Economic Pulse — 2026-09-23

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-09-23*

The full elimination of the diesel subsidy, which took effect with the price set at Bs 17.95 per liter — up from subsidized levels that represented barely a fraction of that cost — stands as the most disruptive economic event Bolivia has faced so far this year, and its knock-on effects are simultaneously reshaping public finances, the cost structure of the productive sector, and the daily lives of millions of Bolivians.

The immediate impact was visible in basic consumer markets. According to Los Tiempos, prices for chicken, vegetables, and interdepartmental bus fares climbed almost instantaneously, with intercity transport rates rising as much as 125% on some corridors. The Confederación de Choferes de Bolivia announced a fleet stand-down for Monday and is demanding the repeal of Decree 5716, while transporters reject the government's plan to convert to Compressed Natural Gas, arguing that the necessary conversion kits are simply not available. The spokesperson for the Cochabamba Governor's Office, Sergio de la Zerda, was more blunt: Sedcam — the departmental road service — is "practically paralyzed" due to a lack of diesel and the jump in operating costs. In Uyuni, the epicenter of salt-flat tourism, tourist transport capacity has been cut in half, according to El Deber.

The government of President Rodrigo Paz defends the measure with figures that partially contradict the sectoral panic. Outgoing Economy Minister José Gabriel Espinoza — dismissed after being censured by the Legislative Assembly in a politically turbulent week — maintained until the end that the subsidy was "an expression of corruption." His interim successor, Óscar Mario Justiniano, was in turn replaced by Christian Morales Burgos, who was sworn in with the explicit mandate to "consolidate stability and accelerate the transformations." Morales takes office at a moment when the Ministry of Economy has detected slow execution of Bs 1 billion earmarked for fuel, deepening the contradiction between the declared fiscal adjustment and the state's actual operational capacity.

The fiscal dimension of the reform is undeniable. The government said Bolivia has reversed its deficit trajectory and posted a surplus in the first five months of 2026, with central-level spending cuts of roughly Bs 8 billion. The diesel subsidy also left behind $1.5 billion in debt to suppliers, according to data cited by a senator and reported by La Patria Bolivia. The Ministry of Economy acknowledged there will be inflationary pressure but insisted that offsetting measures — including the PEPE II cash transfer, which will reach between 2 and 2.5 million people — will cushion the impact on the most vulnerable households.

The financial anchor behind these reforms is the $1.9 billion loan approved by the IMF and ratified by the Legislative Assembly with more than a two-thirds majority — legislative backing that reflects a cross-cutting consensus on the inevitability of adjustment, if not on its pace or its beneficiaries. The Inter-American Development Bank will provide additional financing equivalent to 10.5 months of Renta Dignidad payments. The dollar exchange rate, which the Central Bank set at Bs 11.90 for Tuesday according to Los Tiempos, continued to climb in the parallel market, reaching Bs 11 in some venues according to El Deber — volatility that businesses have described as unsustainable for calculating costs and prices. The digital economy, in that context, has grown precisely on the back of the need to access foreign currency, El Deber noted.

Productive sectors are responding with differentiated levels of alarm. Soy producers warn that the new diesel price significantly raises the cost of agricultural production at a time when Cochabamba — the country's third-largest economy according to the Atlas Económico — is registering a 4.15% contraction and an unemployment rate of one in ten inhabitants. The hydrocarbons sector, which had already accumulated a 13.4% decline and which the Cámara Boliviana de Hidrocarburos y Energía describes as being in "intensive care," now faces the paradox that eliminating the subsidy reduces consumption incentives in a market where domestic production is already insufficient. Bank executives and the former Economy Minister under the Arce administration will be summoned in connection with the Central Bank case, adding a judicial dimension to the institutional crisis.

Industrialists, by contrast, oppose the return of the subsidy and are asking the government to design an emergency plan to sustain economic activity without recreating past distortions. Cainco announced a forum to debate how to "rebuild without experimenting" on the Bolivian economy — a phrase that captures the country's structural dilemma: the reforms are acknowledged as necessary even by their fiercest critics, but consensus on the path forward remains fragile.

What to watch in the coming days is whether Monday's transporter stand-down escalates into a nationwide logistical paralysis, whether new Minister Morales can stabilize exchange-rate expectations before the parallel dollar widens its gap with the official rate, and whether the government upholds its commitment not to extend subsidy elimination to gasoline — a possibility the Ministry of Economy itself has not explicitly ruled out — a decision that could trigger a second round of social tension in a country still digesting the diesel shock.
