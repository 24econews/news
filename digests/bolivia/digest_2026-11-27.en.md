# Bolivia: The Economic Pulse — 2026-11-27

Bolivia is navigating a collision between two opposing forces that rarely converge with such clarity: while the International Monetary Fund validates the country's economic course and opens the door to a second disbursement of $212 million, the transport sector has declared an indefinite strike starting next Monday in protest against the rising cost of diesel, a measure that threatens to paralyze the logistics chain of an economy already subject to extraordinary fiscal pressures.

The staff-level agreement with the IMF, confirmed this Friday, represents the most significant external endorsement for the Bolivian government so far this year. The organization highlighted the reduction in inflation, the strengthening of international reserves, and what it characterized as an orderly transition toward a more flexible exchange rate regime. Having passed the first program review, Bolivia will access a second tranche of approximately $212 million, as confirmed by both the Ministry of Economy and Public Finance and the Banco Central de Bolivia. The signal arrives at a moment when the government is simultaneously negotiating up to $1.25 billion in additional funding with the Inter-American Development Bank and the World Bank, resources aimed at covering outstanding obligations and preventing a halt in public infrastructure projects. The combination of both efforts outlines a state that has regained access to multilateral financing but remains dependent on it to sustain its ordinary functioning.

The paradox is that precisely the reform the IMF implicitly celebrates — the dismantling of the fuel subsidy system — is the one that has triggered the most immediate political crisis. The price of diesel, now set at 20.57 bolivianos per liter, has made Bolivia the country with the most expensive diesel in the region, according to the truckers themselves, who also question its quality. Drivers are demanding the cancellation of the price hike as a precondition for even sitting down at a negotiating table. The government, for its part, has categorically ruled out returning to the subsidy and is calling for talks without yielding on the price. The Cámara de Exportadores de Santa Cruz (Cadex) rejected the strike and proposed alternative formulas to lower the cost of fuel without reinstating the subsidy, while the Cámara de Industria, Comercio, Servicios y Turismo de Santa Cruz (Cainco) called on the government for mitigation measures and a reduction in state bureaucracy. Regional governments and municipalities have warned that the price hike compromises road maintenance and the cost of public services, and Santa Cruz will receive 100 million bolivianos for works in the Plan 3.000 and Tahuichi areas, in what appears to be a gesture of fiscal relief amid the tension.

The structural backdrop is more troubling than the immediate situation. Bolivia — which for two decades sold itself to the world as a gas powerhouse and financed a redistributive model on hydrocarbon revenues — could be forced to import natural gas starting in 2029 if declining production and dwindling reserves are not reversed, according to projections released this Friday. The country that dreamed of living off gas now faces the prospect of paying for it. This reality explains why diesel, which Bolivia does not produce or refine in meaningful quantities and must bring in by tanker from abroad, is unavoidably expensive: the absence of domestic refining capacity and dependence on international refined-product markets make it impossible to decouple the internal price from the global price, regardless of whether international crude has fallen. YPFB signaled this Friday a possible reduction in the diesel price starting Monday, linking it to the drop in international quotations, a cue the government could use to lower the political temperature without formally compromising its position of not reinstating the subsidy.

On the judicial front, former Banco Central de Bolivia director Héctor Pino was sent to pretrial detention at the San Pedro prison in connection with the so-called "Bicentennial banknotes case," a proceeding that adds another layer of institutional turbulence over the BCB at a moment when the institution is trying to project credibility before the IMF.

In the coming hours, attention will focus on whether Monday's trucker strike actually materializes with enough force to pressure the government, and on whether the potential downward correction in diesel prices announced by YPFB is enough to defuse the protest before it escalates. Negotiations with the IDB and the World Bank, whose outcome will determine the state's capacity to finance public works in 2027, along with the formalization of the IMF disbursement in December, will be the other markers that external investors will watch closely to gauge the sustainability of Bolivia's adjustment.
