The International Monetary Fund has delivered its verdict on Bolivia with a precision that leaves little room for interpretation: the country will be the only economy in Latin America to contract in 2025, and projections for 2026 and 2027 suggest that distinction could persist. That ruling, published by Infobae citing the IMF report, is the backdrop against which all of today's economic headlines from La Paz, Santa Cruz and Cochabamba are layered, turning every policy move into a race against the clock.

The most significant development of the day is, precisely, the institutional response to that diagnosis. The Ministry of Economy and Public Finance confirmed that Bolivia has reversed its fiscal trajectory and posted a surplus in the first five months of 2026, a figure that Rodrigo Paz's government is using as the central argument in its stabilization narrative. That same administration has just sworn in Christian Morales Burgos as the new head of the economic portfolio, a technocrat who takes office with the explicit mandate to consolidate stability and accelerate structural reforms in a context where every passing week erodes private-sector confidence. According to the MEFP, Morales arrives with the goal of attracting up to ten billion dollars in new investment, a figure private analysts view skeptically as long as the country fails to resolve its structural imbalances.

The axis of that structural tension is the energy sector, and today's headlines confirm it with an urgency that goes well beyond technical debate. Experts cited by El Deber warn that importing natural gas is now inevitable, a statement that would have been unthinkable barely a decade ago, when Bolivia was South America's leading natural gas exporter. Los Tiempos echoes the same thesis: the exits from the gas crisis are few and difficult. YPFB, the state oil company, appears in the press today not for production achievements but for two distinct reasons: first, for having agreed with sugarcane producers to raise the ethanol blend in gasoline to fifteen percent starting in November, a measure that partially reduces dependence on imported fossil fuels; and second, more alarmingly, the government has denounced systematic diversions of LPG cylinders allegedly involving employees of YPFB itself, the regulatory Authority, and distributors. Police are now escorting cylinder deliveries in La Paz. It is a symptom of institutional decay that worsens risk perception for any investor examining the sector.

In parallel, the government is requiring gold exporters to repatriate eighty percent of their foreign currency earnings, according to El Deber. The measure reflects the dollar thirst that characterizes an economy that only months ago abandoned its fixed exchange rate of 6.96 bolivianos per dollar, in place for fifteen years. The Central Bank has already approved the regulation defining the exchange rate under the new flexible regime, and Bolivia has managed to place one billion dollars in sovereign bonds in international markets with demand five times greater than anticipated, suggesting institutional investors see value in the adjustment — even as dollar deposits in the banking system continue to grow in a context where businesses, according to El Deber, still mistrust the direction of the process.

The thirty-seven-position drop in the global economic freedom index, reported by El Deber, quantifies the accumulated damage of years of state intervention. The same source reports that ten public enterprises in crisis could be liquidated by decree, a signal that the Paz government is willing to dismantle parts of the state business apparatus inherited from the MAS era. The government, for its part, is trying to calibrate the pace of adjustment: the economy minister has asked that the situation of small employers be considered before decreeing the annual wage increase, and the executive is aiming to stabilize the economy without subsidies — though followers of Evo Morales have set a seven-day ultimatum for the diesel subsidy to be reinstated, according to Los Tiempos.
