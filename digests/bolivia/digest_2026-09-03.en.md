# Bolivia: The Economic Pulse — 2026-09-03

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-09-03*

The intervention by supreme decree of Yacimientos Petrolíferos Fiscales Bolivianos marks a turning point that no observer of the Bolivian economy can ignore: the state formally acknowledges that the company which for two decades embodied the MAS extractivist model has collapsed under its own weight, and that five ministries—not one—will need to join forces in an attempt to rescue it.

The measure, decreed amid an acute fuel supply crisis, puts to rest any doubt about the true state of YPFB. According to El Deber, the state-owned firm arrives at 2026 with less gas, less production, and a fresh intervention—a trajectory that traces the full arc from the triumphalist nationalization of 2006 to today's decline. The figures from Los Tiempos are stark: fuel production has fallen to levels insufficient to cover domestic demand. The Minister of Hydrocarbons, Marcelo Blanco, not only announced the intervention of the Agencia Nacional de Hidrocarburos amid alleged "improper handling," but revealed something structurally more significant: the government plans to gradually withdraw YPFB from hydrocarbon commercialization and give greater participation to private operators. It is a quiet reformulation of the Bolivian energy model that, if realized, would represent the deepest reversal of hydrocarbon statism since Evo Morales.

The fuel crisis is not merely a logistics problem. It is the most visible symptom of a fiscal and productive deterioration that Fundación Milenio described this week in unequivocal terms: high fiscal deficit, persistent inflation, and rising cost of living. Current state expenditures have risen 43% in ten years while revenues have grown just 28%, according to General State Budget figures cited by Los Tiempos—a structural gap that admits no painless correction. Public enterprises created by the MAS accumulated losses of 4.058 billion bolivianos over 16 years, according to research reported by Los Tiempos, a figure that explains much of the fiscal hole President Luis Arce's government left as inheritance.

The political corollary to this deterioration arrived with unusual speed. The Plurinational Legislative Assembly censured the Minister of Economy, José Gabriel Espinoza, following an interpellation in which he was to answer 29 questions on economic management. The episode—which included the extraordinary request from Espinoza himself to reschedule his appearance owing to a trip to Santa Cruz and Brazil—was followed by his removal and the appointment of Christian Andrés Morales Burgos as the new head of the portfolio. Interim minister Óscar Mario Justiniano, meanwhile, swore in two vice ministers and the directors of ASFI and the Autoridad de Fiscalización. The ministerial reshuffle in economy—the umpteenth in a matter of months—compounds uncertainty over the continuity of the adjustment underway.

That adjustment has chapters still unresolved. Supreme Decree 5676, popularly dubbed the "dieselazo," which sets a price of 18 bolivianos per liter of diesel for the commercial sector, faces mounting rejection. Business leaders in Cochabamba have formally rejected it and given the government a deadline to reverse it, while the president of the Cámara de Industria, Comercio, Servicios y Turismo (CAINCO) warned that the coexistence of two prices for the same fuel—9.8 bolivianos for small producers with access to up to 2,500 liters monthly, and 18 bolivianos for the rest—creates direct incentives for the black market. The government, for its part, reaffirmed that it will not repeal the decree, though it declared itself open to "optimizing" fuel use. In Cuatro Cañadas, the Minister of Defense arrived accompanied by YPFB technicians to explain the decree on the ground and request the lifting of blockades, which threaten to spread.

The Central Bank, meanwhile, is weighing whether to stop buying gold on the domestic market and is considering converting its reserves into dollars—a signal that the policy of accumulating reserves in precious metals, a hallmark of the Arce era, is being reviewed. The BCB, which also faces the summoning of its executives and of Arce's former Economy Minister as part of an investigation, stated that the banking system holds sufficient foreign currency. The government, for its part, has authorized withdrawals of up to $3,000 from the financial system and normalized remittance transfers—signals that point to a gradual loosening of the exchange controls put in place in prior years. The Central Bank approved a regulation to operate under a flexible exchange rate regime, burying the fixed rate of 6.96 bolivianos per dollar that had artificially sustained the economy for years.

With the BCB itself forecasting economic contraction—projecting a 3.6% decline this year—and the business sector warning that Cochabamba's economy will contract 4.15% in 2026, incoming minister Morales Burgos has narrow room to maneuver. What to watch closely in the coming weeks: whether YPFB's withdrawal from commercialization advances with concrete regulations or remains a declaration of intent; whether the Central Bank consolidates the flexible exchange rate without triggering an inflationary spiral that further erodes purchasing power; and whether the government manages to defuse the blockades before the economic cost of paralysis becomes politically untenable. Domestic debt, which surged to $8.5 billion in 2023 according to Los Tiempos, and external debt exceeding $14.3 billion according to sources cited this week, leave little room for additional missteps.
