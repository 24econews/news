# Bolivia: The Economic Pulse — 2026-11-16

The abrupt dismissal of Economy Minister Gabriel Espinoza and his replacement by Christian Morales Burgos, carried out in the middle of a week laden with political scandals, is the event that today sets the tone for every economic discussion in Bolivia — and not because cabinet reshuffles are uncommon in La Paz, but because it comes at the worst possible moment for the country's institutional credibility.

President Rodrigo Paz executed Espinoza's ouster just as the former minister had finished telling Bloomberg Línea that growth would turn positive in 2026, barely days before the International Monetary Fund released its own projection: a 3% contraction for the Bolivian economy this year. That contradiction between official optimism and the external diagnosis was already politically untenable. What finally blew it up was the criminal complaint filed by the vice president against Espinoza himself, an internal rupture that signals deep fractures within the Paz administration. Christian Morales Burgos, until then deputy minister of the Treasury, was sworn in with the official mandate to "consolidate stability and accelerate the country's transformations" — a formulation that, given the circumstances, sounds more like a survival imperative than a governing program.

The new minister inherits a situation that the executive's own data cannot disguise: the Bolivian economy contracted 3.27% through June, with nine of eleven sectors retreating during the second quarter, according to figures compiled by El Deber. International experts consulted by the same outlet describe the country's position as "vulnerable," a term that carries additional weight when read alongside Bolivia's ranking at 147th in the economic freedom index, as reported by Los Tiempos, and the closure of Manaco after 86 years of production and accumulated losses of 130 million bolivianos — a symbol the manufacturing industry will find hard to ignore.

The fuel subsidy debate only adds institutional confusion to the crisis. YPFB president Sebastián Daroca publicly announced that the gasoline subsidy would be lifted on January 12, a statement the government contradicted hours later by asserting that no date has been set. That the chief executive of the state oil company and the presidency do not even share the same timetable for a measure as sensitive as the elimination of fuel subsidies speaks to an economic policy coordination that, right now, simply does not exist. Daroca, for his part, admitted in remarks picked up by El Deber that Bolivia is not currently attractive to foreign hydrocarbon investment — a remarkable acknowledgment from the man heading the company that is supposed to attract it.

The structural backdrop to that admission is devastating. Bolivia has lost 78% of its proven natural gas reserves since 2013, with only 2.3 TCF currently certified, and the country faces the concrete threat of becoming a net importer of a resource that financed its development model for two decades. The sector's fiscal framework — in which the state captures 93% of the rent and private operators retain only 7% — is what Daroca identifies as the main disincentive for foreign investment, a structure that the new Mining Code currently in public consultation does not appear to be resolving with sufficient speed either, given the rejection by mining cooperatives in Potosí and Fencomin's resistance to continuing the process.

Against this backdrop, the Banco Central de Bolivia executed two moves this week that merit separate attention. On one hand, it began returning $438 million to financial institutions, enabling dollar operations without restrictions; on the other, it reinstated deposit insurance up to $50,000, with the stated goal of reactivating savings in foreign currency. The dollar traded at 11.67 bolivianos on the parallel market, which El Deber characterizes as a possible pause in exchange-rate pressure rather than a structural easing. The economy minister, in his first public appearance, confirmed that the banking system has foreign currency available, though the Ueno case — a bank under intervention whose situation has generated seven red flags identified by sector analysts — is a reminder that the soundness of the financial system should not be taken for granted.

The Santa Cruz Chamber of Industry, Commerce, Services and Tourism (Cainco) was blunt in its diagnosis at the CAF forum: without clear investment laws and legal certainty, attracting foreign capital is a pipe dream. Business leaders from Cochabamba, quoted by El Deber, described a stagnant economy, dominated by informality and in urgent need of reform. The construction sector is demanding price readjustments on public works. Santa Cruz, which contributes 40% to the General Treasury but receives zero in revenue-sharing for its governorship, has just forgiven 50% of overdue taxes and 100% of municipal fines — a sign that even the country's most productive jurisdictions are beginning to manage their own fiscal crisis unilaterally.

In the face of this picture, the government has sought to anchor international expectations: the World Bank approved $500 million in support of economic reforms, Germany committed an initial package of €250 million for forest and water conservation, and Bolivia is advancing on the regulation of a carbon bond market that could monetize its forest reserves. The CAF also formalized a $3.1 billion strategic alliance. These are signals that the international financial community has not abandoned Bolivia, but the speed at which domestic confidence is deteriorating — expressed in the firing of a minister, the contradiction over fuel subsidies, the shutdown of historic factories and the free fall in gas reserves — far outpaces the rhythm at which those external resources arrive.

What needs to be watched in the coming weeks is whether the new minister Morales Burgos can establish an economic narrative coherent with the presidency and with YPFB on the timing and mechanism for withdrawing fuel subsidies. That single variable — how and when to withdraw a subsidy that distorts prices, exhausts fiscal reserves and discourages exploration investment — concentrates in itself most of the political and macroeconomic risk Bolivia faces in the near term.
