# Bolivia: The Economic Pulse — 2026-08-31

The removal of José Gabriel Espinoza as Minister of Economy, forced by an unprecedented parliamentary censure motion against Rodrigo Paz's government, turns Bolivia today into a laboratory case study on the limits of reformism in economies under extreme fiscal pressure: the architect of the adjustment was politically sacrificed just as his most controversial reforms were beginning to generate friction in the streets and in the countryside.

The Legislative Assembly approved the censure of Espinoza after he attempted to reschedule his interpellation citing a trip to Santa Cruz and Brazil, a maneuver lawmakers rejected as contempt. The vice president went so far as to file a criminal complaint against him. The dismissal is politically significant because Espinoza was the face of Bolivia's most radical exchange rate reform in decades: the abandonment of the fixed rate of 6.96 bolivianos per dollar—maintained for nearly twenty years—and the transition to a flexible regime, along with the elimination of the diesel subsidy, materialized through Supreme Decree 5676 and a package of five economic reactivation decrees that several sectors rejected from day one.

Rodrigo Paz responded with a bet on continuity that sounds more like a containment maneuver than a conviction. He first designated Óscar Mario Justiniano as interim minister, who immediately proposed a so-called "Productive Triangle" as the axis of reactivation, and then formally swore in Christian Andrés Morales Burgos, until then Deputy Minister of the Treasury. Paz's message was explicit: Morales must continue negotiations with the IMF and the World Bank, sustain the reforms, and unblock the Investment Law, which still has no date for committee consideration. According to multiple outlets, the new minister is committed to greater economic openness and to bringing public finances into order, but he inherits an environment of enormous institutional fragility.

What makes the moment even more volatile is that the diesel crisis has not been resolved, merely contained. Agricultural producers from Santa Cruz lifted a blockade at the San Pablo Bridge after receiving government commitments to guarantee supply, and the government signed an agreement with tanker truck operators to suspend their strike and open a technical roundtable with YPFB on debts and freight rates. But the Eastern Agricultural Chamber rejected the diesel price adjustment and issued an ultimatum to the government until Monday to repeal DS 5676, threatening to convene an Assembly of the Cruceño Identity. Communities such as Yapacaní gave only 48 hours. The government, for its part, has ratified that it will not repeal the decree. It is a collision foretold.

The costs are already tangible. The blockades that preceded this episode generated, according to figures cited by Los Tiempos, more than $2.7 billion in economic losses over 50 days of disruption, with 14 deaths. The outgoing minister reported export losses exceeding $500 million. Food, transportation, and commerce prices took the hit, and business leaders are demanding an emergency plan. The CEPB warns that the economic impact of the crisis will last for years.

Behind the political storm lie structural data points that compound it. The Central Bank projects a GDP contraction of 3.6% this year, while the Jubileo Foundation notes that the Bolivian economy has been shrinking for three years. The boliviano lost 25% of its value against the dollar in just five weeks following the exchange rate liberalization. Current government expenditure grew 43% over ten years while revenues rose just 28%, according to the General Budget. Public enterprises created under the MAS accumulated losses of 4.058 billion bolivianos over 16 years. The informal economy and smuggling—growing at twice the rate of formal GDP and considered legitimate by nearly half of Bolivians—erode the tax base. Bolivia ranks 117th out of 165 countries in economic freedom, among the three worst in South America.

The mining sector offers the only real breathing space: gold and silver are driving an export boom that lifts the value of foreign sales, although investment in the sector is falling steadily, foreshadowing a cycle reversal if international prices soften. Bolivia holds 80% of the critical minerals demanded by the global energy transition, a geopolitical card that has yet to translate into substantial foreign direct investment, in part because the Investment Law remains stuck in the legislature.

What defines the outlook for the coming weeks is a sequence of deadlines stacking up: the Santa Cruz agricultural ultimatum expires on Monday, the technical roundtable between tanker operators and YPFB must produce concrete results, the new minister Morales must present himself to the IMF and the World Bank with credentials of continuity without triggering a social rupture, and the Investment Law needs to advance in committee if the government wants to maintain dialogue with multilateral organizations. Bolivia's country risk has fallen relative to Argentina and Ecuador, according to recent figures from Red Uno, and S&P upgraded its sovereign rating—signals that international markets value the direction of the adjustment. But that external credibility can evaporate quickly if fuel shortages drag on or if a new cycle of blockades paralyzes the productive economy of the east before Espinoza's successor has had time to settle into the job.
