# Bolivia: The Economic Pulse — 2026-11-25

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-11-25*

The hike in diesel prices to Bs 20.57 per liter, effective since Tuesday, November 25, has unleashed a political and economic storm that cuts across every productive sector in the country and puts the credibility of President Rodrigo Paz's new administration to the test, barely a year after taking office.

The increase, which Hydrocarbons Minister Marcelo Blanco described as "normal" and attributed to international market dynamics, marks an inflection point in the management of Bolivia's energy subsidies. President Paz defended the measure by arguing that the price reflects external conditions, while Blanco promised that "the trend is that fuel prices will come down" and asked citizens to trust the government. That appeal to trust, however, collides head-on with the immediate reaction of at least three organized sectors that have already announced blockades and mobilizations. The La Paz Chamber of Transportation did not rule out protest measures, national transport operators called an extended assembly with warnings of "tough" actions, and the National Federation of Mining Cooperatives of Bolivia, Fencomin, declared that "tolerance is over." In northern La Paz, gold producers declared an emergency, pointing out that diesel is a critical input for their operations and that the new price threatens their operational viability.

The tension is not purely political. The hike comes at a moment when the Banco Central de Bolivia, according to its own report released yesterday, reports that inflation has fallen to 7.1% —the figure for the most recent available period— while simultaneously projecting a close to 2026 at around 9.2%, according to data cited by Los Tiempos. Cheaper fuel has historically been one of the key buffers against Bolivian inflationary pressure, so its partial liberalization opens up a contradiction between the goal of fiscal consolidation and the target of price stabilization. The departmental government has already warned that the higher cost of diesel reduces the funds available for road maintenance, adding an infrastructure dimension to the problem.

The central bank also announced its intention to triple dollar reserves and is studying the conversion of part of its gold holdings into foreign currency, a signal that foreign-currency liquidity management remains a central concern. International reserves stood at USD 3.965 billion, according to the same BCB report published on Tuesday. That said, the recent drop in the international price of gold —an asset that accounts for a significant portion of reserve backing— threatens to erode that recovery before it consolidates, as various sources warned in recent days. The institution also stated that the "exchange-rate realignment" implemented after the corralito crisis allowed the economy to stabilize, with the official exchange rate fixed at Bs 11.75 per dollar, although different quotations persist in the parallel market.

On the fiscal front, the government projected —in a communication last week— a reduction of the deficit to 6.7% of GDP in 2027, a target that presumes continuing the dismantling of subsidies of which the diesel hike is the most visible and politically costly chapter. A senator from Tarija noted that the General State Budget includes Bs 11 billion earmarked for subsidies, which illustrates the magnitude of the adjustment still pending. Bolivia also has an external financing package of USD 10 billion announced last week, which should provide room to cushion the adjustment, although its effective disbursement remains uncertain.

In the energy sector, state-owned YPFB is going through complex weeks: the government filed charges against former executives of the company for irregularities in crude oil imports, while Trafigura, the globally present commodities giant, stated that it had delivered the crude at the port of Arica with certification from an international inspector contracted by YPFB itself, in what appears to be the central knot of the judicial controversy. On top of this, YPFB acknowledged that its IT systems had been compromised. In parallel, the Domo Oso-X3 well, operated by Petrobras —whose shares trade on the São Paulo Stock Exchange (B3: PETR4) and the New York Stock Exchange (NYSE: PBR)— could resume exploratory work within three to four months, in a project that has generated support in Tarija and reopened the debate over the country's gas future, although environmental groups have already announced legal actions to block it.

What merits close monitoring in the coming days is whether the blockade threats from transporters and cooperative miners materialize, and to what degree the government is willing to back down or hold the line on the new diesel price. Any retreat would compromise the credibility of the fiscal adjustment before external creditors and international organizations; any rigidity will sharpen inflationary pressure and social discontent. Country risk fell below 500 basis points in recent days —still in high-risk territory— and that marginal improvement could reverse quickly if social conflict escalates before year-end.
