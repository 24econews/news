# Bolivia: The Economic Pulse — 2026-10-20

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-10-20*

Bolivia posted a fiscal surplus in the first five months of 2026, as confirmed today by the Ministry of Economy and Public Finance — a figure that stands in sharp contrast to the International Monetary Fund's projection of a 3% GDP contraction for the year, the only negative forecast in the region, and one that forces the question of whether the ongoing adjustment is enough to contain structural deterioration or merely buying time.

The tension between these two readings is the central data point of the day. On one side, the government of President Rodrigo Paz is accumulating evidence of short-term stabilization: a fiscal surplus, a sovereign bond issuance of one billion dollars with demand five times above supply, a 1.9 billion dollar agreement with the IMF, and a reduction in country risk that, according to the Ministry of Economy, places Bolivia below Argentina and Ecuador. On the other side, structural indicators continue to deteriorate with stubborn consistency. Bolivia fell 37 places in the global economic freedom index, now ranking 147th. Public debt is projected to exceed 100% of GDP over the course of this year, according to El Deber. Ten state-owned enterprises are in the process of possible liquidation by decree. And the state aviation system, Boliviana de Aviación, operates with just ten aircraft while its workers have declared an emergency over a "serious operational and financial crisis," as the government seeks new private operators to sustain the country's air connectivity.

The IMF agreement is the anchor sustaining the entire stabilization narrative of the Paz government. The first disbursement has already been made, and Bolivia is undergoing its first compliance review with the institution. The program imposes fiscal consolidation, the abandonment of the fixed exchange rate regime that prevailed for fifteen years, and the gradual elimination of fuel subsidies — measures that the Economy Minister himself, Christian Morales, recently sworn in after the dismissal of Gabriel Espinoza, censured by Parliament, has described as a process that will not impact "the most vulnerable." Dollar flexibilization is already in effect and the government has begun returning foreign currency deposits, with withdrawals authorized up to 3,000 dollars. Nevertheless, dollar deposits are rising, as El Deber reports, while the business sector maintains an unyielding distrust: entrepreneurs identify five factors aggravating the crisis and the think tank Milenio recommends further reducing the deficit and deepening ties with the IMF.

The energy front concentrates some of the most troubling signals. Analysts consulted by Los Tiempos describe exits from the gas crisis as "difficult," in a country whose flagship resource has completed a boom-and-bust arc that today leaves it without sufficient reserves to sustain its export model. Residents in La Paz and El Alto blocked roads to protest the irregular distribution of household gas, a sign that scarcity has ceased to be a trade balance issue and has become a daily social tension. In parallel, the government is advancing an electricity reform: Decree 5770 introduces three factors for calculating electricity tariffs, including the average exchange rate and inflation, which in practice constitutes a gradual price adjustment that the executive has for now declined to label a "tariff hike."

In the agro-industrial sector, a decision has emerged with both energy and commercial implications: the government agreed to raise the ethanol blend in gasoline to 15% starting in November, following negotiations with sugar-alcohol mills and the sugarcane sector. The measure reduces dependence on imported fuels and creates a captive market for domestic ethanol production, but its impact on the final price of gasoline and on sugar availability for export will need to be monitored closely. At the same time, the timber sector is facing a low-intensity diplomatic crisis: Bolivian wood incinerated in Chile has drawn formal complaints from importers in the United States and Mexico, a dispute that strains commercial relations at a moment when Chilean President José Antonio Kast's visit to La Paz puts bilateral integration items — including hydrocarbons and sea access — on the table.

The IMF has already approved an additional credit, and alongside the World Bank and the IDB, Bolivia could receive up to 6.9 billion dollars in multilateral financing. CAF has sealed an alliance worth 3.1 billion. These are figures that, added to the sovereign bond placement, outline external credit access that stands in contrast to the collapse of international reserves under the Arce administration. What remains unresolved is whether that financial oxygen will arrive in time to contain the deterioration of the productive fabric: Cochabamba, the country's third-largest economy, registers a contraction of 4.15%, and the study from the Center for Labor and Agrarian Development Studies warns that any recovery is arriving accompanied by greater labor precariousness and poverty. The next government, according to several analysts, will need at least two years to stabilize the economy on solid foundations. The current one has less margin than its official communications suggest.

What to watch in the coming weeks is the outcome of the IMF's first formal review, the evolution of the flexible exchange rate against domestic inflation, the operational viability of Boliviana de Aviación, and the fate of the ten state-owned enterprises up for liquidation. The constitutional reform driven by economic pressures — which already has its first political agreement — could redefine the rules for private investment. And the 2025 electoral process introduces an additional risk: voters, according to polls, place the economy and unemployment as their top concerns, which will pressure every candidate to promise more than the IMF program allows them to spend.
