# Bolivia: The Economic Pulse — 2026-10-13

*Analysis generated with AI | 2026-10-13*

Bolivia's public debt is approaching a threshold that few developing countries cross without lasting consequences: according to projections released this week by El Deber, the Bolivian state's total debt could exceed one hundred percent of GDP in the course of this year, while public external debt had already reached $14.117 billion as of September 2026. It is a figure that, viewed in isolation, might appear manageable for a mid-sized Andean economy, but one that takes on another dimension when placed alongside the other coordinates of the moment: the International Monetary Fund projects a GDP contraction of 3.3 percent for 2026 and inflation that could climb as high as 20.7 percent, while the World Bank anticipates a decline of 2.8 percent. Bolivia is traversing, simultaneously, a fiscal solvency crisis in the making and a confidence crisis already declared.

What makes this moment particularly revealing is not any single data point, but the tension between the official narrative of stabilization and the accumulating evidence that contradicts it. The Ministry of Economy and Public Finance — which this year celebrated two centuries of existence by enumerating five pillars for future wealth — posted a fiscal surplus in the first five months of 2026 and proclaimed it had reversed the trend inherited from the Luis Arce administration. The new minister, Christian Morales, sworn in following the parliamentary censure of his predecessor Gabriel Espinoza, took office with a mandate to consolidate stability and accelerate reforms. At the same time, the Heritage Foundation ranks Bolivia 117th out of 165 countries on its economic freedom index, a drop of 37 places compared with the prior period, and the Cedla research center documents that the economic recovery the government is promoting coexists with greater labor precarity and rising poverty.

The IMF has consolidated its support with a $1.9 billion program, the second disbursement of which has already been confirmed, and Bolivia is awaiting an additional transfer of $214 million earmarked exclusively for shoring up reserves. Bolivia also raised $1 billion in sovereign bonds in an operation that drew demand five times greater than anticipated, a sign that international markets, for now, are distinguishing between deteriorated fundamentals and the reform trajectory the government of Rodrigo Paz is attempting to credential. Moody's, however, warns that it will observe the durability of the reforms before raising the sovereign rating another notch. Country risk has narrowed to 485 basis points, a real improvement, but the rating agency is demanding proof that the changes are not ephemeral.

The fuel front concentrates perhaps the most explosive contradiction. The government has agreed with the IMF to eliminate the gasoline subsidy by 2027, a measure that was consuming a disproportionate share of public spending and that Fitch Ratings identified as one of the largest fiscal costs in the region alongside Mexico and Nicaragua. Meanwhile, the long lines at service stations and the black market for resale have led business leaders to openly propose liberalizing prices to international levels, a position that would have been politically unviable just months ago. The Hydrocarbons Minister, Marcelo Blanco, pushed through a new Electricity Law and clarified that there is no intention of raising electricity tariffs in the near term, but the message on fuels is less reassuring: the adjustment is coming, and the question is how much social damage it will produce before fiscal equilibria are sufficiently anchored to absorb it.

On the corporate front, cement producer Soboce opted for an unusual route by filing an appeal before the Andean Community of Nations to halt the collection of 744 million bolivianos that local authorities intend to enforce, arguing that execution of the arbitrary ruling would have a systemic impact on the economy. The decision to internationalize a tax dispute before a regional body reflects the level of legal distrust that characterizes the business environment. Separately, ten state-owned enterprises in crisis could be liquidated by decree, according to El Deber, which would amount to an implicit acknowledgment that the state enterprise model that dominated the Bolivian economy during the so-called process of change era has reached its operational limit.

Tax revenue rose 14 percent so far in 2026, reaching 34.7 billion bolivianos, a figure the government cites as confirmation that reforms are producing results. But the context qualifies it: the informal economy continues to grow at twice the rate of the formal economy, 90 percent of micro and small enterprises have closed due to the crisis, and Cochabamba — the country's third-largest economy — registered a contraction of 4.15 percent. Departments such as Pando and Beni have declared themselves in a state of economic emergency.

What investors will need to monitor in the coming weeks is the pace at which the government advances the constitutional reform driven in part by the demands of the economic program, the concrete execution of the subsidy adjustment calendar, and the ability of new minister Morales to maintain fiscal discipline without precipitating a deterioration in social conditions that generates the kind of unrest which, as recent experience reminds us, can paralyze the economy with a single road blockade. The presence of the World Bank's Director of Economic Policy in the country this week suggests multilateral creditors remain attentive to the process, but that the margin for hesitation is narrowing.
