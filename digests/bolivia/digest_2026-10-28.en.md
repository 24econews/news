# Bolivia: The Economic Pulse — 2026-10-28

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-10-28*

Bolivia arrived this week at its first formal review before the International Monetary Fund following the approval of the $1.9 billion credit line, a milestone that under normal circumstances would mark a turning point. What complicates that narrative — and what makes today's picture more tense than hopeful — is that the very headlines surrounding that achievement reveal an economy still fragmented between signs of stabilization and a structural reality that refuses to give ground.

The Ministry of Economy and Public Finance has released data pointing to a fiscal surplus in the first five months of 2026 and a consolidated reduction in country risk to levels below those of Argentina and Ecuador. The government of President Rodrigo Paz also managed to place $1 billion in sovereign bonds in international markets, with demand running five times the offer — an unusual signal for a country the IMF projects as the only South American economy that will contract this year and next. The institution forecasts a GDP decline of between 3 and 3.3 percent in 2026, with inflation that could reach 20.7 percent. That is the backdrop against which the government is trying to frame its stabilization as a nascent success.

The most revealing tension of the day comes from the energy front. In just a few years, Bolivia has gone from being the region's leading exporter of natural gas to needing to import it to sustain its own consumption and power generation. The government has authorized that importation by decree, while the Minister of Public Works, Mauricio Zamora, insists that a plan is in place to avert shortages. The reality on the street contradicts that calm: in La Paz, police have had to escort 43,000 LPG canisters to prevent their diversion into contraband, a phenomenon that, according to studies cited by El Deber, is growing at twice the pace of the formal economy and which 48 percent of Bolivians regard as legitimate or even useful for the national economy. State-owned YPFB has announced the rollout of a georeferenced system to control distribution, but the urgency of the measure speaks for itself.

The currency question remains the other node of uncertainty. The dollar continues to climb in the Bolivian market following the abandonment of the fixed exchange rate of 6.96 bolivianos that had held for fifteen years. The Minister of Economy, Christian Morales — who took office after the parliamentary censure and dismissal of his predecessor Gabriel Espinoza — has ruled out further modifications and has guaranteed the payment of bonds and salaries. He has also confirmed that 99.3 percent of the financial system's loans are denominated in bolivianos, which limits the currency mismatch risk for borrowers. However, business associations warn that the flexible regime remains insufficient to reactivate activity, and the financial sector is additionally facing a judicial investigation into the Banco Central case, with bank executives and Arce's former minister summoned to testify.

The Chamber of Industry, Commerce, Services and Tourism of Santa Cruz — CAINCO — has formally raised with the executive the discussion of a new Investment Law, arguing that legal uncertainty is today the main obstacle to reactivation. That position aligns with Bolivia's 37-place drop in the world economic freedom index and its 147th position in the global ranking. Economist Karl Isakson, cited by El Deber, sums it up precisely: "Uncertainty blocks investment, and that stalls the economy." Development bank CAF has sealed a strategic alliance with Bolivia for $3.1 billion, and the Paz government is promising an additional injection oriented toward productive reactivation, but businesspeople in Cochabamba — the country's third-largest economy, with a projected contraction of 4.15 percent in 2026 — warn that local economic stress remains elevated.

Against that backdrop, the news that the mayor of Cochabamba, Manfred Reyes Villa, has announced the arrival of a businessman interested in investing in Boliviana de Aviación — whose management has proposed transforming the state airline into a mixed-ownership company — adds a corporate dimension to the reform agenda, though the materialization of that process is still in very preliminary stages. The discussion also remains alive on the project to convert the Viru Viru airport in Santa Cruz into a regional hub capable of moving 400,000 tons annually, and on the reactivation of the Arica–La Paz railway after twenty years of paralysis.

What remains to be watched in the coming weeks is multifaceted: the outcome of the IMF's first technical review and whether Bolivia meets its fiscal consolidation commitments; the evolution of the flexible exchange rate and its impact on inflation at a time when the Banco Central already projects that poverty will break records this year; and whether the government manages to advance the Investment Law before the political window narrows. The IMF agreement bought time, but the real test of the Bolivian economy is only just beginning.
