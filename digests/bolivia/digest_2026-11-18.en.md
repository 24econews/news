# Bolivia: The Economic Pulse — 2026-11-18

The dismissal of Economy Minister Gabriel Espinoza and his replacement by Christian Morales Burgos — occurring amid a week that Espinoza himself described as one of "economic devastation" caused by social unrest — commands political attention in Bolivia this Tuesday, but it should not overshadow what is truly revealing about the day: an accumulation of hard data confirming that Bolivia's economy is not weathering a cyclical crisis but rather undergoing a process of structural decomposition with few visible exits in the short term.

The most striking figure comes from YPFB, the state oil company, which confirmed that Bolivia holds just 2.30 TCF of proven natural gas reserves — the lowest level in two decades and a 78% decline from the peak recorded in 2013. The country that for fifteen years financed its fiscal expansion model with gas rents — exporting to Argentina and Brazil and subsidizing everything from fuels to flour with those revenues — now faces the concrete prospect of having to import the very gas it once exported. The Hydrocarbons Minister himself conceded that Bolivia would need to invest at least $2.1 billion to reactivate the sector, but simultaneously acknowledged that the current model — where 93% of the rent goes to the State and only 7% is left for private oil companies — makes the country unattractive to foreign investment. It is a trap of the government's own design: external capital is needed to survive, but the fiscal terms drive that capital away.

The underlying macroeconomic reading comes from the IMF, which projects a 3% contraction for the Bolivian economy this year, in line with national figures that already recorded a 3.27% GDP decline through June, with nine of eleven sectors retreating in the second quarter. Cochabamba, the country's third most populous department, projects an even steeper 4.15% drop, with barely 14.6% of its working-age population in formal employment. A study by the Centro de Estudios para el Desarrollo Laboral y Agrario (Cedla) highlights the paradox that defines this stage: there is nominal recovery in some indicators but accompanied by greater labor precariousness and more poverty, suggesting that even the positive signals mask a deterioration in the quality of employment and real income.

Against this backdrop, the Rodrigo Paz government is turning to external financing as its principal stabilization tool. This week, the pursuit of $750 million in IDB credits for macroeconomic and fiscal stability was authorized, on top of the $500 million recently approved by the World Bank. An analyst quoted by El Deber was precise in his diagnosis: "dollars buy time," but they do not correct the structural deficit. The parallel exchange rate, which traded above 15 bolivianos per dollar in recent months, moderated slightly to Bs 11.67, though the underlying pressure persists. The outgoing Economy Minister reiterated that the banking system has foreign currency available, and the Central Bank announced that financial institutions with smaller deposits will receive their dollars first — a measure that reveals both the scarcity of reserves and the strain across the financial system.

The subsidy front adds another layer of complexity. A decree signed this week extends the gasoline subsidy only until January 12, forcing the government to prepare new legislation before that date. The Fundación Jubileo calculated that, without the subsidy, the price of gasoline would climb to Bs 16, versus the current Bs 3.74. The Hydrocarbons Minister confirmed that the subsidy will eventually be lifted, but that no date has been set — a classic signal from governments that know a measure is inevitable but fear its political cost. Meanwhile, the government authorized an additional Bs 180 million for Emapa, the state-owned food company, with the aim of keeping the price of flour subsidized and protecting the country's staple "pan de batalla," and earmarked another Bs 350 million to boost corn and rice production. Subsidy spending continues, in other words, even as hydrocarbon revenues collapse.

The mining sector adds yet another front of uncertainty. The government decided to put the rollout of the new Mining Code "on hold" after strong objections from mining cooperatives, which account for much of the informal employment in the sector. The business chamber Cainco warned that attracting investment requires legal certainty — an argument that is repeated with growing urgency across every productive sector. Bolivia ranks 147th in the economic freedom index, according to data released this week, placing the country in the lowest tier regionally.

On the positive side, Bolivia resumed cotton exports after fifteen years with a first shipment to Paraguay, and negotiated an initial €250 million package from Germany for forest and water resource conservation. Soybeans, which generate $3.5 billion annually and account for half of agricultural growth, remain the country's most solid productive anchor. Yet even in that sector, international markets are raising their traceability requirements for Bolivian beef purchases, adding further competitive pressure.

Room to maneuver is narrowing; external credits provide oxygen, but the countdown on Bolivia's energy model is not pausing.
