# Bolivia: The Economic Pulse — 2026-09-22

*Analysis generated with AI | 2026-09-22*

Bolivia woke up today to an economic tension that admits no half-measures: the decree fully eliminating the diesel subsidy has ceased to be an abstract threat and has become the axis of a political, social, and macroeconomic confrontation that is testing the cohesion of President Rodrigo Paz's new government. The fuel price jumped to Bs 17.95 per liter—more than double the previous subsidized price—and the impact was felt immediately in food markets, freight, and intercity transport fares, with reports of increases in chicken, vegetables, and long-distance bus tickets, according to Los Tiempos.

The transport sector's response was swift and confrontational. Drivers convened a national assembly to define pressure measures against Decree 5716, while the free transport sector entered a recess during negotiations with the government, a sign that dialogue remains fragile. Governor Leonardo Loza publicly demanded that the government "back down" and called for an emergency summit. In response, the Ministry of Defense warned it would not allow roadblocks, language reminiscent of earlier episodes of national paralysis that, according to data cited by Los Tiempos, have cost the country as much as $2.7 billion and fourteen lives in a single recent episode of fifty days of highway closures.

The government responded with a combination of firmness and offsetting measures. The Minister of Economy defended the move as part of the structural adjustment agreed with the IMF, acknowledging that the transition entails pressure on the transport sector. The executive proposed accelerating the conversion of public vehicles to compressed natural gas, announced the payment of the PEPE II bonus to more than two million people, and established ten social protection measures. In parallel, it instructed mayors to monitor prices to prevent speculation, though the municipalities themselves warned that they would need additional budget and compensation to absorb the fiscal impact of the subsidy elimination. The industrial sector, for its part, aligned itself with the government: business leaders rejected any reversal of the decree and called for roads to remain open.

What makes this moment singular is that the elimination of the diesel subsidy is not an isolated event but the most visible piece of a macroeconomic reform of historic proportions. President Paz enacted Law 1765, which formalizes the $1.9 billion agreement with the IMF, approved by more than two-thirds of the Chamber of Deputies and ratified by the Senate. Disbursements will be quarterly and conditional on the fulfillment of reforms that, according to El Deber, include ten structural changes to the Bolivian economy. The Inter-American Development Bank will additionally contribute credits that will cover 10.5 months of Renta Dignidad. Together, this could total up to $6.9 billion in external financing if World Bank commitments are included.

The geopolitical dimension of Bolivia's realignment took on an unusually high profile with the meeting between President Paz and U.S. Secretary of State Marco Rubio. Washington emphasized its support for the IMF agreement and the possibility that a new investment law could open the Bolivian market to U.S. capital in artificial intelligence, energy, hydrocarbons, and mining—sectors in which Bolivia holds, according to Los Tiempos, 80% of the so-called critical minerals demanded by the global economy. Simultaneously, China signed a cooperation agreement with Bolivia and pledged $35 million in economic aid, and Tarija hosted a bilateral economic cooperation forum under the slogan "Bolivia: Toward the World with China." The competition between Washington and Beijing for influence over Bolivian resources, especially lithium, thus becomes an explicit variable in the country's economic landscape.

Markets reflect the complexity of the moment. The dollar exchange rate climbed again to Bs 11, a level at which it will remain until the start of the week, according to El Deber, complicating cost calculations for businesses. Currency volatility—the boliviano was pegged at Bs 6.96 for fifteen years before the shift to a flexible regime—is generating uncertainty in corporate planning. The Central Bank warned that lenders have not yet resolved the loan restructuring process, a sign that the financial system remains under strain. At the same time, the government reported a fiscal surplus in the first five months of 2026, a figure that contrasts with the evident structural deterioration in hydrocarbons, whose output plunged 13.4% according to data cited by El Deber.

In the coming days, the most relevant indicators to monitor will be the outcome of the drivers' assembly and whether blockade measures materialize, the daily dollar quotation under the Central Bank's new flexible regime, the pace of disbursement of the first tranche of the IMF loan, and the speed with which the new Economy Minister, Christian Morales Burgos, can articulate a signal of reformist continuity that reassures both international creditors and the productive sectors now watching the rise in costs with alarm. The real test of the program does not lie in the paper signed with Washington or with the IMF: it lies in whether the government can sustain the diesel reform without the country's highways being shut down again.
