# Bolivia: The Economic Pulse — 2026-11-17

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-11-17*

The Banco Central de Bolivia this week began returning $438 million to the country's financial institutions, simultaneously enabling unrestricted dollar operations and raising the deposit withdrawal cap to $50,000 per account. The move represents the BCB's most significant technical intervention in years and arrives at a moment when confidence in the financial system is under severe strain, exemplified by the case of Ueno Bank, whose depositors could lose up to 12% of their savings to exchange-rate effects. That the central bank is prioritizing institutions with smaller deposit bases in the foreign-currency distribution queue speaks both to the scarcity of dollars and to a political intent to shield the system's most vulnerable actors—though the most powerful signal is the sheer scale of the operation itself: nearly half a billion dollars mobilized at once to restore an exchange-rate normality that had simply evaporated.

The backdrop is an economy that, according to data released this week, contracted 3.27% through June, with nine of eleven sectors retreating in the second quarter. The IMF projects a 3% contraction for the full year, and Cochabamba, the country's third-largest economy, anticipates its own 4.15% contraction, with just 14.6% of its working-age population in formal employment. Bolivia ranks 147th on the economic freedom index, according to Los Tiempos, and international experts consulted by El Deber warn that the country's position is vulnerable to any additional external shock. The parallel-market exchange rate has eased to 11.67 bolivianos per dollar, a mild appreciation that some analysts read as transitory relief rather than structural correction.

The dismissal of Economy Minister Gabriel Espinoza and the appointment of Christian Morales Burgos in his place encapsulate the political urgency of the moment. Espinoza, who only weeks ago told Bloomberg Línea that Bolivia would see positive growth in 2026, left office amid what France 24 described as "a week of scandals." His successor takes over with an official mandate to "consolidate stability and accelerate the country's transformations"—a formulation that the new interim minister Óscar Mario Justiniano, in remarks to El Deber, translated in more concrete terms: "the economy needs cooperation, not ideology." The Santa Cruz Chamber of Industry, Commerce and Services warned for its part that attracting investment requires clear laws and legal certainty, conditions that the Confederation of Private Entrepreneurs of the East considers currently absent.

The underlying energy crisis gives structural depth to all of this instability. Bolivia has lost 78% of its proven gas reserves since 2013 and today has barely 2.3 TCF certified, while the current model allocates 93% of hydrocarbon revenue to the state and only 7% to the oil companies—a split that YPFB's own president publicly acknowledged as incompatible with attracting foreign investment. The Hydrocarbons Minister confirmed that the gasoline subsidy will be eliminated, though he declined to specify a date, contradicting an earlier statement by the YPFB president that had flagged January 12 as the implementation date. Former president Jorge Quiroga labeled that internal contradiction as a signal that "will unleash a brutal wave of speculation," while trucking associations are already threatening mobilizations. More than 700 trucks have now spent three days stranded at the border with Chile, blocked by the dispute.

Against this backdrop, the government of Rodrigo Paz is attempting to project order on two simultaneous fronts. On subnational fiscal policy, the mayors of capital cities reached an agreement with the executive branch for the return of the 12% of the Direct Hydrocarbon Tax withheld for 2026, temporarily defusing pressure from municipal governments that had been demanding an equal 50-50 split of the IDH. On the international front, Bolivia participated in a CAF forum to reposition itself on the regional economic agenda, announced an initial €250 million package from Germany for forest and water conservation, and advanced work on rules for the carbon-credit market—a long-term bet on its roughly 53 million hectares of forest cover. Gold surged past $4,000 an ounce in international markets and silver reached $51.46, prices that are supporting mining exports, although El Deber notes that projected funds for mining exploration will decline, creating tension between current revenue and future capacity.

The closure of Manaco after 86 years of production and accumulated losses of 130 million bolivianos is the industrial symbol of the moment: an emblematic manufacturing firm unable to survive the combination of demand contraction, exchange-rate pressure and rising informality. Cochabamba's business community described to El Deber a stagnant economy in urgent need of reform, while sectors in Potosí rejected the new Mining Code proposal that the government continues to socialize amid demands from cooperatives.

What to watch over the coming weeks is threefold: the speed with which the BCB manages to stabilize dollar operations in the banking system, and whether the return of the $438 million produces the confidence effect it is designed to generate; the effective date and mechanism for the elimination of the fuel subsidy, whose current ambiguity is a direct source of speculative and inflationary risk; and the ability of new minister Morales Burgos to articulate a credible economic program before the IMF, whose staff-level backing remains the main external anchor of Bolivian stability.
