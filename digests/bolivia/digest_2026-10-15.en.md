# Bolivia: The Economic Pulse — 2026-10-15

Bolivia is navigating one of the most regulatorily dense days in its recent economic history: within a matter of hours, the government of President Rodrigo Paz issued two decrees that simultaneously touch the foreign exchange market, the gold export sector and the electricity tariff structure, while the IMF confirmed that the Bolivian economy will be the only one in South America to contract in 2026, with a projected 3% decline. The convergence of these signals — shock reforms at home and an external verdict with no recent precedent — reveals the magnitude of the adjustment the new government is attempting to execute before fiscal deterioration becomes irreversible.

The gold decree carries the most immediate implications for the currency market. According to Opinión Bolivia and Los Tiempos, the measure requires gold exporters to deposit 80% of the foreign currency generated by their foreign sales into the Bolivian financial system. The move addresses a structural hemorrhage: Bolivia is one of the region's largest informal gold exporters, but a substantial share of those dollars never returned to the country, instead feeding the dollar scarcity that for months paralyzed the banking system and drove up import costs. Authorities recently intervened in irregular mining operations in rivers in Potosí, underscoring that the problem is not merely one of exchange rate policy but of territorial control over a resource that largely operates outside the state. The question operators in the sector are now asking is whether the repatriation mechanism will actually be enforceable, given that Bolivia's gold export chain involves cooperatives and traders with little traceability.

The second decree, signed by Paz himself and extensively covered by El Deber and Los Tiempos, indexes electricity tariffs to the average dollar exchange rate and to inflation. It is a decision the government sought to frame as a technical reform, ruling out "immediate hikes," but which in practice institutionalizes the pass-through of currency costs to the end consumer. It is difficult to separate this decree from the broader context of the IMF agreement, which according to El Deber contemplates eliminating the gasoline subsidy by 2027 and has already led to the disappearance of long lines at service stations — a sign that the market is beginning to adjust to the new price reality. The electricity indexation to the dollar is, in that sense, a second pillar of the same price-realignment process, completing what several analysts are calling the "adjustment without anesthesia" that the Paz government is trying to administer with political tweezers.

The financial backdrop is grim. Bolivia expects public debt to exceed 100% of GDP in 2026, while public external debt had already reached USD 14.117 billion as of September, according to El Deber. On top of this comes a 37-position drop in the global economic freedom index, a deterioration that leaves the country in a particularly adverse spot for attracting foreign investment precisely when it urgently needs capital. The IMF agreement, which includes an initial disbursement of USD 214 million earmarked exclusively for shoring up reserves within a total program of USD 1.9 billion, offers financial breathing room but does not resolve the underlying structural problem: an economy that, according to the Ministerio de Economía y Finanzas Públicas, posted a fiscal surplus in the first five months of 2026 thanks to the adjustment, but which the IMF still projects contracting 3% — the only such case in all of South America.

The business sector, organized through Cainco and the Confederación de Empresarios Privados de Bolivia, is pressing for an investment law offering minimum legal guarantees to be approved before year-end. The strategic alliance between banking and industry, discussed at the Congreso Empresarial de Cochabamba, points in the same direction: without a predictable legal framework, tax revenue — which has grown 14% so far in 2026 — will not be enough to offset the drop in private investment. Meanwhile, Bolivian timber exports are set to close 2026 at their lowest level in more than three decades, an indicator of the competitive erosion afflicting the non-extractive productive sector, aggravated by the dispute with Chile over detained timber and by domestic blockades that, according to Los Tiempos, drove up costs and squeezed food, transport and commerce.

The official dollar has accumulated a 2.25% decline over ten days following the exchange rate liberalization, a move that some business associations consider insufficient to reactivate the economy and that stands in contrast to signals from the parallel market. Economy Minister Christian Morales ruled out abrupt changes to exchange rate policy and noted that 99.3% of loans are denominated in bolivianos, which limits balance-sheet risk for the banking system in the face of further depreciation. Bank executives and the former Economy Minister under the Luis Arce administration will be summoned in connection with the Banco Central de Bolivia case, a judicial process that adds institutional tension to an already crowded reform calendar.

What to watch closely in the coming weeks is threefold: first, whether the gold decree effectively increases foreign currency inflows or triggers an evasion response from the informal mining sector; second, whether the Legislative Assembly passes the investment law that the private sector considers urgent; and third, whether the IMF disburses the first tranche of the program on schedule, which will depend on Bolivia meeting its interim fiscal targets. A delay on any of these fronts could reopen pressure on the exchange rate and reverse the fragile stability gains that the Paz government has been touting as its main achievement in its first months in office.
