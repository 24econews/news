> TITLE: Bolivia pivots from gas exporter to importer as structural crisis deepens

# Bolivia: The Economic Pulse — 2026-10-29

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-10-29*

Bolivia is preparing to import natural gas — a commodity that for two decades was the backbone of its export model and the source of foreign currency that financed the so-called 21st-century growth "miracle" — and that symbolic and economic inversion of the country's role in regional energy markets is, today, the story that best captures the depth of the structural crisis facing the government of Rodrigo Paz.

The reversal is dramatic in its historical dimensions. Bolivia was for years the main supplier of natural gas to Brazil and Argentina, contracts that sustained robust international reserves and the expansive public spending that the Evo Morales administration elevated to state policy. Today, with fields in accelerated decline, the Vice Minister of Hydrocarbons has publicly warned that "by 2029 we would run out of gas," and the Paz government has issued a decree authorizing gas imports for domestic and industrial use. The problem is not merely geological: according to experts cited by El Deber, imported gas will cost nearly triple what industry currently pays at subsidized prices. For an economy that the Central Bank projects will contract by 3.6% this year — and that the IMF sees falling 3% in 2025 and 3.3% in 2026, with inflation potentially reaching 20.7% — that energy cost shock arrives at the worst possible moment. ECLAC has already classified Bolivia as South America's slowest-growing economy, a title that would have been unthinkable at the end of the last decade.

What makes the current moment even more significant is the convergence of multiple simultaneous pressures. The parallel-market dollar — which Economy Minister Christian Morales, recently appointed by Paz to replace the censured José Gabriel Espinoza, who was removed following an unprecedented parliamentary vote, has pledged to stabilize below 11 bolivianos — continues to generate distortions in an economy where 99.3% of credit is denominated in local currency but foreign-exchange scarcity is structural. The Economy Ministry has clarified that it is not within its purview to allocate dollars to YPFB, the state oil company, which illustrates the institutional fragmentation compounding crisis management. Meanwhile, gold mining cooperatives, which according to Los Tiempos produce more but contribute less in royalties than private firms, are preparing to import diesel on their own account given the state's inability to guarantee supply — an eloquent sign of the collapse of the energy-subsidy model that for years was emblematic of the Bolivian framework.

The productive sector has been sending distress signals for months that the previous administration ignored, with visible consequences. Cochabamba, the country's third-largest economy, projects a 4.15% contraction for 2026 and formal employment reaching barely 14.6% of the active population. Business leaders at the Santa Cruz Chamber of Industry, Commerce, Services and Tourism (Cainco) have laid out ten urgent decisions to confront the crisis, among them the passage of an Investment Law that the Paz government has spent nearly a year failing to enact. Minister Fernando Aramayo announced that a new bill will be sent to the Legislative Assembly on an expedited basis, but the fact that Bolivia has fallen 37 places in the world economic freedom index — now ranking 147th out of 180 countries — suggests that the investor credibility problem will not be solved by legislation alone: it requires a deeper shift in signals that markets have yet to see.

Against that backdrop, the reduction in the country risk spread to 364 basis points that the Economy Ministry has proudly announced — below Argentina and Ecuador, according to the government — and Moody's recent sovereign upgrade to Caa2 with a positive outlook represent real, if modest, progress. The staff-level agreement with the IMF for a disbursement of roughly $1.9 billion, and the World Bank's backing with an additional $200 million for social protection, offer a financial cushion the previous government did not have, or did not seek. But departmental governors are already demanding a fiscal pact to redistribute resources, drought has killed more than 75,000 head of cattle across six departments, and the government has had to use police escorts to move 43,000 LPG canisters to La Paz and El Alto to prevent diversion to the black market. These are symptoms of a state whose capacity to manage the day-to-day economy is under pressure that official macroeconomic indicators do not fully capture.

Morales, the new minister, has proposed a "Productive Triangle" as the centerpiece of his policy and has called for a broad national accord. The World Bank projects a 4% rebound in 2027 if reforms advance, but warns that 2026 will bring an additional 3.2% contraction. The next government — or the current one, in its most difficult phase — will need, according to economists consulted by El Deber, at least two years to stabilize an economy that today combines GDP contraction, exchange rate pressure, fuel shortages, a collapse in gas production, and a business community that describes conditions as "elevated stress." What to watch in the coming weeks is whether the new Investment Law secures genuine legislative backing, whether the price of imported gas can be absorbed without triggering a new inflationary wave, and whether the flexible exchange rate that Minister Morales defends manages to narrow the gap between the official and parallel markets without unanchoring expectations in a system where informal dollarization is already a market response to institutional uncertainty.
