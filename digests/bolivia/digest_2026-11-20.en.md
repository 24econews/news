# Bolivia: The Economic Pulse — 2026-11-20

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-11-20*

The dismissal of Economy Minister Gabriel Espinoza and his replacement by Christian Morales Burgos amid a week of political scandal was no routine cabinet reshuffle: it was the trigger that laid bare, all at once, the scale of the contradictions running through President Rodrigo Paz's economic management. Within days, the Bolivian government had to simultaneously handle a criminal complaint filed by the vice president against the outgoing minister, conflicting signals on the future of fuel subsidies, an economy that contracted 3.27% in the first half of the year, and a state apparatus seeking to anchor its credibility in emergency external loans.

The new minister, Christian Morales Burgos, took office with the official mandate to "consolidate stability and accelerate the country's transformations," according to the Ministry of Economy and Public Finance. But the stability he inherits is fragile. The IMF projects a 3% contraction for the full year, nine of the eleven sectors of the economy contracted in the second quarter, and Cochabamba — the country's third-largest economy according to the Atlas Económico — projects its own decline of 4.15%, with barely 14.6% of its working-age population in formal employment. International experts consulted by El Deber described Bolivia's position as "vulnerable," an assessment hard to dispute when the parallel-market exchange rate hovers around Bs 11.67 per dollar and the central bank continues to ration foreign currency across financial institutions, prioritizing those with smaller deposit bases.

The government's response has been to turn urgently to multilateral financing. The Inter-American Development Bank approved loans totaling $750 million earmarked for macroeconomic and fiscal stabilization, while the World Bank had already authorized an additional $500 million and the IMF backed a $1.9 billion program with an immediate disbursement of $214 million. CAF, for its part, sealed a strategic partnership worth $3.1 billion. Taken together, Bolivia is building a sizeable external liquidity scaffolding — but one that comes at the cost of adjustment commitments the government itself avoids spelling out clearly to its political base. The decision to raise the maximum lending rate for microenterprises to 17% — a correction of distortions accumulated over years of artificially low regulated rates — has already drawn a warning from businessman Samuel Doria Medina that the measure could deepen the recession. The Financial System Supervisory Authority (ASFI) clarified that loans in force before November 16 retain their original rates, but the directional signal is unmistakable: the cost of money is rising at a moment when the credit portfolio is growing on paper even as starting a business remains, according to El Deber, extraordinarily difficult.

The deepest structural contradiction, however, remains energy. Bolivia today holds 2.3 trillion cubic feet (TCF) of proven natural gas reserves, the lowest level in twenty years and 78% below 2013, according to YPFB data. The Minister of Hydrocarbons himself admitted to El Deber that the country is not attractive for foreign investment in the sector, and analysts noted that the fiscal model — which allocates 93% of the rent to the state and only 7% to oil companies — structurally discourages exploration. A recent court ruling cleared the way to search for gas in the Tariquía reserve, in Tarija, in an area that could double current reserves, though the environmental dispute persists. YPFB presented a sector reactivation plan, and the Ministry of Hydrocarbons speaks of needing $2.1 billion in investment under a prospective new hydrocarbons law. Meanwhile, the threat that Bolivia will have to import natural gas — once a net exporter whose gas financed more than a decade of fiscal bonanza — is becoming an increasingly less hypothetical horizon.

Against that backdrop, the government is trying to manage social pressure with short-term instruments: it transferred Bs 180 million to Emapa to keep the price of bread stable, adjusted the flour subsidy, reaffirmed that "no date has been set" to lift the gasoline subsidy — though the Minister of Hydrocarbons confirmed it will happen — and enacted Decree 5775 to reset prices on public works, preserving the continuity of more than 11,000 projects. Municipalities, in turn, are negotiating the return of $31 million in withheld funds and the 12% of 2026 IDH revenue the central government promised to restore. In this context, more than 700 trucks were stranded for three days at the border with Chile, a logistical disruption that underscores the operational fragility of Bolivian foreign trade.

Amid the grim macro picture, two diversification signals emerged that merit tracking. Bolivia resumed cotton exports for the first time in fifteen years, with an initial shipment of 23 tons to Paraguay, while Peru authorized the product's entry into its territory, opening an additional route. The government is also advancing the design of a regulatory framework for carbon markets, seeking to monetize the country's forest expanse in an expanding global market, though the normative architecture remains undefined. The digital economy is also making strides in payments and remittances, El Deber noted, reflecting a regional trend that Bolivia is absorbing with a lag but with growing traction.

What to watch closely in the coming weeks is the speed with which new minister Morales Burgos translates the commitments of the IMF program into concrete fiscal consolidation measures, the date and mechanism the government ultimately chooses to scale back fuel subsidies — a decision that peasant groups in La Paz have already warned they "will not allow" — and whether the Tariquía court ruling can mobilize private capital toward gas exploration before the decline in reserves becomes irreversible for the balance of payments.
