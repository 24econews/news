# Bolivia: The Economic Pulse — 2026-11-19

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-11-19*

This week's court ruling opening the door to oil exploration in the Tariquía nature reserve is not merely a legal dispute over a protected area: it is the trigger forcing Bolivia to confront, simultaneously and with no easy exit, the extinction of its energy model and the fragility of its public finances. While President Rodrigo Paz's government tries to project stability, the accumulation of adverse data paints a picture that international loans can delay but not reverse.

The natural gas numbers are the most telling. According to YPFB, proven reserves stand at just 2.30 TCF, the lowest level in two decades and a 78% collapse from the peak recorded in 2013. That collapse carries direct and immediate consequences: Bolivia, which for fifteen years was the main natural gas supplier to Argentina and Brazil, is now on the verge of becoming a net importer. The sector itself acknowledges the problem: the current fiscal framework, which allocates 93% of revenues to the state and only 7% to private oil companies, has driven out foreign investment for years. The president of Cainco, the Chamber of Industry, Commerce, Services and Tourism of Santa Cruz, warned that without legal certainty and a competitive regulatory framework it is impossible to attract risk capital to exploration — a position that the Minister of Hydrocarbons himself indirectly endorsed by admitting that Bolivia is not currently an attractive destination for investment in the sector. YPFB identifies a theoretical potential of 14.6 TCF in undeveloped areas, including the block adjacent to Tariquía, but that figure is speculative until drilling takes place, and drilling demands precisely the kind of private capital that the current model has scared away.

The government responded with a highly symbolic move: in the same week, the Ministry of Hydrocarbons confirmed that the gasoline subsidy will be eliminated, though without setting a date, generating what economist and former politician Jorge Quiroga — known as "Tuto" — described as "mixed signals" capable of unleashing a wave of speculation. The Túpac Katari Federation and the La Paz peasant movement have already announced they will not allow the subsidy to end, and the Bolivian Workers' Central (COB) has called a national march in support of the mobilized sectors. The fiscal cost of the fuel subsidy represents one of the heaviest items on the Treasury's books, but eliminating it without social compensation threatens to ignite a conflict that the government, in its first year in office, does not yet have the political capacity to absorb. The ANH, meanwhile, confirmed that diesel remains at Bs 17.95, leaving that other line of subsidized spending intact for now.

The macroeconomic backdrop against which these decisions are being taken is severe. The IMF projects a 3% contraction for the Bolivian economy this year, a figure consistent with second-quarter data: activity fell 3.27% through June, with nine of eleven sectors in negative territory. Cochabamba, the country's third-largest economy according to the Economic Atlas, is projecting its own contraction of 4.15% and formal employment that will barely reach 14.6% of its active population — a sign of precarization that the Center for Studies on Labor and Agrarian Development, Cedla, is already documenting at the national level. Bolivia also fell to 147th place in the economic freedom ranking, slipping on an indicator that foreign investors consult before any official communiqué.

Faced with that picture, the government has resorted to external financing on a massive and accelerated scale. The World Bank approved $500 million, the IDB authorized an additional $750 million in credit lines, and CAF sealed a strategic alliance worth $3.1 billion. The new Minister of Economy, Christian Morales Burgos — appointed after the dismissal of Gabriel Espinoza amid what France 24 described as "a week of scandals" — also swore in three vice ministers with the explicit mandate to "consolidate stability." Analysts are cautious: "dollars buy time," one summarized to El Deber, but they do not halt the structural deficit while spending on subsidies remains intact and gas revenues continue to fall. The exchange rate in the flexible market dropped to Bs 11.67, an apparently positive signal, although in the parallel market the dollar remains at Bs 12, a gap that reflects persistent distrust about the real availability of foreign currency.

There are, within that somber landscape, two glimmers of diversification that deserve attention. Bolivia resumed cotton exports for the first time in fifteen years, with an initial shipment of 23 tons to Paraguay and the formal opening of the Peruvian market, driven by the use of biotechnology that revived the crop. And the government is advancing the design of a regulatory framework for carbon markets, having already secured an initial €250 million package for the management of its forests. Both initiatives are relevant in the medium term — Bolivia has one of the largest tropical forest covers on the continent — but their current scale is insufficient to offset the hole left by the decline in gas.

What markets and international creditors will be watching in the coming weeks is whether the Paz government manages to articulate a credible signal on subsidy reform without triggering a social conflict of significant magnitude, whether the new hydrocarbons framework that the sector minister promised — with an estimated required investment of $2.1 billion — advances in the Legislative Assembly, and whether the Tariquía ruling translates into concrete exploration contracts or is blocked by resistance from indigenous communities and environmental organizations. The negotiation of the 50-50 fiscal pact between the central government and municipalities, which resumed dialogue this week around $31 million in withheld funds, adds another layer of political tension to an already saturated agenda. Bolivia has borrowed financing; what it does not yet have is an economic model to replace it.
