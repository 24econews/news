# Bolivia: The Economic Pulse — 2026-11-26

*Analysis generated with AI | 2026-11-26*

The "diselazo" shaking Bolivia—the hike in diesel prices to Bs 20.57 per liter triggered on Tuesday, November 24 under Supreme Decree 5716—is unleashing a crisis that simultaneously cuts across social stability, public finances, the food chain, and the credibility of President Rodrigo Paz's adjustment model. What makes this moment particularly significant is the convergence of contradictory forces: the government is consolidating its relationship with the IMF and announcing external credits totaling $1.25 billion, but at the same time it is inflaming transporters, farmers, and miners with a measure that, according to Bloomberg, accompanies the boliviano's 41.53% depreciation against the dollar so far in 2026, positioning the currency as the world's worst-performing over that period.

The effects of the diesel hike are immediate and branch out across several fronts. In Chuquisaca, public transport pulled its units off the streets and long lines formed to access gasoline. In Sucre, transporters blocked main arteries until the Police intervened and confirmed the arrest of several protesters. Oruro's heavy transport sector went even further, threatening an indefinite national strike, while drivers' unions in La Paz gave the government a 24-hour deadline to reverse the measure. At the border with Brazil, a telling phenomenon emerged: Bolivian vehicles crossed into the neighboring country to fuel up more cheaply, exposing the paradox that Bolivia—once a net energy exporter—now has the region's most expensive and lowest-quality diesel, according to the transporters themselves.

Pressure on the agricultural sector is equally severe. The Cámara Agropecuaria del Oriente warned that more expensive diesel will drive up costs for the summer crop cycle, which covers 1.5 million hectares. Producers estimated that more than 5,000 families could abandon their planting. Gold miners in northern La Paz declared an emergency. The Jubileo foundation projected that, combined with the other adjustment measures, an additional 275,000 people could fall into poverty. Emapa stepped in by setting flour prices between Bs 290 and Bs 310 per quintal across six departments, a signal that the government recognizes the inflationary pressure on basic foods that the fuel hike is generating.

President Paz defended the measure by arguing that diesel must be governed by international prices, in a context where—paradoxically—crude oil has fallen on global markets but the worldwide refining capacity crisis has kept processed diesel prices elevated. The Minister of Hydrocarbons called for public patience and anticipated that "the trend is for fuel to come down," without offering concrete timelines. The government is weighing compensation for vulnerable sectors, and the president announced the allocation of Bs 882 million for irrigators and producers earmarked for productive projects, explicitly not subsidies. As for gasoline, whose subsidy remains in place, the Economy Minister confirmed that the decision on its elimination will be made in January 2027.

The macroeconomic backdrop qualifies, without softening, the picture of social tension. The Central Bank reported that year-on-year inflation stood at 7.1% as of the report date, though it projects 2026 will close at 9.2%, eleven percentage points lower than the level recorded in 2025. International reserves rose to $3.965 billion, and the BCB said the exchange-rate adjustment allowed Bolivia to exit the financial "corralito" and stabilize the economy. The IMF completed the first review of the Extended Fund Facility agreement for $193 million, and the government approved three additional external loans worth $1.25 billion aimed at bolstering liquid reserves. Meanwhile, the 2027 General State Budget bill authorizes public debt operations in external capital markets of up to $3.5 billion, a sign of appetite for international financing that stands in contrast to the fragility of the boliviano.

The official narrative points to 2027 as the year of recovery and investment, with the government expecting to enact a new investment law before the end of 2026. The Finance Secretary ruled out that the economy had entered recession, characterizing the recent contraction as transitory. Bolivia is also reactivating its regional integration axis: La Paz and Lima are planning new border crossings and a trade corridor to Brazil, and the IDB is backing a border modernization plan. Bolivia also accounts for 45% of the Iquique Free Zone market, a commercial link that is gaining strategic relevance in the context of the rebalancing of its foreign relations.

What will define the coming weeks is not so much the arithmetic of fiscal adjustment but its political sustainability. With the transport sector on the warpath, agriculture on alert, and Jubileo's social warnings materializing in real time, the Paz government faces the test of whether it can manage the pain of reform without the streets forcing it to backtrack—as has already happened with similar episodes in Bolivia and other countries in the region. The decision on gasoline in January, the boliviano's behavior against the dollar, and the first disbursements of the external loans will be the most immediate indicators to watch.
