# Bolivia: The Economic Pulse — 2026-10-19

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-10-19*

Bolivia faces its first formal review before the International Monetary Fund today since the approval of the $1.9 billion credit facility, and the outcome arrives at a moment when President Rodrigo Paz's administration is attempting to sustain a stabilization narrative even as the most recent macroeconomic data paint a notably bleaker picture.

The central paradox defining Bolivia's economic moment is this: the government is reporting a fiscal surplus for the first five months of 2026 and touting the placement of $1 billion in sovereign bonds with demand five times greater than supply, while the IMF projects that Bolivia will register the only economic contraction in South America this year, estimated at 3% of GDP. The Ministry of Economy and Public Finance, headed by the recently sworn-in Christian Morales Burgos, has characterized the agreement with the multilateral lender as the start of a new chapter, but economists cited by local media warn that the risks of the transition far outweigh the immediate opportunities.

Public debt is the figure that best captures the magnitude of the inherited problem. According to reporting published by El Deber, Bolivia expects its indebtedness to exceed 100% of GDP over the course of this year, a threshold that international markets regard as an indicator of systemic vulnerability. External debt already surpasses $14.3 billion, and the IMF agreement — which contemplates potential access to roughly $6.9 billion between the Fund, the World Bank and the IDB — comes with conditionalities that the government has chosen to communicate with caution. The Economy Ministry's statement claiming to have "made the scope of the agreement transparent" is eloquent for what it leaves unsaid: internal political resistance has not disappeared. The faction aligned with Evo Morales is threatening mobilizations if the government does not restore the diesel subsidy within seven days, and the Central Obrera Boliviana has declared a state of emergency, rejecting the adjustment decrees.

That tension between the fiscal adjustment agreed with the IMF and internal redistributive pressures is manifesting with particular sharpness in the energy sector. The government has enacted Decree 5770, which introduces three new factors into the calculation of electricity tariffs, including the average exchange rate and inflation. The Ministry has ruled out an immediate hike, but the ongoing reform of the Electricity Law signals that increases are inevitable over the medium term. At the same time, the natural gas crisis is deepening: the analysts cited by Los Tiempos agree that the exits are difficult, and the candidates who debated at the Universidad Privada de Santa Cruz de la Sierra were unable to articulate a convincing alternative plan that did not rely either on declining gas output or on a lithium industry whose industrialization remains a deferred promise.

The loosening of the exchange rate, which ended fifteen years of a fixed peg, has had immediate effects on the domestic financial architecture. The government has ordered the return of dollar deposits starting July 15 and has normalized the remittance system, while the Banco Central de Bolivia maintains that episodes of foreign-currency liquidity restrictions will not recur. The BCB also faces judicial scrutiny: banking executives and the former Economy Minister under Luis Arce's government will be summoned in connection with the BCB case, a proceeding that combines institutional accountability with an evident element of political score-settling.

Bolivia's 37-position drop in the World Index of Economic Freedom, published this week, has not surprised the private sector, which has been warning for months about five factors aggravating the crisis: regulatory uncertainty, contraction of productive credit, an expanding illicit economy, deteriorated logistics infrastructure, and fiscal pressure on formal industry. The episode involving Bolivian timber incinerated in Chile, which has triggered formal complaints from importers in the United States and Mexico, precisely illustrates the reputational cost of institutional weakness: the Instituto Boliviano de Comercio Exterior described the destruction of the evidence as an act that directly damages the country's export position, while the case threatens to affect supply contracts in high-value markets.

The visit of Chilean President José Antonio Kast and the bilateral agenda between Presidents Paz and Kast have put integration and trade challenges on the table, including the possibility of reactivating the Arica–La Paz railway to boost Bolivian cargo capacity toward the Pacific — a project the government is positioning as a connectivity lever but one that requires investment for which there is no confirmed financing today.

What to watch closely in the coming weeks is the IMF's first formal review, which will determine whether the reported fiscal surplus meets the agreed targets and unlocks the second disbursement. Any deviation in that process, combined with an escalation of mobilizations by the pro-Evo faction over the diesel subsidy, could rapidly erode the confidence the government has patiently built with international markets since the sovereign bond issuance. The flexible exchange rate, meanwhile, will remain the variable most closely watched by savers, importers and the banking system as a whole.
