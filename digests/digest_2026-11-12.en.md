# Argentina: Economic Pulse — 2026-11-12

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-11-12*

Argentina's markets have an unusual focal point today: for the first time since August, a Treasury auction came in expansive, releasing 1.7 trillion pesos into the financial system, and the economic team is celebrating it as a signal that banks would rather lend to the private sector than roll over sovereign debt. It is an inversion of logic that, if confirmed, could mean the return of the so-called "Anker point" — the moment when private credit begins to crowd out fiscal absorption from the system — and would open a new chapter in the most urgent debate in the Argentine economy: whether or not there are enough pesos in the system for activity to rebound ahead of the 2027 presidential elections.

Wednesday's auction is the best prism through which to read that debate. The Finance Secretariat received bids for 11.6 trillion pesos but accepted only 8.87 trillion, implying an official rollover of 103.07% if the maturities of the CER TX26 and TX28 bonds are excluded. But if those payments — which were settled the same day — are included, the rollover drops to 84%, releasing 1.7 trillion pesos to the market. Puente noted that the government had to pay 100 basis points above secondary-market quotes to place its fixed-rate instruments, a sign that liquidity has a price. From the Economy Ministry, Minister Luis Caputo interpreted the result as evidence that the decision not to expand the monetary base belongs to the market, not the government: if banks don't want to roll over Treasury bonds because they would rather lend to firms and families, that is not a problem but a signal of credit maturation. The opposing reading, strongly held by consultancies such as MacroView — the firm of Carlos Melconian and Rodolfo Santangelo — is that the economy needs an additional 40 to 50 trillion pesos in constant-value terms to return to recent activity levels, and that the window to inject them without triggering exchange-rate or inflationary pressure is closing fast.

Against that backdrop, the October inflation print that INDEC releases this Thursday takes on added political significance. Private estimates range between 1.8% and 2.1%, essentially unchanged from September's 1.9%. Fundación Libertad y Progreso and Equilibra pegged it at 1.8%; other consultancies come in closer to the 2% threshold. If the official figure breaches that mark, the narrative of sustained disinflation that the government needs to sustain ahead of the electoral cycle will be weakened. Seasonal prices — vegetables in particular — and public transport pushed higher, while core inflation remains contained. Early November readings, by contrast, are more benign: consultancy LCG reported a 0.2% drop in food and beverages during the first week of the month, though part of the move is attributed to Cyber Monday discounts.

Markets this week operated under the atypical condition of a U.S. holiday for Veterans Day, which deprived local investors of key fixed-income references. With U.S. Treasuries sidelined, JP Morgan's country risk spread could not be measured precisely, though estimates place it below 850 basis points, recovering from the 939 peak marked the previous Friday. Sovereign dollar bonds rose as much as nearly 3% at the long end on Wednesday's session. The Merval, by contrast, posted a fourth straight loss, retreating 0.6% in pesos to close at 83,576 points, with Argentine assets at their lowest in dollars since October 24. YPF, whose ADR trades on the New York Stock Exchange, rose 0.2% to USD 37.34; Grupo Galicia lost 0.5% and Mercado Libre advanced 1.4%. Morgan Stanley published a report this week arguing that the sell-off in Argentine debt "has gone too far," since bonds already price in a 41% to 52% probability of an adverse post-electoral scenario, up from 11%-20% three months ago. Fernando Sedano, the bank's executive director for Latin America, was more measured at the Alaya Summit: he acknowledged that the worst is probably over, but warned that the financing program designed with country risk at 400 basis points "has become somewhat obsolete" at 850, closing off access to the dollar-denominated Argentine-law debt the government had planned to issue.

The external context is not helping either. October inflation in the United States came in at 0.4% month-on-month, one tenth above expectations, and cemented bets that the Federal Reserve will raise rates by 50 basis points in December, taking them to the 4.25%-4.50% range. The dollar strengthened globally and 10-year Treasuries were placed at 5.02% in the latest auction, a sign that sovereign funding costs across the developed world keep climbing. For Argentina, which depends on global financial conditions not deteriorating further before the elections, the scenario is uncomfortable. The official exchange rate eased slightly — the wholesale rate closed at 1,544 pesos, 0.1% lower than the previous session — and the Banco Central bought USD 29 million on the session, bringing November's total to USD 80 million and the year's accumulation to USD 16.043 billion. Reserves, however, fell by USD 312 million on an interest payment to the IMF and stood at USD 49.330 billion.

The tension between the strength of the export sector and the weakness of manufacturing defines the real-economy landscape. Agro-industrial exports marked an all-time record in the September-October bimester, with nearly 2 million tons of wheat shipped, and mining has accumulated USD 6.723 billion in exports in the first nine months of the year, 59% more than the same period of 2025 and already above total 2025 exports. Lithium led sector growth with a 173% year-on-year increase. Vaca Muerta produced 700,000 barrels per day in September, 28% more than a year earlier, and oil companies accumulated an additional USD 5.165 billion in revenue between January and August. But manufacturing is running an average 1.8% year-on-year decline so far this year, according to INDEC, and the loss of almost 290,000 formal jobs since December 2023 — a third of which correspond to industry — makes employment the most politically sensitive variable. Stanley Black & Decker confirmed this week the closure of its abrasive disc plant in Justo Daract, San Luis, laying off 61 workers and citing loss of competitiveness against imports. In Tierra del Fuego, more than 1,000 jobs in the air conditioning sector are at risk starting December 15 under a new regulation that allows the six local manufacturers — Newsan, BGH, Radio Victoria, Solnik, Midea/Carrier and Electrofueguina — to reduce the local content of their equipment. The penetration of Chinese electric vehicles into Brazil, which tripled in value to USD 6.260 billion in the first nine months, is simultaneously squeezing Argentine auto exports to Brazil — down 7.8% year-to-date — and Brazilian auto exports to Argentina, which fell 18%.

On the microeconomic and regulatory front, the government extended the concessions of five hydroelectric plants through April 30, 2027, while moving forward with new tenders; approved the sole bid presented to concession the Paso de los Libres border complex — the country's second-largest customs point — for at least two decades; and the consortium formed by YPF, Eni and ADNOC's investment arm carried out this week the largest pipeline tender in Argentine energy history, valued at around USD 2 billion, for the trunk gas pipeline of the Argentina LNG project. In parallel, the Comisión Nacional de Valores suspended Atómico 3, the first virtual-asset provider sanctioned in Argentina on possible fraud charges, after detecting that it was offering tokens supposedly backed by lithium reserves in San Juan without accrediting mining concessions in its name.

What to watch in the coming sessions includes Thursday's official inflation print, which will set the tone for the monetary debate; the evolution of private credit as evidence of whether the "Anker point" is real or illusory; country-risk dynamics as markets digest the prospect of higher U.S. rates; and the progress of RIGI tenders, whose capacity to generate hard currency in the short term — with barely two jobs per million dollars invested and disbursements stretching out to 2056 — will continue to be questioned by those who see in the scheme a long-horizon bet incompatible with the government's electoral urgencies.
