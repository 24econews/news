# Argentina: The Economic Pulse — 2026-11-19

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-11-19*

The contado con liquidación breaking through $1,700, an FX gap that widened to 10% for the first time in over a year, and country risk hovering around 850 basis points: Thursday's session condenses, in just a handful of indicators, the core tension running through Argentina's economy as 2026 draws to a close. This is not a one-off crisis but rather the crystallization of several simultaneous pressures the market had been processing separately, now converging into an unmistakable signal of distrust.

The immediate trigger came from abroad. The depreciation of the Brazilian real, which has lost 18% since Lula da Silva's re-election and closed last week near 5.89 per dollar, is pressuring Argentina's real exchange rate at a particularly delicate moment. Brazil is the country's main trading partner, and when the real weakens, Argentine competitiveness erodes by contagion, regardless of what the local central bank does. Add to that the global strengthening of the U.S. dollar, up 6.42% year-to-date and at its highest level since February 2025, driven by expectations of tighter Federal Reserve policy, whose October minutes left the door open to a rate hike of up to 50 basis points in December. The VIX, Wall Street's fear gauge, jumped 11% to 25.45, dangerously approaching the 30 threshold that historically sets off alarm bells among institutional investors.

But external factors don't tell the whole story. Local FX pressure also reflects something more structural: volume traded in the wholesale market fell 40% versus the same point in October, an unmistakable sign that dollar supply is retreating. The official exchange rate, up just 7.2% year-to-date against inflation of 26.3%, implies an accumulated FX lag that the market is pricing in more aggressively as the election year approaches. Economy Minister Luis Caputo took to the stage to push back on that reading at the Somos Pymes event, urging business owners to "change their mindset" and ruling out any FX jump heading into 2027, arguing that the trade surplus — which went from $8 billion to $30 billion in a year — guarantees dollar availability. The central bank, for its part, has been buying dollars, though at a pace that halved between August and October compared with the average of the first seven months of the year.

The underlying problem, however, is not the FX moment but the financial horizon of 2027. A report from IERAL at Fundación Mediterránea, authored by Jorge Vasconcelos, estimated that the Treasury will face debt maturities of between $28 and $29 billion next year, in addition to transferring $5.6 billion net to the IMF. Given currently available funding sources, the shortfall is estimated at $17 billion. The government is said to have missed at least two or three windows during 2026 — when country risk approached 400 points — to tap international credit markets, following Ecuador's path. With the JP Morgan spread above 848 points, that option looks improbable today without a major catalyst.

In this context, the suspension of the Primarias Abiertas Simultáneas y Obligatorias (PASO) for 2027 has become the only political asset the market is willing to price positively. The bill advanced in Senate committee and is expected to reach the floor next week. Analysts and international funds agree in cataloguing it as a "positive event" for Argentine assets: it removes the possibility of a political shock in August that has historically disrupted FX and financial expectations, as happened in 2019 and 2023.

The divide between stagnation and long-term investment is clearly on display in the gap between sectors. While Neuquén set an all-time record by collecting $1.441 billion in oil royalties between January and September — already surpassing the 2025 total with three months to go — and while energy and mining project combined export contributions of $21 billion this year according to the sector's secretaries, manufacturing industry is down 12% in the year versus 2022, with the sectors most exposed to imports retreating by as much as 19%. The textile sector has lost 29,000 jobs since December 2023. The delinquency rate among micro-SMEs reaches 17.6%, four times higher than the broader corporate sector, and gastronomy leads the sectoral ranking with 29.2% of its loan book in distressed status. More than 18,000 micro-SMEs are in bank default. According to a BDO report prepared for the NGO Lógica, Argentina has the heaviest tax burden on personal loans among 30 economies analyzed: taxes make credit 39% more expensive, and it is the only country in the world that simultaneously applies VAT, Ingresos Brutos, municipal fees and stamp tax on a single loan.

Against that backdrop, Minister Caputo unveiled a payment chain scheme to finance SME suppliers through corporate credit from large RIGI companies, and the Unión Industrial Argentina formally submitted to Treasury Secretary Carlos Guberman a Federal Fiscal Pact proposing cuts to payroll contributions, Ganancias and Ingresos Brutos. The City of Buenos Aires launched the RIMI CABA, with a quota of 300 billion pesos and a 25% tax credit on SME investments. These are signs that pressure from industry is beginning to generate responses, though the magnitude of the structural problems — the tax burden, the cost of credit, import competition — far exceeds the speed of any incremental response.

Next week will be the moment to watch three things: whether the Senate approves the suspension of the PASO and the market responds with country-risk compression; whether the BCRA manages to sustain its pace of reserve accumulation in a context of reduced dollar supply; and whether the government makes progress in rolling over the remaining dollar-linked note maturing at month-end — after swapping 60% this week — as a signal that the short-term financial program remains under control.
