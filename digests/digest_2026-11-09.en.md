# Argentina: The Economic Pulse — 2026-11-09

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-11-09*

Argentina's country risk brushed 940 basis points this week — its highest level since the October 2025 midterm elections — and yet the official dollar closed Friday at $1,570, with a gap of barely 8% against the parallel rate. That disconnect between the sovereign thermometer and the FX market is the most significant feature of Argentina's current moment: for the first time in recent memory, the government has deliberately insulated the institutional investor alarm signal from transmitting to the real economy via the exchange rate, though the cost of that insulation is increasingly visible in other variables.

The strategy is explicit. As the consultancy Outlier describes it, the economic team intervened simultaneously in the Mercado Libre de Cambios, the futures market and the financial dollars to turn the exchange rate into a managed variable, immune to political noise. The contado con liquidación closed around $1,679 and the blue at $1,555 — levels that in previous episodes of stress would have implied an imminent devaluation. Not this time. What the market did instead was punish sovereign debt: dollar bonds have fallen roughly 20% from their July highs, when country risk was trading at 400 basis points. In four months, the JP Morgan indicator has more than doubled. EcoAnalytics notes that the central bank is maintaining an implicit crawling peg of about 1% monthly, below inflation, in a scheme that preserves the FX anchor but accelerates the deterioration of the real exchange rate.

The external backdrop made the picture materially worse. The US midterm elections, with a Democratic victory wresting control of both chambers from Donald Trump, unleashed a storm on Wall Street that on Thursday wiped out 5.5% of the Nasdaq and 4.4% of the S&P 500. The session was described by several analysts as the worst of the year for global markets: fears of stricter regulation on artificial intelligence, combined with signals that Republican tax cuts will face legislative resistance, triggered a massive tech sell-off that dragged down every risk asset. Brent crude posted its steepest weekly drop since the start of the pandemic, tumbling 16% to $98 a barrel on expectations of a thaw in the conflict with Iran. For Argentina — which designed its export program on the premise of high energy prices — the oil decline is an ambiguous signal: it eases the domestic inflation front but squeezes revenues from Vaca Muerta projects. The YPF ADR fell 17.6% on the week, to $38.70, while Vista Energy shed 18.2%. The MSCI Merval retreated 8% in pesos and 9% in dollars measured at the CCL.

Behind those numbers lies a political dispute that the market is pricing with increasing literalness. The implicit probability of Javier Milei's reelection, according to bond prices, fell to 45% — what economist Fernando Marull described as "an overreaction" relative to polling, which gives the ruling party close to a 50% chance. Economy Minister Luis Caputo, who on Friday appeared before agricultural business leaders gathered for the 160th anniversary of the Sociedad Rural Argentina, dismissed the notion that the turbulence reflects problems with the model and pinned the cause on investor fear of a potential political alternation. "What some people are afraid of is that President Milei won't be there," he said. Milei himself doubled down from the same stage: loosening monetary policy would be "pouring fuel on the fire," and the government is not considering any fiscal stimulus. The monetary base has fallen 13.3% in real terms over the past year, and according to a Quantum report, the central bank "remains focused on preventing monetary surpluses from pressuring the exchange rate and inflation." To sustain that anchor, the Treasury injected close to $2 trillion in long-dated dual bonds — an intervention detected by 1816 — aimed at holding a ceiling on peso instrument rates.

The cracks in the growth model, however, are increasingly hard to ignore. The August EMAE came in 2.4% below the February 2025 peak, and if that level holds, the economy could end the year with a 0.2% contraction, leaving a negative carryover into 2027. Deputy Economy Minister José Luis Daza admitted at the ABECEB Forum that "I was expecting higher growth." SME retail sales fell 1.6% year-on-year in October according to CAME, with a negative 2% cumulative figure for the year to date. Manufacturing contracted 1.8% year-on-year in September, with ten of sixteen divisions in the red. The machinery and equipment segment collapsed 26.8%, dragged down by a 45.4% drop in agricultural machinery manufacturing, where firms such as Pauny, Metalfor and Vassalli are carrying multimillion-dollar debts and reduced output against imported-equipment competition. Poverty, according to private calculations based on INDEC microdata, reached 34.4% of the population in the second quarter, affecting more than 16.4 million people.

The counterpoint to that gloomy picture is a dollar boom that the central bank is converting into reserves with surprising efficiency. The BCRA has already accumulated more than $16 billion in purchases during 2026, exceeding the annual target agreed with the IMF by more than 60%, with the official estimate of closing the year near $20 billion. The key is an export sector growing at two speeds: energy, mining and agriculture are posting record output while the industry oriented to the domestic market contracts. BCRA Vice President Vladimir Werning defended that dynamic at the same ABECEB Forum, arguing that "the trickle-down effect" from export sectors will eventually impart dynamism to the rest of the economy.

Meanwhile, the construction of the great export skeleton is advancing at its own pace. The Argentina LNG consortium — comprising YPF, Italy's Eni and XRG, the investment arm of ADNOC — on Monday launched the largest pipeline tender in Argentine energy history, a $2 billion bid process to supply pipe for a 48-inch gas pipeline that will connect Vaca Muerta with the Golfo San Matías. Tenaris, part of Grupo Techint, is competing for both pipelines and is concentrating its biggest bet on the 24-inch pipeline. In parallel, the Southern Energy consortium — which includes Pan American Energy, YPF, Pampa Energía, Harbour Energy and Golar LNG — has already begun welding along the pipeline route, with Río Negro Governor Alberto Weretilneck as witness. The final investment decision for Argentina LNG is scheduled for November 30 at the YPF Tower. The 27 projects approved under the RIGI total commitments of $76.173 billion, though 67% of disbursements will extend out to 2035 and the latest reach 2056. Imports under the regime hit a monthly record high of $140 million in October, reopening the debate over how much of that demand transfers to local industry: according to CIPIBIC, two-thirds of external purchases correspond to tariff positions with available domestic production.

What is missing from that framework is precisely what the market is penalizing: political certainty that the model will survive the electoral cycle. Democratic control of the US Congress adds a first-order geopolitical complication. AmCham Argentina warned this week that "the willingness to use the IMF or other instruments to come to the rescue diminishes when there is a divided government." The Argentine Treasury faces $20 billion in net debt maturities in 2027, without having regained access to international markets at sustainable rates. Economist Martín Redrado, in an interview with La Nación, spoke directly of "material fatigue" in the program and stressed that "the financial program the economic team presented in July is now obsolete." Market bets and government bets are, for the first time in months, clearly divorced. What gets settled in the coming weeks — the awarding of the Argentina LNG pipeline, the October inflation print that INDEC will publish mid-month, and the dynamics of the peso curve after the Treasury's intervention — will determine whether that gap closes with confidence or widens under more pressure on sovereign bonds.
