# Argentina: The Economic Pulse — 2026-10-15

On Wednesday, October 14, Argentina woke up caught between two contradictory forces that precisely define its current economic dilemma: abroad, a hotter-than-expected US inflation reading —3.7% year-on-year in September, driven by rising fuel prices against the backdrop of the conflict with Iran— reshaped expectations around the Federal Reserve and pushed Argentina's country risk back above 600 basis points; at home, the Central Bank bought USD 291 million in a single session, its largest purchase since late July, and the Treasury closed a peso-denominated debt auction with a 136% rollover rate. The paradox is striking: local fundamentals show relative strength that international markets simply refuse to acknowledge.

The index compiled by JP Morgan climbed 14 units to 611 basis points, dragging the S&P Merval down 1.2% to 2,835,843 points and hitting ADRs of Argentine companies, which closed broadly lower, with Satellogic as the worst performer, down 9.3%, followed by Bioceres at -7.7% and Cresud at -4.4%. The only exceptions were Vista Energy, up 1.4%, and YPF, which advanced 0.9% to USD 52.25 on the very same crude oil rally that is complicating the rest of the global economy: Brent held above USD 106 per barrel. The sovereign bond market —Bonares and Globales— lost an average of 0.3%, and what was declared earlier this year as a key objective of the economic team, Argentina's return to voluntary international debt markets, now looks like an expectation that will have to be postponed indefinitely.

In Bangkok, where the IMF and World Bank Annual Meetings are being held, Minister Luis Caputo rehearsed the financial strength narrative before investors convened by JPMorgan. He assured them that the 2027 program would show a USD 2 billion surplus even in the scenario where the Bonares maturities cannot be refinanced, and that if needed, the currency swap with China could be activated. IMF Managing Director Kristalina Georgieva described Argentina's fiscal discipline as "exemplary in the world, despite global shocks," though that praise coexisted with the growth projection cut the same institution just announced for Argentina: the October WEO put it at 2.1% for 2026, down from the 3.5% projected in April. The third review of the Extended Fund Facility program, whose approval would unlock a pending disbursement of approximately USD 869 million, remains open. Technical teams continue working, but with no closing date in sight.

The economic team's response to the inability to place the Bonar 2029 —whose yield demanded by the market is prohibitive— is a pragmatic reconfiguration of the financial program: more dollars via BCRA purchases, more peso financing, and the use of the Treasury auction surplus —which captured an additional ARS 1.75 trillion over maturities, with the LECAP maturing in January 2027 as the star instrument— as a precautionary buffer against the ARS 95 trillion that will need to be rolled over by year-end. The Central Bank has accumulated USD 15.301 billion in purchases during 2026, well above the annual target of USD 10 billion agreed with the IMF. Gross reserves hovered around USD 49.7 billion this week, with the wholesale exchange rate trading at ARS 1,513, 27.8% below the ceiling of the managed floating band.

Nearly 59,000 companies are in irregular payment status according to Fidelitas data, 53% more than twelve months ago. Formal private employment has now contracted for 14 consecutive months, with 265,765 jobs lost since November 2023. The government maintains that the electoral objective is to bring the year-on-year figure to around 20% by mid-2027. These are two signals confirming that international private capital continues to bet on the Argentine energy sector, even as the sovereign debt market remains closed.

### **Telecom Argentina (BYMA: TECO2)**
