# Argentina: Economic Pulse — 2026-11-02

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-11-02*

Argentina's country risk brushed against 800 basis points for the first time since the 2025 midterm elections, but what truly stands out about this session is not the number itself but the combination surrounding it: during the same period in which the JP Morgan indicator hit its year-to-date high, the real multilateral exchange rate reached its lowest levels since May 2025. That paradox—a market demanding sovereign risk rates typical of a country teetering on the fiscal brink, while the peso appreciates in real terms against its trading partners—defines the economic moment Argentina faces as November begins, and distills into a single tension all the dilemmas the government of Javier Milei will need to resolve before the 2027 presidential elections.

The immediate trigger was external. Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva's reelection in Brazil caught off guard investors who had bet on a liberal victory, and the response was immediate: the real fell nearly 9% from the runoff result, the Bovespa racked up a weekly decline of 18.2%, and the EWZ ETF that tracks Brazilian stocks in New York shed more than 20% at one point. The contagion onto Argentine assets was disproportionate relative to other emerging markets. According to data from Econviews, since the Federal Reserve meeting on September 16, Argentine risk has accumulated 240 basis points of increase, compared to around 70 in a basket of countries with similar ratings—Ecuador, Egypt, El Salvador, Ghana, Pakistan, and Sri Lanka—meaning a rise 3.4 times that of its peers. In October, the divergence was even more pronounced: local risk climbed nearly six times what the Latin American average did. All fourteen Argentine ADRs closed lower on the Monday following the runoff, the MSCI Merval in dollars shed 4.4% in a single session, Global bonds lost 2.5% that day, and the Buenos Aires index ended the week with an accumulated drop of 10.6% measured in contado con liquidación pesos, with names like Grupo Galicia falling 15% and Edenor 17.2%.

Consultancy 1816, one of the most closely followed in the City of Buenos Aires, described the situation as "peculiar": the country reached its highest risk level since the 2025 elections on the same day the real exchange rate touched eighteen-month lows. Central Bank president Santiago Bausili celebrated that the wholesale dollar rose a mere 1% during the entire week of the Brazilian shock—closing October at $1,534 in the wholesale segment—and presented it as proof of the exchange rate regime's resilience. The CCL dollar, however, approached $1,700 and the exchange rate gap climbed above 7%, its highest level since before the Brazilian elections. That differential, modest in historical terms but widening, is the signal that worries the market most heading into 2027.

The external context compounds domestic vulnerabilities that were already in place. The economy faces what several analysts call a technical recession: the August EMAE barely advanced 0.1% in seasonally adjusted terms, insufficient to offset July's collapse, and private projections point to growth of just 1% for 2026, in line with the latest IMF revision, which cut its estimate from 3.5% to 2.1%. Access to the voluntary debt market remains closed—the government suspended placements of the Bonar 2029 in the face of rates it is unwilling to validate—and the 2027 financing program is already raising doubts among investors, although the IMF technical staff approved the third review of the program, paving the way for a disbursement of roughly $900 million expected during the first week of December. This Monday the Treasury must meet an $800 million maturity with the institution, whose payment mechanism—whether it will tap its own dollars or buy foreign currency from the BCRA—had not been confirmed as of this edition's close.

Against that backdrop, the economic team is arranging loans with multilateral guarantees worth an additional $1 billion before year-end, according to the IMF itself in its review statement. The BCRA, for its part, closed October with net purchases of $1.395 billion—its best showing since July—and gross reserves rose to $48.562 billion, an increase of $7.397 billion so far this year. Those figures coexist, paradoxically, with voices such as that of former minister Martín Redrado, who warned at the Argentina Fintech Forum that in 2027 "we are going to have exchange rate tension" and noted that some 200 trillion pesos in Treasury instruments mature before the elections, potential fuel for a run in the event of a loss of confidence.

The real economy displays this dichotomy with striking clarity. YPF, whose ADRs trade on the New York Stock Exchange, reported the third-highest quarterly result in its history—operating profits of $2.503 billion in the third quarter—and its CEO Horacio Marín promised Wall Street investors they will be "very surprised" by Vaca Muerta's production in December 2027, when just one day before the conference call production had set a record of 252,330 barrels. Simultaneously, the company submitted the Bajo del Toro project to RIGI for $8.729 billion, the first of three RIGI applications totaling $44 billion gross over 26 years. Natural gas production also hit historic highs in 2026, averaging 147.7 million cubic meters per day between January and September, according to the Rosario Board of Trade. But while Vaca Muerta thrives, Pauny—a Córdoba-based tractor manufacturer that claims to build six out of every ten units circulating in the country—has accumulated 146 bounced checks worth more than $1 million and operates three days a week paying only 70% of wages. Plantium and Gentec, a well-known agricultural technology firm, shut its Santa Fe plant and laid off 84 workers. Manufacturing industry output is down 12% versus 2022, and Gross Fixed Capital Formation fell 12% year-on-year in the first quarter and 11% in the second.

The government is attempting to address these gaps with several simultaneous initiatives. Vice Minister of Economy José Luis Daza confirmed they are evaluating extending the maturities of FGS funds for mortgage loans beyond five years and advancing with international organization credit lines. The IDB approved a $200 million investment guarantee to expand electricity transmission capacity in the AMBA region and the Patagonian corridor. A new tender for national highways is in preparation, with a scheme that for the first time will allow bidders beyond current concessionaires. On the fiscal front, November kicks off with adjustments to nearly all regulated services: electricity rises between 2.94% and 3.18% depending on the distributor, gas between 2.95% and 3.65%, Buenos Aires city and provincial transport 3.9%, although the 104 national bus lines keep their fare frozen at $742.81 thanks to a subsidy increase decided in order not to pressure inflation. Private consultancies project October inflation of between 1.8% and 2%, unable to break through the floor the government needs in order to consolidate its disinflation narrative ahead of the electoral cycle.

What lies ahead will set the market's temperature in the coming weeks. On Tuesday, November 3, the U.S. midterm elections take place, and their outcome will have direct consequences for Argentina: a Democratic Congress could politically complicate—though not technically block—Washington's financial backing via the Exchange Stabilization Fund. The IMF Executive Board's approval of the $900 million disbursement, expected in December, and the evolution of country risk in the coming sessions will be the most sensitive thermometers of a market that, as economist Sebastián Galiani observed, understands that "this transformation needs time and continuity," but demands signals that this time will not run out before the ballot boxes speak again.
