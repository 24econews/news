# Mercosur Weekly Briefing — Week of July 27–30, 2026

## The Big Picture

The dominant theme of this week across Mercosur was not growth — it was the collision between structural fragility and the appearance of financial credibility. Bolivia signed a staff-level IMF agreement for $1.9 billion while its diesel supply chain was simultaneously collapsing, with 80% of interdepartmental buses grounded and agribusiness threatening productive paralysis. Uruguay placed a $1.25 billion sovereign bond at sub-7% rates in local currency amid a fiscal debate in which the independent Consejo Fiscal Autónomo publicly accused the government of overstating growth projections. Paraguay attracted IMF praise and fresh World Bank financing while private-sector voices warned that its signature competitive advantage — cheap, abundant electricity — will be exhausted in three years without urgent reform. In each case, the headline number — the bond, the rating, the IMF seal — masked a deeper structural tension that investors ignoring the fine print will eventually be forced to confront.

The second theme was the widening divergence between commodity windfalls and domestic economic health. Argentina's crude oil revenues surged 47.7% in the first half, making Vaca Muerta the country's top export for the first time — yet manufacturing contracted 3% in the same period and new-car sales are tracking a 28–30% year-on-year plunge in July. Chile's Pucobre posted a 79% rise in half-year earnings on copper price gains, while Colbún's profits collapsed 53% as the energy sector absorbed the costs of decarbonization and pipeline disruptions. Brazil's real estate credit grew 23% and in-home food prices posted their sharpest July decline since 2010 — against a backdrop of Santander Brasil's ROE falling to 12.5%, CAGED registering its weakest June job creation since 2020, and agribusiness insolvencies running 33% ahead of last year's pace. Commodity tailwinds are masking credit deterioration, investment contraction, and employment stress in ways that aggregate GDP figures will not fully capture until it is late to reposition.

The third thread — and the one most relevant for global fixed-income investors specifically — is the region's collective exposure to the Middle East conflict and U.S. monetary policy simultaneously. The Federal Reserve's hold, accompanied by a dissent toward tightening and a spike in 30-year Treasury yields, hit emerging market assets across the board: Argentine sovereign bonds fell an average of 0.3% and country risk climbed to 445 basis points, while Chile's IPSA moved inversely to Wall Street precisely because Santiago read the Fed pause as a relative positive for the region. Meanwhile, Brent oscillating between $82 and $90 after the resumption of U.S.-Iran hostilities is compressing margins for Bolivia's YPFB, straining Chile's Mepco fuel-subsidy mechanism, and complicating Brazil's fragile balance between food-price relief and energy cost pressure. No Mercosur economy is insulated from either vector, and the combination of elevated U.S. rates and elevated oil is, historically, a stress test this region has repeatedly failed.

## Country by Country

### 🇧🇷 Brazil
Santander Brasil's second-quarter net income of R$3 billion — down 17.6% year-on-year and roughly 12% below consensus — opened earnings season with a result that JPMorgan responded to by downgrading the stock and cutting its price target from R$36 to R$30, sending SANB11 units down more than 7% in the session. The result is more than a single-bank story: with private-sector delinquency entrenched, agribusiness insolvencies up 33% year-on-year in Q1, and CAGED recording only 145,000 new formal jobs in June — the weakest reading for the month since 2020 — the financial system is absorbing simultaneous shocks from the real economy. The one genuine bright spot is Finance Minister Durigan's reported proposal to tighten the spending growth cap from 2.5% to 1.5%, which would represent the Lula government's most significant fiscal pivot since the framework was created — if it survives the political pressures of an election year.

### 🇦🇷 Argentina
President Milei's nationally televised announcement of a Central Bank charter reform — prohibiting monetary financing of the Treasury, requiring a two-thirds congressional majority to remove BCRA authorities, and introducing criminal penalties for violations — is the most consequential institutional signal of his presidency since dollarization was ruled out. The proposal arrives as gross international reserves hit $49.2 billion, the highest since July 7, and a Treasury auction achieved a 144.5% rollover, including R$4.72 trillion in a new dual bond that captures surging demand for exchange-rate hedging ahead of the 2027 electoral cycle. Yet the real economy continues to bifurcate sharply: agricultural and energy exports are at historic highs, Vaca Muerta has attracted Robeco's first Argentine equity purchase in nearly a decade, and IMF Managing Director Georgieva personally visited Loma Campana — while manufacturing is 10% below 2022–23 levels, gross investment contracted 7.6% in H1, and nearly three million Argentines are in arrears with digital wallets.

### 🇨🇱 Chile
The debate over Codelco's future dominated the week with unusual intensity after the Partido Republicano proposed privatization — a suggestion the government moved quickly to reject, though Finance Minister Quiroz left the door open to private partnerships and capital injections. The conversation is happening at the worst possible moment for the company: new CEO Jorge Gómez is requesting a unanimous board confidence vote to operate through a deep restructuring phase, former Finance Minister Felipe Larraín warned that political timing would depress any sale price, and UCLA's Sebastian Edwards argued the miner "needs major surgery" and must rethink its SQM arrangement. Meanwhile, Colbún's half-year profit collapsed 53% to its lowest in five years, gasoline has accumulated a Cl$229.8 per liter increase since March as the Mepco strains under oil-price pressure, and Masisa doubled its half-year losses attributing the deterioration directly to Trump tariff-driven demand destruction in its key export markets.

### 🇺🇾 Uruguay
The government's $1.25 billion dual-tranche sovereign bond — placed at below 7% on the local-currency portion with demand more than double expectations — was the week's clearest signal that Uruguay's fiscal credibility remains intact in international markets even as the domestic picture grows more complicated. Economy Minister Oddone simultaneously acknowledged a "fairly high probability" that 2026 growth projections will be revised downward from an already-modest base: the economy closed 2025 at 1.8% growth, opened 2026 at 0.8–0.9% in Q1, and the World Bank has cut its full-year forecast to 1.6%. The government is betting on green hydrogen, data centers — with Google confirmed as evaluating a presence in the country — and a new pulp mill to bridge the gap between the growth it has promised and the growth its economy is actually delivering.

### 🇵🇾 Paraguay
The headline number — 5.6% year-on-year growth through May, nearly double the regional average — is real, but private-sector voices this week issued a warning that should reframe how investors read it: the energy abundance that has been Paraguay's primary investment pitch will be exhausted in three years if ANDE is not reformed and the sector opened. The utility's mounting losses, the absence of a defined tariff adjustment, and demand growth on a trajectory to outstrip capacity are not distant risks — they are present-tense constraints on the investment narrative. Against that backdrop, the Ministry of Economy and Finance returned to the Bolsa de Valores de Asunción with a 360.5 billion guaraní Treasury bond placement and simultaneously opened a window for external debt issuance, as public debt has grown $1.343 billion in five months and interest servicing costs have risen between 12.9% and 16.8%.

### 🇧🇴 Bolivia
The IMF's staff-level agreement for a $1.9 billion, 36-month program — potentially expandable to $2.8 billion including contingent resources — is a lifeline for an economy whose international reserves have fallen more than $10 billion from their 2014 peak and whose external debt now totals a record $14.358 billion, with 90% of newly contracted credit financing operating expenditure rather than investment. The program's credibility, however, faces an immediate test: the boliviano is already trading at Bs 11.80 per dollar despite the flexible exchange rate introduced a month ago, Minister Espinoza's pledge to bring it below Bs 11 is so far unmatched by market conviction, and the diesel shortage grounding 80% of interdepartmental buses and threatening agribusiness paralysis is a daily reminder that the fiscal reform the IMF requires and the subsidy regime the government maintains are, for now, irreconcilable. Parliamentary ratification — by no means guaranteed — is the first gate.

## Corporate Watch of the Week

**Ambev (ABEV3 / NYSE ADR)** — Brazil's brewing giant posted Q2 net profit of R$3.47 billion, up 24.5% year-on-year on the back of World Cup-driven volumes, but missed revenue and EBITDA estimates from JPMorgan, Itaú BBA, and XP — a result that raises a pointed question about how much of the beverage sector's recent optimism was priced around tournament-driven demand that has now passed.

**Globant (NYSE: GLOB)** — The Argentine technology company accumulated a near-25% gain over five sessions, closing the week at $38.14, in a rally the market attributed to its repositioning around generative artificial intelligence. The move is notable precisely because it came as broader Wall Street tech earnings — with the exception of Microsoft — disappointed, suggesting investors are specifically rewarding Latin American tech exposure with credible AI narratives at a moment of elevated selectivity.

**Enel Américas (Santiago Stock Exchange / international ADR)** — The pan-regional utility posted H1 earnings of $512 million, up 18.5%, driven by Brazil and Colombia where currency appreciation and tariff indexation more than offset Argentine weakness. The result stands in sharp contrast to domestic Chilean peer Colbún's 53% profit collapse, illustrating how geographic diversification within the region is functioning as a genuine earnings buffer in the current environment.

## What to Watch Next Week

- **Bolivia's parliamentary ratification of the IMF program** is the single highest-stakes near-term event in the region. A delay or rejection would immediately reignite pressure on the boliviano and test whether the government can hold the flexible exchange rate without the program's institutional backstop. Watch the political arithmetic in La Paz closely.

- **Brazil's PNAD Contínua labor market data** will either validate or undercut the government's employment resilience narrative after CAGED's weakest June since 2020. Combined with the continuation of Q2 bank earnings — Itaú, Bradesco, and Banco do Brasil are the names to watch — the week will provide the clearest read yet on whether credit stress is systemic or idiosyncratic to Santander Brasil.

- **Paraguay's Caja Fiscal pension reform vote** and **ANDE's tariff adjustment decision** represent the two domestic policy gates that will determine whether the country's fiscal and investment narratives remain intact heading into H2. A diluted Caja Fiscal reform or a further delay on energy tariffs would be the first concrete signals that structural reform momentum is losing political traction.
