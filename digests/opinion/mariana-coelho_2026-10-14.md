> TITLE: Market Euphoria Masks Brazil's Farm Credit Crisis Regardless of Runoff Winner
> DATE: 2026-10-14

Brazil's October 4 presidential vote handed the agribusiness sector something it rarely gets: a moment of genuine uncertainty dressed up as clarity. Flávio Bolsonaro's first-round lead — 47.03% of valid votes against Lula's 45.16% — sent the Ibovespa to its largest single-day gain since March 2020, pushed the dollar below R$5 for the first time since May, and compressed ten-year yields by 100 basis points in a matter of hours. For farm-sector producers, exporters, and rural creditors watching from Mato Grosso to Rio Grande do Sul, those numbers felt like relief. They should be read more carefully than that.

The market reaction was real, but so is the structural context in which it occurred. Economists polled by Folha de S.Paulo estimate that stabilizing public debt will require a minimum adjustment of R$250 billion over the next four years. Gross government debt already stands at 82.9% of GDP, up 4.2 percentage points between January and August alone. The Focus bulletin released the morning after the election — closed before the result was known — had already revised inflation above 5% and trimmed GDP growth estimates. September's services PMI showed the sharpest contraction in nearly a year. None of that changed when the votes were counted.

What changed was the political pricing of fiscal adjustment. Markets appeared to read Flávio's lead as a higher probability of spending discipline. Whether that discipline actually materializes is a separate question, and agribusiness traders should not confuse the two. The rural credit delinquency data that Valor Econômico published the same week is a useful corrective: the balance of loans more than 90 days past due in agribusiness jumped from R$3.8 billion in July 2023 to R$38.4 billion in April 2026, a tenfold deterioration in under three years, with total problem loans in the sector reaching R$188.8 billion and renegotiated financings up 630%. That is a systemic condition, not a cyclical blip, and it will not be fixed by equity-market enthusiasm.

On the export side, there is more nuance. Soybean processing capacity grew 13.1% year-on-year, according to Abiove, reflecting solid global demand for meal and oil. Green coffee exports hit their highest volume in 20 months in September. These are genuine operational achievements. But meat exports took a concrete hit: revenues fell 15% and volumes dropped 12% in September, penalized simultaneously by Chinese restrictions and suspended European certifications. Development Minister Márcio Rosa reported progress in negotiations with Brussels, yet the impasse remained unresolved. Whatever government takes office in January will inherit a market-access problem in animal protein that no fiscal package resolves overnight.

The real's appreciation after the first round created its own distortion. Export-oriented sectors — mining, pulp, steel — fell sharply in the sessions following the result. Suzano lost nearly 6% and Embraer shed more than 4%. A stronger real reduces the reais-denominated revenue of every commodity exporter, and the rural credit delinquency figures suggest many farm operations are already running on thin margins. A sustained rally in the currency, even if driven by positive fiscal expectations, acts as a headwind for the very sector whose trade surplus has kept Brazil's external accounts afloat.

Now the honest scenario work. If Flávio Bolsonaro wins the October 25 runoff, his team would likely need to deliver a credible version of the fiscal consolidation the market has already priced. Bradesco BBI and JPMorgan both see Petrobras as a vehicle for capital inflows under this scenario, and Bradesco raised its Petrobras price target from R$52 to R$62 by year-end 2027, anticipating divestments and governance improvements. For agribusiness, the operative question is whether a Flávio administration would reduce the cost of rural credit by enabling Selic cuts — Morgan Stanley estimates potential inflows of between R$176 billion and R$945 billion into Brazilian equities depending on reallocation magnitude, which would ease overall financial conditions — or whether fiscal adjustment falls on programs that support smallholders and infrastructure spending that enables farm-to-port logistics. The Mercosur-EU agreement is now signed; execution under either government will determine whether Brazilian beef and soy actually benefit from improved European market access.

If Lula wins the runoff, the scenario is different in emphasis, not in the underlying arithmetic. Planning Minister Bruno Moretti pledged a gradual four-year adjustment, and Valor Econômico reported that Lula was weighing announcing Geraldo Alckmin as Finance Minister to signal fiscal discipline to centrist voters — a move revealing how much the first-round result reshaped the terms of the economic debate. For agriculture, a Lula second term would likely preserve rural social programs and resist cuts to farm-support mechanisms, but the cost of credit would remain elevated if the Selic stays high due to unresolved fiscal credibility concerns. The meat export problem with China and the EU is a trade-policy file, not an ideological one; both governments would face the same technical negotiation.

The UNCTAD growth projection — 1.8% for 2026, 1.5% for 2027 — is a useful anchor against the euphoria. Brazil's agribusiness sector has the export volumes and processing capacity to outperform the broader economy, as it has in recent cycles. But the rural credit delinquency crisis is a systemic risk that predates the election and will outlast it. The runoff matters for the cost and availability of financing, for the pace of Selic normalization, and for the diplomatic bandwidth available to reopen meat markets in Beijing and Brussels. On all three dimensions, the direction of fiscal policy after October 25 is the variable that actually governs farm-sector investment conditions — not the Ibovespa print on the morning after.
