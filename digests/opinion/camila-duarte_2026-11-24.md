> LENS: Social-democratic / pro-redistribution
> TITLE: Growth Numbers Can't Hide Paraguay's Institutional Collapse
> DATE: 2026-11-24

Paraguay's banking crisis isn't a local accident. It's what happens when years of spectacular macro numbers paper over the institutional rot underneath — and when no one with power asks who, exactly, is depositing state money in a bank that shouldn't have been allowed to hold it.

The numbers are now beyond dispute. The collapse of Ueno Bank has committed USD 888 million in public funds, drawn from some of Paraguay's most consequential state entities: the Instituto de Previsión Social, Itaipú Binacional, and Yacyretá. The Banco Central del Paraguay estimates the total fiscal hole could reach USD 1.5 billion — a figure the country's former Deputy Minister of Economy has already labeled the worst fiscal management episode since 2003. And the scandal deepens with each news cycle: prosecutors have ordered the arrest of the BCP's own legal director on suspicion of tampering with reports; the central bank's former head turned himself in to authorities; Itaipú's internal rules explicitly prohibited deposits with institutions less than five years old in the market, a threshold Ueno did not meet, yet Itaipú deposited there anyway; and the new Director General of Itaipú, installed with a mandate to clean house, is himself under journalistic scrutiny for his own prior ties to the bank.

The government has reacted with the preferred tools of administrations that conflate financial solidity with institutional health. The BCP issued emergency liquidity measures. A digital platform was launched to validate depositor refunds. The Chief of Staff guaranteed one hundred percent recovery of public funds, without explaining the mechanics. A new economy minister replaced his predecessor with softer language — "intelligent austerity" in place of "war economy" — as though the problem were one of tone. These are not solutions. They are the administrative scaffolding that gets erected when the structure behind it has already given way.

What the Ueno collapse actually reveals is a governance failure of the first order, one that sits uneasily alongside the macroeconomic narrative Paraguay has spent years building for international audiences. The IMF projects the country as the region's fastest-growing economy for 2026, with activity expanding at 5.5 percent through September. The BCP's international reserves actually rose by USD 600 million during the turbulence. Sovereign bond markets, until recently, treated Paraguay as a credible frontier issuer. None of that is false. But it was never the whole story. The underground economy — documented at 47.1 percent of GDP, with flows of USD 23.5 billion — provides the structural context that made Ueno's rapid rise possible. An entity that should not have been receiving state deposits was receiving them from Itaipú, from IPS, from entities managing the pensions and utilities of ordinary Paraguayans. That is not a supervision failure at the margins. That is a failure of the entire architecture of accountability that separates public money from private risk.

This matters beyond Paraguay's borders because the region has spent the better part of a decade debating fiscal rules, primary surpluses, and credit ratings as though these were the primary guarantors of economic security. They are not. Brazil's current crisis is partly one of fiscal credibility; Argentina's is partly one of debt sustainability. But Paraguay's crisis is something more specific, and in some ways more instructive: it is what happens when growth statistics are permitted to substitute for institutional oversight. When a country can post 4 to 5 percent annual growth for a decade while nearly half its economy operates in the shadows, while pension funds park money in unqualified banks, while the board members of state entities hold equity stakes in the very institutions receiving public deposits — that growth is not a foundation. It is a surface. And surfaces crack.

The workers of micro and small enterprises who held deposits at Ueno — and who are now reporting revenue losses of up to 35 percent, according to trade associations — did not cause this crisis. They are absorbing it. The savers who protested outside the central bank's headquarters demanding information are the same people whose pension contributions were placed, without their knowledge or consent, into an institution that an internal rule said should never have received them. The social cost of institutional failure is never distributed evenly. It concentrates, as it always does, on those with the least capacity to absorb it and the least political leverage to demand accountability.

Paraguay's macro credentials are real. The fiscal adjustment being debated in the Senate is necessary. The international financing secured from the IMF, World Bank, and CAF matters. But none of it resolves the prior question: who was watching, and why weren't they? Until that question is answered with more than arrests and reshuffles, the country's fastest-growth numbers will remain exactly what the Ueno crisis has shown them to be — statistics that describe an economy, not a system capable of protecting the people inside it.
