> TITLE: Fitch sees what Paraguay won't admit: governance failures block investment grade
> DATE: 2026-10-08

Paraguay's investment-grade ceiling isn't a macro problem. It's a governance problem that fintech and digital reform can't paper over — and Fitch knows it.

That's the inconvenient truth sitting at the center of Paraguay's economic story this week, and it's one that deserves more rigorous scrutiny than it's getting from the government in Asunción. The guaraní became Latin America's best-performing currency. Economic activity grew 5.6% through July. The World Bank projects 4.7% expansion for the full year. And yet Fitch Ratings, for what feels like the hundredth time, looked at those numbers and declined to hand Paraguay investment grade, maintaining the BB+ rating with a positive outlook that never seems to become anything more. President Santiago Peña's response — "the rating agencies take their time" — is the kind of line that sounds serene but reads as evasion. Fitch isn't slow. It's reading something the official narrative doesn't want to acknowledge.

What the rating agency is reading, former Finance Minister Benigno López spelled out with unusual candor: Fitch doesn't evaluate macroeconomic aggregates in isolation. It evaluates institutional quality. And on that dimension, Paraguay's week was not encouraging. Former minister Carlos Borda identified the trilogy holding back the upgrade — debt, deficit, and corruption — while the Unión Industrial Paraguaya delivered its own list of pending structural reforms. The fiscal math carries its own warning signs: wages, pensions, and debt service already consume close to 70% of public financing, leaving minimal room for productive investment. Customs revenue fell 9.1% year-to-date, undermining the new Dirección Nacional de Ingresos Tributarios's stated goal of raising the tax burden from 10% to 12% of GDP. The 2027 budget, according to specialists, perpetuates rigid spending logic without the execution improvements that would make additional resources meaningful. The MEF itself has projected that fiscal balance won't be restored until 2028. That's not a timeline that earns you a ratings upgrade today.

Then there's the Atome case, which deserves far more attention than it's received. The British firm formally notified termination of its contract with ANDE for a green hydrogen project — a contract around which significant investment expectations had been built — while simultaneously keeping international arbitration against the Paraguayan state active. President Peña acknowledged the core problem himself: "the legal framework does not exist" for the preferential tariff the company had been promised, without clarifying what prior commitments created that expectation. This is exactly the kind of episode that poisons the investment climate in ways that headline GDP numbers cannot compensate for. You can announce that the EU-Mercosur agreement opens a window for Paraguay to position itself as a clean manufacturing platform. You can convene business forums and talk about green hydrogen exports. But if an investor reads the Atome case and understands that informal promises carry no legal weight and that resolving the dispute requires arbitration, the calculus changes immediately.

As a techno-optimist, I want to be excited about Paraguay's digital trajectory. The DNIT is advancing tax digitalization through electronic invoicing expansion. The BCP is promoting financial modernization. These are real, if modest, gains. But I've seen this movie in too many emerging markets: governments invest in the visible, legible layer of modernization — digital tax systems, fintech-friendly regulation, export dashboards — while the underlying institutional substrate that actually determines investment risk remains unreformed. Paraguay ranks 109th out of 139 countries in the global innovation index and places 461st in urban economic competitiveness, well below Buenos Aires and Mexico City. Those metrics don't improve because you merged two ministries or digitized customs declarations. They improve when the rule of law becomes predictable, when contracts made informally can be enforced formally, when a confidential central bank report on a digital bank's operational failures — as happened with Ueno Bank this week — doesn't trigger political scrambling but routine supervisory action.

The Itaipú renegotiation crystallizes the whole picture. Paraguay derives enormous fiscal significance from the binational hydroelectric complex — yet in the same week that the facility supplied 17.5% more energy to ANDE, it remitted $23 million less to the state under Annex C. Peña's response is to wait for Brazil's electoral calendar to determine when talks can resume. That's a sovereign country outsourcing its strategic fiscal agenda to its neighbor's political cycle. Investment grade requires demonstrating that you have the institutional capacity to advance your own national interests independently and reliably. Waiting for Brasília is not that demonstration.

Fitch is not slow. Paraguay's macro story is genuinely good, and the market for its sovereign bonds reflects that. But investment grade is a qualitative certification of institutional maturity, not just a reward for growth. Until Asunción treats corruption, contractual reliability, and fiscal rigidity as first-order problems rather than talking points for the next ratings cycle, the BB+ ceiling will hold. And no amount of guaraní appreciation will lift it.
