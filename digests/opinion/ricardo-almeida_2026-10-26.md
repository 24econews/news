> LENS: Market-liberal / fiscal conservative
> TITLE: Conservative fund managers who dodge accountability destroy the case for private pensions.
> DATE: 2026-10-26

Chile's pension system just proved that the most dangerous fund manager is the cautious one.

While Brazil's electoral chaos and Argentina's oil-versus-recession paradox have dominated regional headlines, the story deserving the sharpest scrutiny this week sits in Santiago. Chile's AFP Fundo E — the conservative multi-fund designed specifically to shield workers on the verge of retirement from volatility — has generated losses exceeding US$7.7 billion over the 2018–2026 period, with a real-terms decline of 10.39% so far this year alone. The Superintendencia de Pensiones has opened sanctioning proceedings against all seven administrators. A Chamber of Deputies investigative commission is incoming. And AFP Capital's general manager, Renzo Vercelli, told Diario Financiero, without a trace of remorse, that he would make the same investment decisions again.

That last detail is the one that should infuriate every market liberal in the room.

The failure of Fund E is not an argument against privately managed pensions. It is an argument against privately managed pensions without functional governance, transparent risk frameworks, and administrators who are genuinely accountable to the people whose retirement savings they hold. The AFPs placed leveraged bets on falling long-term U.S. interest rates through duration-extending swap strategies — bets that were speculative by nature, embedded in a fund explicitly marketed as conservative, and apparently insufficiently scrutinized by boards that former CPC chairman José Antonio Guzmán now concedes lacked adequate control mechanisms. UDP academic Salvador Valdés has quantified the governance failure with surgical precision: the correlation between annuity prices and 20-year bonds — the theoretical justification for the strategy — was a mere 8% at monthly frequency in 2025. That is not a hedge. That is a story told to boards that weren't asking hard enough questions.

Defenders of the system, including Guzmán himself, are correct that over 45 years the AFP model has delivered strong aggregate returns. That is true, relevant, and should not be discarded. But it is also a convenient refuge from the specific accountability question this episode raises. Markets only function well when failure carries consequences. When a general manager publicly states he would replicate a strategy that cost Chilean savers the equivalent of a small sovereign wealth fund, and faces no immediate professional consequence for that admission, the disciplinary mechanism that justifies private management in the first place has broken down. The argument for market-based pension systems rests on the proposition that private administrators, facing reputational and competitive pressure, will manage risk more prudently than bureaucracies. That proposition requires the pressure to actually exist — and to actually bite.

The political damage compounds the financial one. Chile's pension reform debate had been moving, haltingly, toward a framework that preserved private administration while adding a degree of solidarity and state oversight. The Kast government had positioned its AFP overhaul as the market-friendly alternative to the left's statist restructuring proposals. That political narrative is now severely compromised. Every opposition politician reaching for the nationalisation playbook has been handed a gift they did not earn. Fintual, which is launching its own AFP under the new framework, claims it flagged this excessive risk exposure in 2023 — and if that is accurate, the question of why the regulator did not act is as damaging as the losses themselves. Former CMF Vice-Chair Mauricio Larraín is explicit: the US$7 billion in asset damage has already materialized and is not reversible by future rate cuts. The injury is permanent. What remains open is whether the institutional response will be proportionate.

Moody's affirmed Chile's sovereign rating this week, noting the government's commitment to fiscal adjustment and improved 2027 prospects. That institutional stability is real and worth preserving. But investment-grade credibility and a functioning pension system are not substitutes for each other. The former speaks to how Chile is perceived by sovereign bond investors. The latter determines whether Chilean workers can retire with dignity. The AFP industry has spent thirty years making the case that private management serves both goals simultaneously. Fund E's administrators have spent this year demonstrating what happens when governance fails to enforce that compact. The Superintendencia's sanctioning process, the congressional investigation, and the question of restitution are not attacks on market principles. They are the market principles — accountability, consequence, and the non-negotiable requirement that those who manage other people's money answer for how they do it.

The government that defends private pensions most convincingly is the one that punishes their mismanagement most decisively. Santiago has a narrow window to demonstrate which kind it is.
