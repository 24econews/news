> PERSONA: Eduardo Ferraz
> LENS: Centrist institutionalist / technocrat
> TITLE: Macro Success Cannot Hide Paraguay's Governance Emergency
> DATE: 2026-10-15

The guaraní is performing splendidly. Paraguay's macroeconomic headlines read like a regional success story: 5.7% cumulative growth through August, IMF projections of 4.5% for the full year, the best-performing currency in Latin America, and a Finance Minister attending the Bangkok Annual Meetings to display credentials that most of his regional counterparts would envy. Look a few centimeters beneath that surface, however, and you find an institution managing over USD 800 million in public deposits — including funds from one of the hemisphere's largest binational energy projects — that has been caught disguising its ties to related companies, a regulator that apparently failed to detect the problem until it became a crisis, and a government response that generated more questions than answers. Paraguay's Ueno Bank affair is not a financial technicality. It is a governance emergency dressed up in a macro-success story, and the region's commentary class has largely allowed the growth figures to crowd out the harder question.

The facts deserve to be stated plainly. The Banco Central del Paraguay confirmed it detected maneuvers by Ueno Bank to conceal links with related companies. The institution's stake in related firms reportedly surged 8,000%. Deposits at the bank include funds from Itaipú, the binational generator that is the structural backbone of Paraguay's low-cost electricity model and one of the country's principal comparative advantages. Most troublingly, Itaipú itself stopped disclosing which banks hold its funds precisely when its Paraguayan deposits jumped from USD 57 million to USD 537 million — nearly a tenfold increase. That sequence — opacity deployed at the moment of maximum exposure — is not a coincidence to be explained away. It is the signature of regulatory capture, or at best of supervisory failure of a severity that demands institutional accountability, not reassuring press conferences.

The institutionalist case against complacency here is straightforward. Credit-rating agencies and multilateral lenders — the IMF, the IDB, Fitch — have consistently identified the gap between Paraguay's macroeconomic fundamentals and its governance indicators as the primary obstacle to achieving investment grade, a status that would materially lower the country's cost of capital and open infrastructure financing to a broader pool of private savings. Fitch has already warned that the Caja Fiscal pension reform was insufficient. Former Finance Minister Benigno López publicly characterized the BCP's congressional report in terms that translate, generously, as "disorderly and inconsistent." Investment grade is not simply a trophy; it is the mechanism through which sound fundamentals convert into lower borrowing costs, deeper capital markets, and the kind of long-cycle investment that Paraguay needs to reduce its structural dependence on soybean weather and hydrocarbon royalty negotiations with Brasília. Every episode of supervisory failure — every Ueno Bank — extends the timeline to that prize and raises its cost.

One must also reckon honestly with what the currency strength story obscures. The guaraní's appreciation, driven by agricultural export flows, is generating a fractured productive sector: large agro-industrial exporters pressing for exchange-rate predictability because their margins are compressed by a stronger local currency, and motorcycle manufacturers — Paraguay's most dynamic non-soy manufacturing segment — making the same complaint from the opposite direction, arguing that imported competition is becoming relatively cheaper. Both camps are right, and neither can be fully satisfied without addressing the structural question: an economy that grows because it rains on its soy fields and because Itaipú turbines turn is not yet an economy that has earned the institutional premium it is seeking in international capital markets. The Ueno case is the proof.

President Peña's convening of a banking-sector meeting to contain reputational damage was the correct procedural instinct. But containment is not resolution. The BCP must now demonstrate, with actions rather than communiqués, that it can enforce its own prudential rules against an institution managing public funds at this scale. If related-party exposure of the magnitude apparently discovered at Ueno passed undetected through regular supervisory cycles, the question is not simply what to do about this bank — it is what the supervisory framework itself failed to catch, and whether that framework requires legislative reinforcement. The parliamentary committee reviewing the 2027 budget is currently focused, understandably, on line-item spending pressures from the National University and the Supreme Court. It should be equally focused on whether the BCP's resources, mandate, and independence are calibrated to the actual risk profile of the financial system it oversees.

Paraguay's macroeconomic story is genuinely impressive and should not be dismissed. But the country is at an inflection point where the quality of its institutions will determine whether the next decade consolidates the growth trajectory or produces the kind of crisis that erases it. A guaraní at the top of regional performance charts while a systemically significant bank conceals its related-party network is not a paradox to be celebrated — it is a warning that the foundation requires inspection before the building goes higher.
