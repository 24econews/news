> TITLE: Investment Incentives Cannot Substitute for Political Certainty Argentina Desperately Needs
> DATE: 2026-11-06

Argentina holds the most genuinely contestable story this week. The Brazil coverage is dramatic but the argument about Lula's fiscal credibility crisis is largely settled consensus among analysts. The real disagreement worth interrogating is in Buenos Aires: Argentina has a fiscal surplus, record hydrocarbon production, Vaca Muerta delivering historic outputs, and IMF institutional backing — and yet country risk hit 925 basis points, the currency was hammered, and implied default probabilities reached 24%. The contestable question is whether RIGI-style investment incentive regimes can actually substitute for the political certainty investors are repricing, or whether they are structurally insufficient when electoral risk dominates the horizon.

---

The bond market is telling Argentina something the government refuses to hear clearly: resource wealth and fiscal discipline are necessary conditions for investor confidence, but they are not sufficient ones. Country risk at 925 basis points, with two-year credit default swaps implying a 24% default probability, is not a market misfiring. It is a rational repricing of political continuity risk — and no investment incentive regime, however generously structured, can fully substitute for that missing variable.

This is the core tension that the current Argentine moment crystallizes for anyone who thinks seriously about commodity cycles and capital attraction in resource-rich emerging economies. The RIGI framework, which grants 30-year fiscal stability, accelerated depreciation, and export remittance flexibility to qualifying large-scale investments, has produced genuinely impressive early results. YPF submitted the Bajo del Toro project with an estimated $8.7 billion in capital and projected exports of $25 billion between 2027 and 2055. Pampa Energía's Rincón de Aranda field reached 26,000 barrels per day, with the Vaca Muerta Sur pipeline expansion targeting one million barrels per day of capacity. Natural gas production hit all-time highs in 2026, with Vaca Muerta now supplying 67% of national output. These are not speculative numbers — they are operational realities being reported to Wall Street.

And yet the market is not rewarding them. Argentine energy stocks collapsed as much as 8.5% in a single session while global markets rallied on the US midterm results. Vista Energy hit its lowest price since April. YPF fell to $41.61 in New York. The Merval moved in the opposite direction from the Dow Jones on the same day. The explanation offered by economist Fernando Marull is analytically precise and worth taking seriously: of the roughly 400 basis points by which the sovereign spread has widened since mid-year, only half reflects global factors. The other half is a pure repricing of Milei's reelection odds, which markets have collapsed from approximately 90% to around 50%.

Here is the structural problem for anyone who designs RIGI-style frameworks as the primary instrument of resource nationalism's opposite — investment-friendly stability guarantees. These regimes work well when they solve the right problem. In a country where the investment deterrent is regulatory unpredictability, excessive taxation, or arbitrary contract modification, a 30-year fiscal stability clause is a genuinely powerful instrument. The historical record from Chile's early mining contracts, from Peru's estabilidad tributaria agreements, from Colombia's oil sector stabilization arrangements, all confirm this logic when political continuity is reasonably assured.

But RIGI cannot solve electoral discontinuity risk. A 30-year fiscal stability clause signed under government A is worth precisely what government B decides it is worth after the next election — and in Argentina, that calculation has played out badly enough times that markets discount it structurally. The businessmen at the ABECEB Forum were explicit: larger-scale investments will wait until 2028, when there is greater political certainty. The RIGI is, in their own words, "the only concrete bet on the table" for now — meaning it is capturing the most committed long-horizon capital, the LNG final investment decision YPF plans to sign in Abu Dhabi, the pipeline expansions, the upstream development. But the broader private investment signal remains absent. Capital goods imports fell 13.9% by volume in the first nine months of the year. Gross fixed capital formation contracted 11% year-on-year in the second quarter.

This is not an argument against RIGI. It is an argument about what RIGI can and cannot do. The framework is performing its intended function — locking in commitments from sophisticated long-term capital in sectors with 25-year investment horizons where project economics dominate political noise. For Vaca Muerta crude, for LNG, for the pipeline infrastructure supporting export capacity above $25 billion annually, the stabilization architecture matters enormously and is working. The IMF's Kristalina Georgieva citing Argentina alongside Greece, Portugal, and Ireland as fiscal discipline examples is not rhetorical theater — it reflects genuine progress on the primary balance and the structural fiscal correction the program required.

The mistake would be to treat RIGI as a substitute for the political work that determines whether the program survives 2027. Deputy Minister Daza described the economy as "very weak" and growing "less than expected" at a public forum. Poverty rose to 34.4% in the second quarter. Consumer credit has contracted for six consecutive months. Industrial output has fallen for nine consecutive months, with 95,000 jobs lost since the start of the administration. These are the conditions under which electoral reelection bets collapse from 90% to 50% — and no stabilization clause for a copper mine or an LNG terminal changes the political arithmetic of an electorate experiencing those conditions.

The Milei government has demonstrated that fiscal correction is achievable in Argentina even under conditions most economists considered politically impossible twelve months ago. That is a genuine accomplishment. What it has not yet demonstrated is that the correction can be sustained through an electoral cycle without the kind of broad-based recovery in employment, real wages, and domestic consumption that would give the voting public a material reason to maintain continuity. Resource nationalism debates in Latin America have always been fundamentally about who captures the rents from subsoil wealth and whether ordinary citizens feel they benefit from extraction booms. Vaca Muerta's production records are real. The question the ballot box will ask in October 2027 is whether those records translated into anything a family in Córdoba or the GBA could recognize as improvement in their own lives.

RIGI bought Argentina time and capital commitments it needed. It cannot buy the political outcome on which everything else depends.
