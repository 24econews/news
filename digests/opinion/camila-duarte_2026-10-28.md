> PERSONA: Camila Duarte
> LENS: Social-democratic / pro-redistribution
> TITLE: Bolsa Família won the election; now fiscal reality must be faced.
> DATE: 2026-10-28

Lula's re-election is, above all, a verdict on Bolsa Família. Not a verdict against it — the opposite. The 15.04% readjustment announced weeks before the first round, and the sustained defense of social transfer programs throughout the campaign, sent a clear signal to the millions of Brazilians who depend on those payments: the architecture of income support built over two decades of PT governance would not be dismantled. That is the most consequential distributive fact of October 25, and it deserves to be named plainly before we get to the yield curve.

But naming it plainly also requires honesty about the fiscal terrain on which that commitment now rests — and that terrain is treacherous.

Press reports are unambiguous on the numbers. The package of electoral measures announced by the Lula administration had already reached R$290 billion in commitments for 2026 and 2027 before the runoff was decided. By the time consultancy Pinotti & Schwartsman compiled the full tally, that figure had grown to R$356.3 billion, with R$88.5 billion added since September alone. The government's own projections place gross public debt approaching 90% of GDP by 2029. In the sessions after the runoff, the National Treasury canceled bond auctions and paid yields above 15% on 2029 paper in an emergency buyback, and the Ibovespa fell 13.88%, its steepest single-session drop since March 2020, as the EWZ plunged nearly 20% and the real depreciated sharply.

Those numbers matter for social policy, not just for portfolio managers. Social programs are financed by the state. A state that pays IPCA+ 6.42% on its long-dated bonds — and faces pressure toward 8% real yields, according to BTG Pactual's post-election projections — is a state whose fiscal space for everything else contracts with each basis point. The Selic, held at historically elevated levels, compressed household consumption growth from 5.1% in the fourth quarter of 2024 to just 0.9% in the second quarter of 2026, according to Ipea. Delinquency among Minha Casa Minha Vida beneficiaries is already surfacing on homebuilders' balance sheets. These are not abstractions; they are the lived conditions of the same population Bolsa Família is designed to protect.

Here is the core tension a social-democratic framework must confront honestly: the distributive gains of the past four years are real, but they were partly financed through mechanisms — front-loaded benefits, debt forgiveness for microentrepreneurs, Desenrola 3.0 with capacity to absorb up to R$90 billion — that Fitch Ratings described, after the result, as creating an unavoidable fiscal tightening in 2027 regardless of ideology. Fitch did not say this to punish the left. It said it because the arithmetic of debt service and primary balances is indifferent to political preference. Former Finance Minister Mailson da Nóbrega and tax reform architect Bernard Appy, cited in press reports, converged on the same diagnosis: there is no way to avoid adjustment in 2027, whoever governs.

What the re-election does preserve — and this is not a small thing — is the institutional continuity of the programs themselves. If the government pursues fiscal consolidation through revenue measures or spending reprioritization rather than through transfer cuts, Bolsa Família and its ecosystem of social supports could survive the squeeze intact. Planning Minister Bruno Moretti's reported signal that the government will seek a constitutional amendment to target the center of the fiscal rule, rather than the lower bound of the tolerance band, suggests the team is at least aware that a credibility signal is urgently needed. But awareness and credibility are different currencies, and markets in New York and London, according to Folha, were blunt: without a concrete spending-containment package presented quickly, capital will continue to exit.

The choice of finance minister is therefore not a technocratic detail. It is a distributive decision. A minister who can anchor expectations and unlock a modest decline in the long end of the yield curve is a minister who frees fiscal space for social expenditure. A minister who cannot do that, or one who treats the primary surplus as nonsense the way Lula did during the campaign, according to press reports, will preside over a squeeze in which social programs and the interest bill compete directly for the same resources. That competition has a predictable winner when debt service grows faster than revenues.

The congressional arithmetic complicates this further. Press reports said that R$18.5 billion in foreign funds had flowed into Brazilian equities since the first round betting on a Flávio Bolsonaro victory. The ruralist caucus, industrialists, and center-right bloc that define the legislative majority will now govern from opposition with consolidated fiscal concerns. If the government pursues its social agenda without a credible fiscal framework to anchor it, those coalitions will use budget negotiations as leverage — and the history of Brazilian fiscal politics suggests they are effective at extracting concessions that do not always benefit the poorest.

None of this diminishes what the re-election means for distributive continuity. The 15.04% Bolsa Família adjustment is on the books. The Minha Casa Minha Vida program, which accounted for 58% of residential launches and 51% of new home sales in the second quarter, remains the government's central housing instrument. The tax reform, paradoxically, is now moving forward — 25 working groups between the Receita Federal and the IBS Steering Committee were established the day after the election — and a rationalized VAT system could, over time, improve the efficiency of redistribution even if the transition creates short-term liquidity pressures for business.

The verdict of October 25 is that Brazilian voters, by a margin of over four million in the official TSE count, chose not to reverse the distributive architecture of the past four years. Honoring that verdict fiscally — not just rhetorically — requires acknowledging that Bolsa Família's durability depends on the state's solvency. The government inherited a mess it partly made. It now has to govern through it without dismantling the very programs that justify its mandate.
