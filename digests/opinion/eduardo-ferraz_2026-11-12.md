> LENS: Centrist institutionalist / technocrat
> TITLE: Strong macro cannot substitute for strong institutions: Paraguay's Ueno test
> DATE: 2026-11-12

The Paraguay crisis deserves the column this week. It is the region's most genuinely contestable story: a country with real, documented macroeconomic strength — 5.7% cumulative growth through August, IMF projections nearly double the global average, inflation under control — that has simultaneously suffered one of the most severe institutional failures in its democratic history. Reasonable people looking at the same facts genuinely disagree about whether the Ueno Bank collapse is a containable supervisory episode or a structural indictment of Paraguay's governance model. That tension is worth unpacking with precision.

---

Paraguay's Ueno Bank crisis is not, at its core, a banking story. It is a stress test of the proposition that strong macroeconomic fundamentals can substitute for strong institutions — and the results are unambiguous. They cannot.

The facts on the ground are damaging enough in isolation. A single digital bank, whose non-performing loan ratio jumped from 0.10% to 14.49% before its collapse, managed to accumulate deposits from Itaipú Binacional (US$163.8 million), the national electricity utility ANDE (US$21 million), the social security institute IPS (exposure exceeding US$500 million by internal estimates), Petropar, Senave, Yacyretá's pension fund, and the Ministry of Economy's own Caja Fiscal (roughly US$60 million). The former president of the Banco Central del Paraguay has been arrested. The 5G network concessionaire linked to the same group faces contract termination. The national port project in Asunción has stopped. The core banking tender for the state development bank is under review. By the time the guaraní became, by Bloomberg's accounting, the world's worst-performing currency, the institutional cascade had long since exceeded anything that could be called a contained supervisory failure.

What makes this contestable — and what the Paraguayan government and rating agencies are both tempted to emphasize — is that the underlying macroeconomic data remain genuinely impressive. The BCP reports 5.7% cumulative growth through August. The IMF projects Paraguay will expand at nearly twice the global average in 2026. The Deposit Guarantee Fund moved quickly: 93% of eligible depositors received advance payments, covering 1.2 million savers. The new BCP leadership was confirmed by the Senate within days. Country risk, though elevated, has not broken the investment-grade narrative outright. Moody's and S&P have both declined, for now, to trigger a ratings action. This is the institutionalist's temptation: to treat the episode as a shock to manage rather than a signal to interpret.

The temptation should be resisted. What the Ueno collapse reveals is not an isolated fraud but a systematic failure of the boundary between public resources and politically connected private institutions — the precise failure that multilateral governance frameworks exist to prevent. Public entities placed funds in a single digital lender not because it offered superior risk-adjusted returns but because it had the right network of political and institutional ties. Nobody in a position to interrupt the flow chose to do so. That is not a supervisory gap. It is a governance architecture that made capture rational and intervention costly. The business groups — Cainco, the Unión Industrial Paraguaya, the Asociación Rural del Paraguay — understood this immediately when they demanded investigations "without privileges." They were not asking for speed. They were asking for a signal that the rules apply uniformly, which is precisely the signal that had been absent.

The procedural consequences are specific and urgent. Paraguay's 2027 sovereign bond program, its access to external markets at sustainable rates, and the credibility of RIGI-adjacent investment frameworks in the region all depend on whether institutional credibility can be rebuilt on a compressed timeline. The Deposit Guarantee Fund has absorbed 50% of its total resources on a single case. Ueno's New York-issued bonds open the door to international litigation. The Ministry of Economy has already submitted a US$276 million budget cut as an opening move in what Minister Lovera calls a fiscal realignment toward 2028. These are real constraints, and they will bind tighter if the judicial process is perceived as selective or incomplete.

The lesson is not specific to Paraguay. Bolivia's multilateral financing agreements coexist with an economy contracting at 3.6% because legal certainty was sacrificed for state control over too long a period. Argentina's export boom coexists with a country risk that has doubled in four months because markets cannot price the institutional continuity of a program past a single electoral cycle. Brazil's record trade surplus coexists with a fiscal situation that forces even the central bank to acknowledge that issuing debt in reais is no longer a sufficient shield against mistrust. In each case, strong macro numbers have been unable to compensate for institutional weaknesses that sophisticated investors — and rating agencies, eventually — price with considerable accuracy.

Paraguay's window is real but narrow. The country's agricultural and energy fundamentals are not in question. What is in question is whether the speed of judicial accountability, the scope of supervisory reform at the BCP, and the credibility of the revised budget process will be sufficient to convince international capital markets that the Ueno episode was an aberration rather than a preview. Moody's warning about governance weaknesses was measured in language but not in implication. The guaraní's depreciation was not. Sound macroeconomics earns a country the opportunity to reform its institutions. It does not replace the need to do so.
