> LENS: Regional sovereigntist
> TITLE: IMF's Austerity Push Serves Washington, Not Brazil's Interests
> DATE: 2026-07-31

The moment that crystallizes everything is this: Brazil has formally invoked the WTO dispute mechanism against U.S. tariffs, simultaneously opened negotiations toward a Mercosur-China trade agreement through a phone call between Lula and Xi Jinping, and is now approaching a Mercosur-South Korea working group — all while IMF projections place Brazilian gross debt at 96.5% of GDP and the Fund recommends "a more ambitious and less gradual" fiscal adjustment than the one currently planned. Read these facts together, and a pattern emerges that should alarm anyone who still believes Mercosur nations can chart their own course.

The IMF's recommendation, buried in its annual review of Brazil, is the sentence that deserves the most scrutiny. Not because Brazil's fiscal position is irrelevant — a primary deficit of R$91.2 billion in the first half of 2026, the second-worst result since 1997, is a serious matter — but because of *when* and *how* the Fund chooses to speak. Brazil is simultaneously absorbing a 12.5% tariff surcharge from Washington affecting 47.3% of its exports to the United States, facing a 55% surtax trigger on beef exports to China as it approaches 80% of its annual quota, and processing the competitive shock of US$5.2 billion in Chinese automobile imports flooding its market in just five months. This is the most complex external pressure the region has faced in a generation. And the IMF's response is to counsel greater austerity, faster.

This is not a coincidence of timing. It is the logic of structural adjustment dressed in updated language. Anyone who lived through the 1999 Brazilian crisis, or Argentina's 2001 collapse, or the Brazilian recession that followed the 2015-2016 adjustment demanded by precisely these external creditors, knows the pattern. The conditionality changes its vocabulary — from "fiscal consolidation" to "fiscal framework integrity" — but the architecture remains the same: when a developing nation faces external shocks beyond its control, the multilateral prescription is to compress domestic demand, reduce the spending room that governments need to cushion those shocks, and in so doing, transfer the cost of adjustment onto workers and poorer consumers. The FGV data is unambiguous on this point: the Consumer Confidence Index fell to 88.3 in July, with the sharpest deterioration among lower-income households, whose current financial situation index dropped 3.5 points. Average non-earmarked credit rates to households stand at 64% per year, the highest in nearly a decade. This is not an economy that needs squeezing. It is an economy that needs maneuvering room.

What Brazil actually needs — and what Lula's diplomacy this week instinctively gestures toward, even if it does not yet fully embrace — is precisely the regional leverage the IMF framework is designed to constrain. The Lula-Xi phone call, the acceleration of Mercosur-China talks, the WTO filing, the invocation of the Reciprocity Law against Washington: these are the instruments of a country that understands it cannot negotiate in isolation. Mercosur's collective bargaining weight is the asset that turns Brazil from a price-taker into a participant. The Mercosur-EU agreement, long delayed and still contested, the nascent opening toward Seoul, the possibility of a trade architecture with Beijing — these represent the sovereign alternative to accepting external dictates one country at a time, each isolated from the others, each more vulnerable alone. The CNI calculates that US$10.8 billion in Brazilian exports are simultaneously hit by U.S. tariffs. That figure, multiplied across Argentina, Uruguay, Paraguay and Bolivia, constitutes a regional exposure that demands a regional response, not individual fiscal contractions advised by Washington-based institutions.

I will say clearly what intellectual honesty requires me to say: Brazil's fiscal numbers are genuinely worrying. Record tax collection alongside a near-record deficit is not a statistical curiosity — it is evidence of a structural spending dynamic that Finance Minister Durigan himself acknowledges, including his discussions with Lula about tightening the real spending cap from 2.5% to 1.5%. Lula's comment about financing science and technology "outside the fiscal framework" is the kind of political improvisation that corrodes credibility precisely when it is most needed. These are real problems, and they deserve serious domestic debate. But the appropriate authors of that debate are Brazilian voters, Brazilian institutions, Brazilian economists — not the IMF, whose 96.5% debt projection uses its own methodology, and whose track record in the region includes a role in every major crisis from which Mercosur has spent decades recovering.

The genuinely contestable question of this moment is not whether Brazil needs fiscal order — it does. The question is who sets the terms, on whose timeline, and in whose interest. When an institution headquartered in Washington recommends faster austerity to a government simultaneously under tariff attack from Washington, the conflict of interest is not subtle. Mercosur's path to genuine sovereignty runs through exactly the kind of diversified trade architecture Lula is now scrambling to construct under emergency conditions. That architecture — with China, with the EU, with South Korea, with the region itself — should have been built from a position of strategic calm, not assembled under simultaneous fire from the world's largest economy and the world's most influential multilateral lender. The lesson is painful and it should be stated plainly: regional integration deferred is regional leverage forfeited. The bill for that deferral is being presented right now, and it is enormous.
