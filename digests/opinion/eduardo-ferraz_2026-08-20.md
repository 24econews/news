> PERSONA: Eduardo Ferraz
> LENS: Centrist institutionalist / technocrat
> TITLE: Uruguay's Stability Must Finally Translate Into Growth and Jobs
> DATE: 2026-08-20

The IMF praised Uruguay's stability. Kristalina Georgieva said so herself, in Montevideo. Then she added the caveat that will define the Frente Amplio government's remaining mandate: stability alone is not enough.

That sentence should be framed on Gabriel Oddone's office wall. Uruguay has, by every institutional metric, done the hard work correctly. Standard & Poor's recently upgraded the country two notches, placing it at the lowest sovereign risk in Latin America. Sovereign spreads are trading at levels unseen since before the pandemic. A domestic bond auction attracted bids of nearly 9.7 billion pesos — more than double what the government offered — at a rate below 7%. When Montevideo sounds out international investors for a new external issuance, market appetite is not the problem. Uruguay has built something genuinely rare in this region: institutional credibility that survives electoral cycles and does not need to be rebuilt from scratch every five years.

And yet. GDP grew 1.8% in 2025, below official projections. The World Bank has trimmed its forecast for 2026 to 1.6%. Oddone himself has acknowledged a "fairly high probability" of revising growth projections downward. The CERES leading index posted a 0.3% gain in May — a figure so modest it barely qualifies as signal. Half of all Uruguayans, according to surveys cited by El Observador, consider the country's economic situation to be poor. Inflation has pierced the Central Bank's tolerance floor, now sitting at its lowest level in seventy years — a statistic the government touts with evident pride, but which equally reflects demand so subdued that price pressures have essentially evaporated. The BCU holds the policy rate at 5.75% and speaks of maintaining price stability, while pesification — the strategic goal of reducing dollar use in domestic transactions — advances more slowly than the government projected, with no clear inflection point in sight.

This is the genuine tension worth examining, and it is more interesting than the crisis narratives dominating the region's headlines this week. Uruguay is not Brazil, where Casas Bahia's R$17.3 billion bankruptcy filing and eleven consecutive sessions of Ibovespa losses signal an economy in active distress. Uruguay is not Argentina, where country risk has climbed above 517 basis points while Wall Street rallied, or Bolivia, where a fuel subsidy dismantlement triggered a legislative censure and near-universal productive sector revolt. Uruguay's problem is more subtle and, in some ways, harder to solve: it has secured the trust of international creditors without yet converting that trust into tangible welfare for broad segments of its own population.

The fiscal architecture deserves close reading here. The Consejo Fiscal Autónomo warned Parliament of an overestimation in MEF growth projections. The 2024 deficit was, in structural terms, comparable to 2019. The Unión Industrial Asociada describes the fiscal pressure on the formal economy as among the highest in the world — a characterization that resonates in a moment when the government has raised the IRPF on wages for two consecutive years. Oddone's response — that competitiveness will not be won through the exchange rate, that microeconomic reform and deregulation are the correct instruments — is intellectually defensible. The president of the Asociación Rural del Uruguay declared the sector "at the limit," and the government's refusal to use the dollar as an adjustment variable is a deliberate and correct choice. Exchange-rate devaluationism is a populist shortcut that produces short-term relief at the cost of long-term credibility — exactly the kind of institutional capital Uruguay has spent decades accumulating. Throwing it away to ease agricultural margins for one electoral cycle would be precisely the wrong trade.

But the competitiveness bill Oddone has promised — cutting red tape, deregulating intermediaries, easing formal employment costs — must materialize, not remain a prospectus. The AFAP pension reform debate adds regulatory uncertainty at exactly the moment the government most needs to project stability to private investors. Oddone has ruled out confiscation and nationalization in explicit terms, and he deserves credit for that clarity. But the rifts within the Frente Amplio on this question are real, and uncertainty about pension savings is not a cost-free political signal when the government simultaneously needs institutional investors to extend the same confidence in domestic reforms that bond markets are extending in sovereign debt.

The solar tender in Fray Bentos — thirteen domestic and international bids for a new photovoltaic park — and the decade of renewable investment it signals is the most honest illustration of what Uruguay's stability can produce when translated into concrete policy: durable, low-controversy, institutionally anchored investment that does not depend on electoral outcomes. That is the model. Stability is not an end state; it is the platform from which genuine development must be built. Uruguay has the platform. The Georgieva warning stands.
