> PERSONA: Eduardo Ferraz
> LENS: Centrist institutionalist / technocrat
> TITLE: Paraguay's Growth Masks a Regulatory System Primed to Collapse
> DATE: 2026-11-26

The day Paraguay's Senate passed a $25.5 billion national budget, the country's Finance Minister was simultaneously trying to explain to Brazil's foreign minister how $220 million of Itaipú Binacional funds had vanished into a failed private bank that his own government's regulators had supervised. That juxtaposition — a legislature projecting fiscal ambition while the executive manages an institutional implosion — is not an irony. It is a diagnosis.

The Ueno Bank collapse is, at its core, a governance failure of the precise kind that multilateral institutions exist to prevent — and that technocratic frameworks exist to detect before they become crises. What the reporting from Asunción this week makes clear is that the failure was not hidden. ABC Color notes that a report titled "Delirio colectivo" had warned of the institution's fragility at least a year before resolution proceedings began. The Banco Central del Paraguay, whose mandate is exactly this kind of supervisory oversight, missed it, ignored it, or lacked the institutional capacity to act on it. That distinction matters less than the outcome: USD 888 million in public funds committed, a potential total deficit of USD 1.5 billion according to the BCP's own projections, and a bilateral diplomatic crisis with Brazil over Itaipú deposits that now must be replaced by a government with no clear mechanism to do so.

Defenders of the Peña administration will point, correctly, to the macroeconomic fundamentals. Economic activity grew 5.5% year-to-date through September. The IMF is in Asunción conducting a periodic review and will find, by regional standards, a genuinely robust picture. The DNIT tax reform — merging customs and tax collection under a single authority with an ambition to raise the tax burden from 10 to 12 percent of GDP — reflects exactly the kind of institutional modernization that technocrats and multilateral lenders should, in principle, applaud. These achievements are real and should not be dismissed. But they are precisely what makes the Ueno catastrophe so instructive, and so dangerous: strong macroeconomic aggregates coexisting with institutional fragility at the supervisory level is not stability — it is a compression spring.

The structural diagnosis emerging from the reporting is unsparing. Paraguay's informal economy, according to figures cited by ABC Color, reaches 47.1% of GDP, with flows of USD 23.595 billion. That is not a marginal phenomenon — it is a parallel economic ecosystem large enough to absorb, sustain, and conceal an institution like Ueno for years. When a bank can grow rapidly enough to hold deposits from Itaipú Binacional, the Banco Nacional de Fomento, ANDE, and foreign cooperation funds from Luxembourg, while simultaneously failing to meet its own charter's basic governance requirements, the problem is not Ueno. The problem is the regulatory architecture that permitted it. Director Zárate's admission — that the deposit clause at the center of the scandal "was never presented to the board" — is not a confession of one bank's dishonesty. It is an indictment of an oversight system that apparently had no mechanism to verify whether key contractual provisions governing public deposits had ever been approved by the institution's own governance bodies.

What should concentrate the attention of regional investors and multilateral partners now is the cascade of second-order effects that the reporting documents with uncomfortable specificity. The contamination of Itaipú tariff renegotiations — already a delicate bilateral process — by a domestic banking failure is the kind of institutional spillover that credit rating agencies weigh when assessing sovereign risk. The cancellation of the BNF's core banking modernization tender because the winning provider was tainted by Ueno connections means that Paraguay's public development bank will delay its own operational reform precisely when the financial system needs demonstrably clean institutions. The 30% drop in events-sector bookings reported as an indirect consequence of the bank's closure is a reminder that institutional confidence is not an abstraction — it moves through the real economy in ways that GDP growth figures do not capture until it is too late.

Paraguay has built, over a decade, a reputation for macroeconomic seriousness that is rare in South America and that it cannot afford to squander on the argument that this was an isolated incident. The Multisectoral Forum's characterization of the 2027 budget as "fiscal irresponsibility" may be politically inflated, but the underlying concern — that the government is proposing to pay for the past with future debt while the precise perimeter of the Ueno losses remains unresolved — is legitimate and deserves a formal, institutionally grounded response. The IMF review underway is the right moment to provide one. What the multilateral community should be pressing for is not a bailout but a supervisory audit: a credible, independent assessment of the BCP's oversight framework, with binding recommendations and a public implementation timeline. That is what institutional credibility looks like in practice — not growth numbers, but the willingness to subject your own regulatory failures to transparent external scrutiny before the next Ueno is already accepting deposits.

Populists of every variety will offer a simpler story: blame the bankers, blame the regulators, blame the previous government, blame Brazil for the Itaipú complications. The harder, more necessary work is to build the supervisory institutions that make the next Ueno impossible — and to do that work while the economy is still growing, not after the spring has already snapped.
