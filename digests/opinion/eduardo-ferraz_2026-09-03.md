> LENS: Centrist institutionalist / technocrat
> TITLE: Paraguay's Growth Masks a Fiscal Governance Crisis Investors Should Fear
> DATE: 2026-09-03

The numbers coming out of Paraguay this week invite a certain kind of reassurance. The Banco Central del Paraguay reports GDP growth of 5.9% in the first half of 2026, the soy complex has injected USD 3.5 billion into the economy through July, and the IMF has praised the country's macroeconomic fundamentals. President Santiago Peña accepted the World Bank's reclassification of Paraguay as an upper-middle-income economy with understandable satisfaction. By the standard metrics that international investors typically scan first, Paraguay looks like a regional success story.

But beneath that headline sits a detail that should concentrate minds in Asunción, and among the sovereign creditors who have so far continued to roll over Paraguayan debt without serious friction: USD 280 million in government obligations that did not appear in the Ministry of Economy and Finance's own books. This was not uncovered by an external audit or a whistleblower. It surfaced, according to former minister César Barreto's own public statements, during a routine account review. The government responded by convening former finance ministers for what President Peña framed as a collective analysis of the fiscal situation. The gesture was well-intentioned. It was also an implicit admission that the official fiscal narrative had developed a gap wide enough to require an unusual institutional intervention to close.

This matters beyond Paraguay's borders because it illustrates a recurrent pattern in the region that technocrats and institutional investors alike should be unwilling to normalize: the decoupling of growth performance from fiscal governance quality. Paraguay's Fiscal Responsibility Law has been complied with in only three of its years in force — a figure that would ordinarily trigger a serious sovereign credit review. Public debt rose USD 1.498 billion in the first half of this year alone. Interest payments expanded 12.9% year-on-year. Wage spending in the central government grew 9.9%. Salaries, pensions, and debt service now account for nearly 70% of budget rigidity. The new 2027 budget bill proposes a deficit of roughly 6% of GDP and contemplates approximately USD 2 billion in borrowing, at a moment when the MEF is simultaneously reviewing whether to amend the Fiscal Responsibility Law itself to accommodate those numbers. Former deputy economy minister Manuel Ferreira's assessment — that the target of returning to a 1.5% deficit by 2028 is a government "pipe dream" — is not ideological provocation. It is a sober reading of the arithmetic.

The governance response to date has been procedurally inadequate. Ruling out new taxes while announcing a review of exemptions is not a fiscal consolidation plan; it is a deferral. The informal economy, estimated at 47% of GDP, represents a structural revenue floor that cannot be raised through administrative tinkering alone. The Dirección Nacional de Ingresos Tributarios, the flagship institutional reform of the Peña era, promised to raise the tax burden from 10% to 12% of GDP and generate USD 400 million in additional annual revenue. The results so far have not validated that optimism, and the Central Bank has declined to comment publicly on its own prudential criteria — a silence that compounds rather than relieves market uncertainty about regulatory oversight.

The IMF's continued public endorsement, and Moody's decision to maintain the sovereign rating, reflect the fact that Paraguay's external position remains defensible: agricultural export flows are strong, international reserves are adequate, and the government has market access. But institutional credibility is not simply a function of debt service capacity in a given quarter. It is a function of whether the rules that govern public finances mean what they say. A Fiscal Responsibility Law that has been honored in fewer than half its years in force is not a fiscal anchor. It is a suggestion. And a 2027 budget that proposes to exceed the statutory deficit ceiling, accompanied by a discussion about whether to revise the law's indicators rather than the spending trajectory, is precisely the kind of procedural erosion that rating agencies and multilateral lenders eventually price in — not immediately, but irreversibly.

Paraguay has earned its investment grade through two decades of genuine macroeconomic stabilization. That achievement is real and should not be discounted. But investment grade is not a permanent endowment. It is renewed continuously on the basis of institutional behavior. The combination of off-book liabilities, a fiscal law observed more in breach than in compliance, an informal economy approaching half of GDP, and a budget process now openly debating whether to amend its own constraints is not the profile of a sovereign that can afford to treat its credibility as a fixed asset. The growth numbers are good. The governance numbers are not. Asunción should understand, before its creditors are forced to remind it, that the second set of numbers is the one that determines whether the first remains sustainable.
