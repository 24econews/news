> LENS: Geopolitical realist
> TITLE: Paraguay's Fiscal Collapse Would Reshape Its Geopolitical Position Dangerously
> DATE: 2026-09-07

Paraguay's fiscal credibility crisis is the most genuinely contestable story in the region this week — and the one most consequential for anyone thinking in terms of sovereign risk, supply chains, and great-power positioning.

The International Monetary Fund has formally flagged a fiscal deficit of 3.5% of GDP in Asunción, arriving simultaneously with a 2027 budget that the country's own former finance ministers are calling unfeasible, and with a congressional faction pushing to raise the legally permitted deficit ceiling to 6% of GDP. The government of Santiago Peña projects 4.2% GDP growth, an 11.2% tax-to-GDP ratio, and a guaraní exchange rate that currency analysts are already describing as an "illusory view." Former minister Dionisio Borda — not a political opponent, but one of the country's most credible fiscal voices — said plainly that the budget shifts the problem onto the next administration. That is not partisan noise. That is the country's institutional memory on fiscal discipline speaking, and it deserves to be taken seriously.

The conventional response to this story is to note that Paraguay's country risk stands at just 107 basis points, that Moody's held its sovereign rating, and that first-half GDP growth reached 5.9%. All true. But this is precisely where the geopolitical realist parts company with the market cheerleader. Country risk at 107 basis points reflects the stock of credibility Paraguay has accumulated over a decade of relatively disciplined fiscal management. It does not reflect the flow of decisions being made right now. Bond markets price credibility slowly and punish its loss suddenly. Paraguay has spent years building investment-grade status — a rare achievement in its neighborhood — and it is now systematically mortgaging that inheritance in exchange for spending that is structurally rigid: wages, pensions, and debt service already consume nearly 70% of the budget, with the Ministry of Economy's own union demanding a 69% salary increase even as the government preaches austerity to the IMF.

The strategic dimension that is going almost entirely unreported is what Paraguay's fiscal trajectory means for its external positioning. Paraguay is the only country in South America without a diplomatic relationship with China, maintaining formal ties instead with Taiwan — an arrangement that Washington regards as a strategic asset and that Taipei pays to preserve in the form of financial and diplomatic support. That positioning is not free. It costs Paraguay access to Chinese financing, Chinese infrastructure investment, and Chinese commodity markets on the most favorable terms. A country operating comfortably within its fiscal rules can afford that cost. A country borrowing USD 2 billion annually to plug a deficit, while sitting on a shadow economy worth 47% of GDP that its tax authority cannot effectively reach, is a country whose external options are quietly narrowing. The Dirección Nacional de Ingresos Tributarios — the flagship institutional reform of the Peña era, born from merging the tax and customs authorities — was supposed to deliver an additional USD 400 million in annual revenue and lift the tax burden from 10% to 12% of GDP. The results so far have not justified that optimism, and the system suffered technical failures during a filing deadline this week, which is a small detail that illustrates a larger institutional fragility.

The energy dimension compounds the strategic exposure. Paraguay has guaranteed electricity supply only through 2030. New generating capacity requires ten to fourteen years of lead time to come online. Cryptocurrency operators and AI data center operators — sectors with zero strategic value to Paraguayan citizens — are lobbying to extend their preferential electricity tariffs for another decade, which would directly crowd out cheap energy availability for the rest of the economy. Itaipú has already transferred USD 27 million less to the Paraguayan state this year. The Chaco holds potentially significant natural gas reserves in the Carandayty area, but those remain speculative and undeveloped. A country that cannot fund its fiscal accounts, cannot tax its informal economy, and is approaching an electricity cliff within four years is not a country in a position to maximize its leverage in any bilateral negotiation — whether with Washington over the Taiwan relationship, with Brasília over Itaipú renegotiation terms, or with Buenos Aires over Mercosur's internal distribution of EU trade agreement quotas.

On that last point: Paraguay this week was again pressing for an equitable distribution of the 25% quota share in the EU agreement that the rest of the bloc refuses to concede. Asunción has a legitimate complaint. But leverage in that dispute comes from fiscal credibility, institutional strength, and the ability to credibly threaten alternatives. A Paraguay visibly struggling to meet its own fiscal rules, with an IMF publicly registering concern and a budget its own experts call unrealistic, walks into that negotiation weakened.

Paraguay's investment-grade rating is a strategic asset, not merely a financial one. Losing it — or even allowing the perception to solidify that fiscal consolidation has been deferred indefinitely — would reshape the country's external positioning in ways that no amount of GDP growth can quickly undo. The Peña government has narrow time to demonstrate that this week's budget is a one-cycle departure, not a structural inflection. The IMF's red flag should be read not as a technical caveat but as a geopolitical warning: small open economies with constrained tax bases and ideologically fixed external alignments cannot afford to spend their fiscal capital. Paraguay is spending it.
