> PERSONA: Lucía Ibarra
> LENS: Regional sovereigntist
> TITLE: Lula's Victory Threatens Washington's Financial Control Over South America
> DATE: 2026-10-28

Lula's return to the Palácio do Planalto is not merely a Brazilian event. It is a regional inflection point, and for those of us who have spent careers arguing that Mercosur's greatest untapped asset is its collective refusal to be managed from Washington and Geneva, the result of October 25 carries weight that goes well beyond São Paulo's bond curve.

Let me be direct about what the market reaction actually reveals. The Ibovespa's 13.88% collapse — the worst single session since March 2020, with circuit breakers triggering at the opening bell — the real suffering its worst one-day depreciation since January 1999, and EWZ plunging nearly 20% in New York: all of this is not primarily a judgment on Lula. It is the unwinding of a massive speculative position built on the assumption that Flávio Bolsonaro would govern Brazil as a subordinate to the investment strategies of New York and London. According to press reports, R$18.5 billion in foreign funds had flowed into the stock exchange since the first round betting on that outcome. When it didn't materialize, the exit was violent. Markets that price entire democracies as trade positions should not be surprised when elections produce surprises.

The fiscal picture is genuinely serious, and I will not pretend otherwise. The Lula government's electoral-era package of measures reached R$290 billion in commitments for 2026 and 2027, and consulting firm Pinotti & Schwartsman puts the total fiscal impact of recent government announcements at R$356.3 billion. Public debt already hovers around 82% of GDP, the highest since the pandemic. The DI for January 2031 was trading at 12.78% before the vote; long rates will go higher. Alfredo Menezes of Armor Capital was projecting at least 100 basis points of rise in long yields. BTG Pactual estimated the Ibovespa decline in dollar terms, combined with currency depreciation, could reach 30%. These are real numbers, and a government that press reports said dismissed concern about primary surpluses as nonsense during the campaign will need to produce a credible fiscal signal quickly — the finance minister appointment, as Valor Econômico and Folha de S.Paulo both noted, is the most immediate test.

But here is what the sovereign perspective demands we not lose in the noise of the circuit breakers: Brazil under Lula is the only plausible anchor for an economically integrated Mercosur that does not simply become a procurement zone for whoever holds sway in Washington. Brazil surpassed Saudi Arabia as China's second-largest supplier of crude oil. The National Treasury conducted a roadshow in Beijing to gauge conditions for a potential sovereign panda bond issuance. Vale filed with NAFMII to register a yuan-denominated debt program. Oil and derivatives generated a trade surplus of US$31.22 billion in the first nine months of the year, a historical record. Brazil is not a country that needs to accept terms. It is a country with structural leverage that it has historically been too timid to use.

If the re-elected government pursues a consistent posture of diversifying Brazil's financial relationships — building on the panda bond initiative and the China oil relationship — it would shift the gravitational field of the entire bloc. Argentina is under an IMF program with a country risk above 700 basis points, accepting US investment packages conditioned explicitly on political continuity beyond 2027. Bolivia is the only economy in the region the IMF projects contracting, dependent on a $1.9 billion credit with strings that include subsidy elimination. Paraguay navigates its own fiscal constraints. Uruguay preserves its investment grade but cannot break out of structural stagnation. The asymmetry is stark: these economies need regional solidarity more than they need further integration into a global financial architecture that prices their debt according to their willingness to follow instructions.

The question is whether the fourth term can thread the needle: enough fiscal credibility to stop the bleeding in long rates, enough strategic autonomy to avoid the position Argentina and Bolivia now occupy, negotiating under IMF programs. JP Morgan downgraded the real and government bonds from overweight to marketweight. Goldman Sachs acknowledged what it called selective opportunities anchored in Brazil's strategic external positioning. That phrase — strategic external positioning — is doing a great deal of work. It describes a country with Petrobras producing 2.96 million barrels of oil equivalent per day, with the world's largest iron ore exporter, with a carbon credit market attracting Google and McKinsey, with a pulp sector building a $4.6 billion plant in Rio Grande do Sul. That is not a country that negotiates from weakness.

The tax reform, paradoxically, is moving forward. The Receita Federal and the IBS Steering Committee established 25 working groups immediately after the election. The unified VAT rate announcement was expected within days. If the new government treats reform implementation as the fiscal signal markets need — rather than another round of promises about primary surplus targets — it could stabilize long rates without surrendering the policy space Mercosur integration requires.

None of this is inevitable. If the government pursues a repeat of the third term's spending trajectory without any corrective signal, the real depreciation alone will export inflation to every economy that trades with Brazil. A weaker real makes Brazilian soybeans more competitive and hammers Argentine industrial exporters to their main market — which absorbs 12.7% of Argentine foreign sales. Paraguay's guaraní strength, already straining its exporters, would face additional pressure. The regional spillover of Brazilian fiscal dysfunction is not abstract; it moves through exchange rates, commodity prices, and capital flows that hit every Mercosur economy within weeks.

The result is certified. What comes next is not. But the framework for reading it should be this: a Brazil willing to diversify its financial relationships, defend policy space, and build a Mercosur that negotiates collectively with external creditors is worth the turbulence that markets are now repricing. A Brazil that capitulates to the adjustment logic that has kept Argentina in permanent crisis is a loss not just for São Paulo, but for the entire bloc. The finance minister's name will tell us which direction this goes.
