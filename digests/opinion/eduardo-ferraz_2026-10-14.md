> TITLE: Congressional strength matters more than presidential votes in Brazil's fiscal crisis.
> DATE: 2026-10-14

The most important question about any Brazilian government is not what it promises, but whether it can actually govern. Elections produce mandates; Congress produces legislation, budgets, and, ultimately, fiscal outcomes. The first round of October 4 confirmed something that goes well beyond the horse-race result: the PL cemented itself as the largest party in both the Chamber and the Senate. That single fact reshapes the institutional arithmetic of the next four years more decisively than any individual vote share.

Let me be direct about what the first-round result means as a matter of institutional design. Flávio Bolsonaro secured roughly 47% of valid votes against Lula's 45%, a margin that surprised virtually every major polling house. Markets read the PL's congressional consolidation as a signal of governability — greater parliamentary muscle, the reasoning went, means a higher probability that a fiscal adjustment package can actually pass. The Ibovespa's 7.7% single-session surge, the dollar falling to its lowest level since May, and a 100-basis-point compression in ten-year yields within hours of the result were all expressions of that institutional bet. The National Treasury's subsequent placement of R$33.28 billion in fixed-rate bonds — the largest first-auction volume since December 2020 — confirmed that institutional investors were willing to lock in long-duration rates, wagering that the rate cycle would converge downward under a more fiscally orthodox administration.

That enthusiasm, however, is a bet on coalition math, not on certified policy. And coalition math in Brazil is never simple. The PL's congressional strength is real, but translating legislative plurality into a working majority on specific fiscal measures — spending cuts, pension reform, the future of the tax-exemption regime for infrastructure debentures — requires negotiation, concession, and political capital that no first-round result automatically confers. The market's initial reaction priced in the best-case scenario: a government with both the will and the votes to deliver the R$250 billion in adjustment that economists calculate is necessary to stabilize public debt over four years. What it has not yet priced in is the friction that inevitably intervenes between electoral arithmetic and legislative reality.

The fiscal backdrop makes this governability question urgent rather than abstract. Public debt already stands at 82.9% of GDP, up 4.2 percentage points between January and August alone, and economists warn the next administration will have until 2028 to prevent gross debt from exceeding the 87.6% peak reached during the pandemic. Valor Econômico has laid out seven reasons why the adjustment math known so far may be insufficient to stabilize debt at all. Infrastructure debentures — with an outstanding stock of R$2.3 trillion — are already showing their widest spreads since 2024, partly because markets are working with the hypothesis that some form of taxation of these instruments is coming regardless of who wins the runoff. The IMF, in its October World Economic Outlook, cut Brazil's growth projection even as it raised the global figure — a reminder that the country's structural challenges predate the election and will outlast the post-first-round euphoria.

If Flávio Bolsonaro wins the October 25 runoff, the institutional question becomes whether his team can convert congressional plurality into a coherent governing coalition capable of sustaining a credible adjustment path. His economic coordinator has already signaled a preference for a collegiate decision-making structure rather than a super-minister model, and his team is studying a fiscal package focused on containing mandatory spending — pensions, healthcare, education — equivalent to roughly 2% of GDP. Research houses project additional Ibovespa upside of between 11% and 45% in the event of a Flávio victory, depending on the degree of repricing and the effective implementation of that adjustment. The conditionality embedded in that range is the point: markets are not rewarding a platform, they are pricing a probability distribution. The spread on incentivized debentures widened from 1.08 to 1.27 percentage points after the first round — a signal that even within the optimistic scenario, investors are demanding compensation for policy uncertainty. The institutional test for a Flávio government would be whether the PL's congressional position translates into durable majority coalitions on each specific reform, or whether it fragments under the pressure of sectoral interests and the centrist parties whose support would still be required.

If Lula wins the runoff, the governability challenge is different in character but no less severe. The first-round result has already forced a repositioning: reports indicate that the Lula camp is weighing an announcement of Vice President Geraldo Alckmin as future Finance Minister, a signal designed explicitly to project fiscal discipline toward centrist voters and financial markets. That move reveals how much the first round reshaped the terms of the debate — the economic discussion has abruptly shifted from household debt and consumer welfare to fiscal credibility and the public debt trajectory. A re-elected Lula government would face a Congress in which the opposition holds its strongest position in years. The planning ministry has already described a gradual four-year adjustment as the government's plan, but passing that adjustment through a legislature where the PL leads both chambers would require either a genuine cross-partisan consensus or the kind of political transactions that have historically been among the more expensive features of Brazilian democratic life.

What both scenarios share is the structural constraint that no election resolves: the fiscal path is narrow, the adjustment required is large, and the congressional votes needed to pass it must be assembled one by one. The services PMI already showed contraction at its sharpest pace in nearly a year, household financial vulnerability worsened in 22 of 27 states in the second quarter, and UNCTAD projects GDP growth decelerating to 1.8% in 2026 and 1.5% in 2027. These are not problems that a favorable market reaction corrects; they are the environment in which the next government must govern.

From an institutionalist perspective, the lesson of the first round is unambiguous: the congressional composition matters more than the presidential vote share, and both matter less than the quality of the fiscal program the winner brings to office. Medley, the asset manager, warned explicitly that the market rally has a short shelf life — that history may repeat itself — a reference to the frustrations that followed similar political repricings in Brazil's recent past. The antidote to those frustrations is not a particular candidate; it is a credible, concrete, and governable adjustment program presented to Congress with sufficient coalition depth to survive the legislative process. That test begins on October 25, but it will not be settled there. It will be settled in the budget proposals, the congressional votes, and the fiscal printouts that follow — long after the market's initial enthusiasm has either found its footing or faded.
