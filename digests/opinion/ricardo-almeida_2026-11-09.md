> TITLE: Paraguay's Growth Story Collapses Under Weight of Institutional Rot
> DATE: 2026-11-09

The story that deserves this week's column is hiding in plain sight beneath the Brazilian avalanche of headlines: Paraguay's Ueno Bank collapse. It is genuinely contestable — a fast-growing economy with real macro fundamentals, a governance scandal of first-order severity, and a live debate about whether the institutional failure negates or merely complicates the growth story. Reasonable people looking at the same facts draw opposite conclusions. That is exactly where opinion belongs.

---

Paraguay spent years selling a compelling story to foreign investors: low taxes, fiscal discipline, a booming agricultural export base, GDP growth running at nearly twice the regional average, and an investment-grade sovereign rating that its neighbors could only envy. The story was largely true. It is now colliding, violently, with a second truth that was always lurking beneath the surface — that robust macroeconomic numbers and rotten institutional governance can coexist for a surprisingly long time, right until the moment they cannot.

The collapse of Ueno Bank is not a garden-variety bank failure. When a mid-sized digital lender goes under, the story is usually contained: depositors are protected, the guarantee fund absorbs the hit, the system moves on. What transforms the Ueno case into a first-order crisis is the staggering concentration of public money inside a single private institution whose directors were simultaneously on the receiving end of its own loans. Itaipú Binacional had roughly USD 163 million frozen there. The Instituto de Previsión Social — the pension fund responsible for paying retirees — had up to USD 355 million deposited, up from barely USD 20 million not long before. ANDE, Petropar, Senave, and the Caja Fiscal of the Finance Ministry itself all had funds trapped. The Finance Ministry filed a criminal complaint to recover its own money. The state, in other words, was not a passive victim of a private sector fraud. It was an active, massively overexposed creditor of an institution it was simultaneously supposed to be supervising.

That is not bad luck. That is a governance failure of the first order, and it demands being named as such. The Banco Central del Paraguay's belated reaction — acknowledged even by the Asociación de Bancos — allowed a concentration of risk that any functioning supervisory framework should have flagged and curtailed years earlier. The question of how the IPS's exposure grew from USD 20 million to USD 355 million without triggering an internal alarm, a regulatory intervention, or a congressional inquiry is not a technical detail. It is the central question. When the answer is finally excavated, it will almost certainly involve a combination of political connections, supervisory capture, and the particular variety of cronyism that thrives in economies where growth is real enough to suppress scrutiny. Paraguay's political economy has historically been permeable to exactly this kind of arrangement: strong aggregate numbers providing cover for the allocation of rents to insiders. The Ueno case is that dynamic taken to its logical conclusion.

Moody's warning about fiscal and sovereign risks is being read by the government and by Asoban as an external threat to be managed through communication. It should instead be read as a diagnostic. The rating agencies are not inventing problems; they are pointing at a genuine structural weakness that the growth narrative had allowed investors to discount. Those investors — including the Brazilian entrepreneurs whose interest in Paraguay reportedly cooled following the Ueno revelations — are now doing what markets do when institutional confidence evaporates: they reprice. The guaraní becoming the world's worst-performing currency against the dollar in this episode is not a panic reaction detached from fundamentals. It is the market integrating, belatedly, information about the quality of the institutions that underpin those fundamentals.

None of this means Paraguay's real economy is a fiction. A 5.7% cumulative expansion through August, IMF projections of growth at nearly double the global average, and a tax and fiscal framework that remains meaningfully more competitive than its neighbors — these are facts, not government talking points. The tragedy of the Ueno collapse is precisely that it is squandering a genuine competitive advantage. A country with Paraguay's growth profile and its relatively low tax burden should be attracting capital at this moment, not watching its currency crater and its sovereign bonds get flagged by rating agencies. The cost of the governance failure is being paid not just by the 1.2 million depositors who needed advance payments from a guarantee fund now consuming half its total resources, but by every Paraguayan exporter, every pension beneficiary, and every foreign investor who had begun to take the country seriously.

The incoming economy minister Óscar Lovera, the new BCP leadership, and President Peña — who faces not just a financial crisis but impeachment pressure — will now have to do something that is genuinely difficult: separate Paraguay's macroeconomic story from the Ueno story in the minds of international creditors, while simultaneously conducting a credible investigation into how public funds of this magnitude ended up concentrated in an insolvent bank run by people with loans from that same bank. That is not a communications problem. It is a governance reconstruction problem. And the experience of every country that has gone through something similar suggests that the reconstruction is slower, more painful, and more politically costly than the initial crisis it follows.

Markets do not punish growth. They punish growth without accountability. Paraguay is learning that distinction the hard way.
