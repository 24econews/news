> TITLE: Brazil's fiscal talk crumbles when fuel subsidies extend alongside austerity admissions.
> DATE: 2026-07-27

Brazil's government is telling the market it believes in fiscal consolidation. On the same day it extended a fuel subsidy, it admitted it needs to cut spending. The market, charging 8% real yields on inflation-linked bonds out to 2037, is not convinced — and it is right not to be.

The Diesel Economic Subvention Program extension signed by Finance Minister Dario Durigan last Thursday encapsulates everything structurally dishonest about Brazil's current economic management. Durigan's justification was internally coherent in the narrowest accounting sense: higher oil prices generate additional Petrobras revenue, which finances the subsidy. Fine. But on that same day, the same minister told an interviewer that a hypothetical Lula 4 government will need to cut mandatory spending and tackle tax benefits to stabilize the debt trajectory. You cannot simultaneously argue that the fiscal framework is credible and that it depends on commodity windfalls to sustain consumer subsidies. The market noticed the contradiction. The NTN-B curve, with real yields above 8% at virtually every maturity through 2037, is essentially the bond market's written verdict on that incoherence — and Santander analysts, who say they have never seen anything analogous in 18 years covering that market, are not known for rhetorical excess.

Let us be precise about the numbers, because the scale matters. Gross public debt reached 81.1% of GDP in May, according to the Central Bank. The 30-year U.S. Treasury is trading near 5.16%, its highest level since 2007, compressing the relative attractiveness of emerging market paper at precisely the moment Brazil needs foreign appetite for its debt. The IMF's Article IV, released Thursday in an unusually warm tone, praised Pix and acknowledged economic resilience — but still found it necessary to urgently recommend that the Central Bank be granted financial and budgetary autonomy, an institutional gap that remains unresolved. When the Fund, in a constructive mood, still flags structural vulnerabilities, the structural vulnerabilities are real. Meanwhile, the Independent Fiscal Institution estimated that the Propag program alone could reduce federal revenues by R$190.7 billion over 30 years — a liability the 2026 electoral debate, as columnist Solange Srour has rightly noted, has treated with deliberate silence.

The fuel subsidy is not the largest number on the table, but it is the most symbolically damaging because it exposes the government's revealed preferences under pressure. Every time oil rises, the political reflex is to absorb the shock fiscally rather than allow price signals to transmit. This is exactly the logic that, when applied at scale during the 2011-2014 Rousseff years, contributed to the fiscal collapse that produced the 2015-2016 recession. Sergio Vale of MB Associados made precisely that comparison when assessing the R$18.5 billion Brasil Soberano 3 package announced in response to U.S. tariffs — noting the structural parallel with post-2008 stimulus programs that prioritized short-term demand management over the supply-side adjustments the economy actually needed. Stimulus that papers over competitiveness deficits does not solve them; it merely delays the reckoning while accumulating debt.

The U.S. tariff shock is real, and the government's decision not to retaliate is the correct one — credit where it is due. Vice President Alckmin's restraint and Lula's stated willingness to keep the negotiating table open preserve options that a retaliatory posture would foreclose permanently. But the R$13.5 billion in Treasury-funded credit lines that accompanied that restraint is the government speaking from both sides of its mouth: fiscally responsible at the diplomatic window, fiscally expansionary at the credit window. And the timber sector, which has openly declared it has no plan B for the American market, will not be saved by subsidized credit lines. It needs market diversification, which requires the kind of structural trade openness that Brazil's political economy has resisted for decades.

The diagnosis is not complicated, even if the politics are. Brazil is a country paying 8% real yields on its own inflation-linked debt, running gross public debt above 81% of GDP, with a Correios losing R$8.5 billion in 2025 alone and a government that responds to every external shock with a new credit program. The National Treasury may want to tell a story about a path back to investment grade. That story requires a government that stops extending fuel subsidies on the same day its finance minister admits the spending structure is unsustainable. Until those two things are reconciled, the NTN-B market will keep writing its own, more honest, assessment.
