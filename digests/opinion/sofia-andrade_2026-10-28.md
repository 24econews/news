> TITLE: Will Lula's Return Make Petrobras a Fiscal Instrument Again?
> DATE: 2026-10-28

Petrobras sits at the intersection of every tension that Lula's return to the Palácio do Planalto now concentrates. The market's verdict was delivered without ambiguity: Petrobras ADRs fell nearly 20% in pre-market trading on the NYSE when the result became clear, and the company shed R$59.2 billion in market value since Friday, according to press reports. The state-run oil company's CEO called the move speculative, arguing the company's fundamentals do not justify the decline. The market, at least in the short term, disagrees — and the market's logic is not irrational.

To understand why, you have to read Petrobras not as a single company but as the focal point of a recurring cycle in Brazilian resource governance. Every time a government with expansionist social instincts controls the presidency, the same questions resurface: Will dividend policy be redirected toward state coffers? Will fuel pricing be decoupled from international benchmarks to manage domestic inflation? Will capital allocation shift from deep-water production to refinery expansion or petrochemical ventures with questionable returns? These are not hypotheticals invented by foreign fund managers. They are patterns with documented history in Brazil, which is precisely why Bradesco BBI cut its recommendation on Brazilian equities from overweight to neutral on Sunday night, explicitly citing the need for concrete progress on the fiscal agenda.

The timing is particularly charged. Oil has structurally reshaped Brazilian exports in a way that would have seemed implausible a decade ago. The trade surplus in oil and derivatives reached US$31.22 billion between January and September this year, up 40% from the same period in 2025 and a historical record for the interval, according to Valor Econômico. That figure amounts to half of the country's total trade surplus. Petrobras produced 2.96 million barrels of oil equivalent per day according to ANP data. Brazil has overtaken Saudi Arabia as China's second-largest supplier of crude oil in the first nine months of 2026. These are not marginal facts; they define Brazil's external accounts, and by extension the government's fiscal room for maneuver.

This is where the commodity cycle logic becomes central. When oil revenues are this large, the temptation to treat a state oil company as a fiscal instrument — rather than a value-maximizing enterprise — is at its most acute. The Lula administration's electoral package already reached R$356.3 billion in fiscal impact, according to Pinotti & Schwartsman, with R$88.5 billion added since September alone. Fitch Ratings was blunt: regardless of Sunday's outcome, Brazil will see tighter fiscal policy in 2027. Yet the campaign gave no credible commitment on the primary surplus; press reports said Lula went so far as to call the obsession with surpluses nonsense. Into that fiscal gap, Petrobras's dividend stream becomes an obvious temptation.

If the government pursues a strategy of using Petrobras to cross-subsidize fuel prices or redirect dividends toward social spending, the consequences for the company's investment cycle would be severe at precisely the wrong moment in the global commodity curve. The offshore pre-salt fields require sustained capital expenditure to maintain production. The Braskem restructuring — with roughly US$11 billion in debt under negotiation and Petrobras as a key shareholder — adds another axis of pressure on the state company's balance sheet. BTG Pactual estimates that in the five most recent periods of strong foreign capital inflows, Petrobras alone attracted roughly R$18 billion, nearly a third of total flows into the market. That magnetic role in foreign investor positioning will be difficult to sustain if governance uncertainty persists.

The choice of finance minister is the most immediate test, and its implications for Petrobras are direct. According to Folha de S.Paulo and Valor Econômico, Piauí Governor Rafael Fonteles leads the shortlist. The choice of a fiscal conservative who credibly signals arm's-length treatment of state enterprises would partially decouple Petrobras's trajectory from the worst-case governance scenarios. A minister perceived as amenable to using the company as a fiscal buffer would almost certainly push long rates toward the levels Alfredo Menezes of Armor Capital is projecting — at least 100 basis points above current levels — and compress the valuation case for the stock further.

There is also an energy policy dimension that extends beyond Petrobras. The end of the 6x1 work schedule, which takes effect December 15, directly affects the oil sector: according to press reports, the oil sector with 70,000 workers in special offshore regimes was among the first to raise concerns about legal uncertainty. Labor cost increases in capital-intensive offshore operations are not trivially absorbed. An FGV Ibre study cited in press reports estimates the new workweek could raise labor costs by up to 20%. In an industry where project economics depend on stable operating cost assumptions, that figure complicates investment planning regardless of who holds Petrobras's chairmanship.

The broader investment-incentive question is where Brazil most visibly diverges from the RIGI-style logic Argentina has been deploying to attract capital to Vaca Muerta. Argentina approved three Vaca Muerta projects under the RIGI regime in a single week, drawing substantial investment commitments. Brazil's pre-salt is the larger prize, but the institutional framework surrounding it is generating exactly the kind of uncertainty that sends capital elsewhere. Resource nationalism debates are not new — they cycle through every commodity boom — but Brazil is entering a Lula fourth term at a moment when global competition for energy investment capital is fiercer than at any point in the past decade. India is seeking to turn diplomatic commitments into concrete business deals in Brazil's critical minerals space; MMG is advancing on Anglo American's nickel assets; Capstone Copper is committing US$3 billion to Chilean copper. Capital has options.

The fiscal plan the government signals in its first weeks will determine whether this is a managed correction or a sustained repricing of Brazilian resource-sector assets. A credible spending-containment package, combined with a finance minister who treats Petrobras as a commercial entity rather than a fiscal instrument, could recover significant ground. The unified VAT rate under the tax reform is expected to be announced shortly, potentially offering a vector of regulatory rationality. But the campaign record is unambiguous, and markets price behavior, not intentions. Lula won. The question now is which version of resource governance accompanies that victory.
