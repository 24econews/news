> LENS: Market-liberal / fiscal conservative
> TITLE: Lula's Win Doesn't Solve Brazil's Inexorable Fiscal Crisis
> DATE: 2026-10-28

The runoff result settles the election. It does not settle Brazil's fiscal arithmetic.

Lula won 51.79% of valid votes to Flávio Bolsonaro's 48.21%, a margin of 4,430,191 votes in the TSE's official count — reversing the first-round result and triggering the Ibovespa's steepest single-session drop since March 2020. The Ibovespa plunged 13.88% on Monday, the real depreciated as much as 12% in futures markets, and the National Treasury canceled its scheduled bond auctions and stepped into the market with an extraordinary R$7.1 billion buyback paying yields above 15% on 2029 paper. The EWZ fell 19.82% on the day. BTG Pactual projected that long-term real yields could exceed 8% — against a historical average of 6.7% during Lula's third term. The TSE's count is certified and the result is final.

Let me be clear about what the market was really pricing: it was not pricing in Flávio Bolsonaro's character or his politics. It was pricing in the possibility of a fiscal inflection. That bet is now off the table. What remains on it is an inheritance the re-elected government cannot escape — a package of electoral measures press reports put at R$356.3 billion in fiscal impact, of which R$88.5 billion were added in September alone. Gross debt was already hovering near 82% of GDP, the highest since the pandemic. The government's own projections, reported before the vote, placed the debt trajectory approaching 90% of GDP by 2029. Those numbers don't change with the ballot count.

This is the core problem with the mandate question. Markets and commentators will debate what Lula's re-election "means" for reform. But Fitch Ratings gave the clearest answer available: regardless of Sunday's outcome, fiscal tightening in 2027 is not ideological — it is arithmetic. The budget sent to Congress was crafted, according to Valor Econômico, with overestimated revenues and underestimated expenditures. Former Finance Minister Mailson da Nóbrega and tax reform architect Bernard Appy told Valor that there is no way to avoid adjustment in 2027 regardless of who won. Even Felipe Salto at Warren Investimentos, who cautioned against market euphoria over a Bolsonaro win, acknowledged that fiscal stabilization requires an immediate plan with only gradual effects. That observation applies with equal — arguably greater — force to the government that actually won.

The primary surplus trajectory is where the mandate is being tested in real time. During the campaign, according to press reports, the president went so far as to dismiss the obsession with primary surpluses as nonsense. That statement now belongs to the record of a re-elected president. It will be quoted every time the government tries to signal fiscal credibility, and it will cost something in yield premiums. Alfredo Menezes at Armor Capital projected a rise of at least 100 basis points in long rates upon the result. JP Morgan downgraded its recommendation on the real and government bonds from overweight to marketweight. Goldman Sachs acknowledged that little will change in the trajectory of public accounts. These are not ideological readings — they are the market's assessment of the gap between stated preferences and fiscal arithmetic.

What happens next depends entirely on choices press reports explicitly frame as unresolved. According to reporting in Folha and Valor, the president-elect's team was already preparing, before the polls closed, a constitutional amendment that would allow the new government to target the center of the fiscal rule and freeze additional budget resources if necessary, and signal a reduction in subsidized credit. Planning Minister Bruno Moretti signaled that the government will reopen discussions with Congress on legislation enabling it to aim at the midpoint of the fiscal target rather than the lower bound. If that amendment is designed credibly and the government genuinely commits to it, it could represent a material softening of the market shock. That is a scenario, not a settled outcome — and the credibility of such a move bumps directly into the electoral record of the past two months.

The Finance Ministry appointment is the most consequential near-term signal. Folha and Valor report that Piauí Governor Rafael Fonteles leads the shortlist, with Vice President Geraldo Alckmin also circulating as an alternative. A former Treasury Secretary quoted by Valor was emphatic: action is needed before year-end, not in the new year. Jeferson Bittencourt at ASA assesses that adjustments to the 2027 budget proposal will be the key piece for any credible course correction. The market will not wait for inaugural speeches.

There is one genuine structural positive in the short-term landscape: the tax reform is moving forward. The Receita Federal and the IBS Steering Committee established 25 working groups the day after the election to coordinate implementation. The TCU is reviewing the CBS reference rate. A Roit study warns that revenue mismatches could tie up corporate working capital for up to 63 days during the transition — a real liquidity pressure — but the reform itself represents the only vector of regulatory rationality that is politically immune to the election result. It passed; it is being implemented; it continues regardless.

Here is the honest assessment from someone who wanted to see a more credible fiscal path: the market's pre-election positioning was excessive, and a Bolsonaro win would not have been the fiscal magic the rally implied. Salto said as much before the result. But Lula's win, combined with an electoral record of R$356 billion in commitments and explicit hostility to surplus discipline, means the adjustment that is arithmetically inevitable will now arrive later, more painfully, and with less institutional credibility than it needed to. The Congress composition — with the ruralist caucus now firmly in opposition — narrows the coalition available for any reform that requires supermajorities. If the government pursues the fiscal amendment Moretti signaled, it will need to pay in political coin for every vote it assembles from an opposition that has no incentive to make the next four years look easy.

The Selic has not been cut. Long rates have blown out. The real cratered. Gross debt is approaching 90% of GDP by the government's own projection. The re-elected government inherits all of this with a mandate built on promises that point in the opposite direction. Whoever the finance minister turns out to be, they are walking into a room where the numbers are already on the wall.
