> LENS: Regional sovereigntist
> TITLE: Mercosur's Fatal Flaw: No Architecture to Stop Financial Capture
> DATE: 2026-11-13

The story that demands a column isn't Brazil's fiscal drama or Argentina's inflation print. It's Paraguay — and specifically what the Ueno Bank collapse reveals about the structural vulnerability of small Mercosur economies when their institutions are captured by politically connected capital, and when the region has no collective architecture to respond.

---

The numbers from Asunción this week read like a confession that no one in the region's financial capitals wanted to hear. Ueno Bank's non-performing loan ratio jumped from 0.10% to 14.49% in the period before intervention. The Deposit Guarantee Fund will exhaust half its total resources covering the fallout. Itaipú Binacional — the binational hydroelectric engine that generates a substantial share of Paraguayan state revenue — had over USD 163 million frozen inside a single digital bank. The Caja Fiscal, the pension fund for public employees, reportedly committed the equivalent of USD 60 million to the same institution. The former president of the Banco Central del Paraguay has been arrested. A prosecutorial team entered the central bank itself to investigate. And through all of this, the guaraní posted the steepest depreciation of any currency in the world at the moment the presidential resignation was announced.

This is not a story about one rogue bank. It is a story about what happens when an economy grows at nearly double the global average — the IMF projected 5.7% expansion through August — but builds that growth on an institutional foundation too porous to withstand the weight of politically connected capital. The Vázquez group didn't simply mismanage a bank. It systematically captured state resources across Itaipú, Yacyretá, Senave, Petropar, and Conatel, apparently leveraging political relationships that nobody in the supervisory chain was willing to interrupt. As analyst Manuel Ferreira put it with appropriate bluntness: "They stole a bank." And they did so, it appears, while regulators watched for ten months before acting.

The regional dimension of this episode deserves far more attention than it is receiving. The collapse of Ueno Bank is not Paraguay's problem alone. Brazilian entrepreneurs who had intensified investment interest in Paraguay following Lula's reelection are now stepping back. Itaipú Binacional is a joint Brazilian-Paraguayan entity — which means Brazilian state resources are entangled in this scandal too. Uruguay's central bank president felt compelled to publicly reassure markets about Uruguayan financial system soundness precisely because Ueno's reach extended across borders. And according to reporting from La Diaria, the criminal network allegedly laundering money through Ueno Bank includes associates of drug trafficker Sebastián Marset — a reminder that the corridors of weakly supervised regional finance are not merely a governance problem but a security one.

What Mercosur lacks — and what this crisis exposes with surgical precision — is any collective supervisory architecture capable of flagging these failures before they metastasize. We have spent three decades building a bloc whose principal achievement this year has been finally ratifying a trade agreement with the European Union. That is not nothing. But we have no regional mechanism to monitor the financial integrity of institutions that hold binational funds. We have no cross-border early warning system for the kind of portfolio deterioration that Ueno was apparently concealing. We have no shared deposit guarantee framework that could have cushioned the guaraní's collapse. The Mercosur we built was designed to lower tariffs, not to prevent politically connected fraud from hollowing out a member state's financial system.

The IMF's response deserves particular scrutiny. Moody's warned of "governance weaknesses" but expressed confidence in an "orderly transition." Standard & Poor's indicated the political crisis doesn't alter Paraguay's rating — for now. The Economy Ministry rushed to insist investment grade "is not in danger at this moment," a temporal qualifier that markets duly noted. What none of these institutions said is that Paraguay's macroeconomic strength and its institutional fragility have coexisted for years in plain sight, and that the analytical frameworks deployed by external creditors systematically underweighted the governance risk precisely because growth numbers were strong. This is the same pattern that produced Argentina's 2001 collapse, that produced Bolivia's current devastation. High growth and weak institutions are not a stable equilibrium. They are a countdown.

Paraguay has a right to rebuild its own institutions on its own terms. What it does not have — what no small economy in this region has — is the luxury of doing so in isolation, without regional solidarity, without collective supervision, without a Mercosur that functions as something more than a trade negotiating bloc. The new BCP board is talking about restoring credibility. The Economy Ministry is submitting a USD 276 million budget cut under the banner of a "war economy." These are necessary steps. They are not sufficient ones.

The Ueno crisis is an invitation — perhaps the most urgent one this bloc has received in a decade — to ask what regional integration is actually for. If Mercosur cannot respond collectively when one of its members suffers a governance failure of this magnitude, when binational assets are frozen, when the regional currency market is destabilized by institutional capture, then the bloc is an administrative convenience for trade flows, nothing more. That is not sovereignty. That is managed vulnerability, sold to us as integration.
