> LENS: Social-democratic / pro-redistribution
> TITLE: Paraguay Confused Growth Statistics With Building Supervisory State
> DATE: 2026-11-10

The Ueno Bank scandal in Paraguay is not primarily a story about one failed fintech. It is a story about what happens when a state outsources its institutional memory to growth statistics and forgets to build the oversight architecture that makes growth durable.

Look at the facts as they stand. The Instituto de Previsión Social — the institution responsible for paying pensions to Paraguayan workers — had up to USD 355 million deposited at a single digital bank tied to a private business group. The Itaipú binational entity, the world's largest hydroelectric generator by annual output, had USD 163.8 million frozen there. The Ministry of Economy's own Caja Fiscal held the equivalent of roughly USD 60 million in the same institution. Yacyretá, Petropar, Senave: all exposed. These are not reckless private actors chasing yield. These are the custodians of public money, of pension contributions, of the revenue that flows from one of Latin America's most strategically valuable energy assets. And they were all, somehow, concentrated in a single mid-sized fintech whose resolution procedure is now consuming 50 percent of Paraguay's entire Deposit Guarantee Fund.

The reflexive defense from markets and from Asunción sounds familiar: the fundamentals are solid, the IMF projects near-double the global average growth rate, the rating agencies are holding steady. S&P Global said the political transition does not alter the sovereign rating. Moody's called it an "orderly" process. And it is true that Paraguay has earned its macroeconomic reputation — low inflation, sustained growth, fiscal prudence relative to its neighbors. That record is real and should not be dismissed. But here is the problem with leading every conversation about Paraguay with its growth statistics: GDP expansion does not, by itself, generate the supervisory capacity to catch a scheme in which 27 individuals, including the former president of the central bank, allegedly participated in hollowing out a financial institution that had extended its tentacles into every corner of the public sector. Those are different things. One measures output. The other measures whether the state can protect its citizens and its own resources from politically connected actors who understand, with great precision, where the oversight gaps are.

The Asociación de Bancos praised the speed of the deposit guarantee response while simultaneously criticizing the BCP's belated reaction. That is a generous framing for what is, in substance, a supervisory failure of the first order. The IPS did not accumulate USD 355 million in a single institution over a few months by accident. ABC Color reported the figure climbed from barely USD 20 million in a relatively short period. That trajectory — an institution linked to a private business group absorbing an ever-larger share of public pension savings, with no apparent regulatory intervention until the edifice collapsed — is not a technical oversight. It is a governance failure with a name and a political economy behind it. And Paraguay's social-democratic neighbors, and indeed its own workers who will now wait longer for pensions and year-end bonuses, are bearing the cost of that failure.

What makes this episode genuinely instructive for the broader Mercosur region is the particular danger it illustrates: the illusion that macroeconomic stability is a substitute for institutional construction. Bolivia's crisis demonstrates what happens when fiscal populism and resource nationalism are allowed to run unchecked for decades. Brazil's current turbulence shows the cost of allowing fiscal frameworks to erode while social programs are defended without the revenue base to sustain them. Paraguay's version of the same underlying problem is subtler and, in some ways, more insidious: the country has been fiscally disciplined while simultaneously failing to build the regulatory and supervisory state that is the other half of what makes markets function and workers' savings safe. Growth without oversight does not produce development. It produces exposure.

The Chamber of Deputies suspended its review of the 2027 budget. The new economy minister, Óscar Lovera, inherits a fiscal picture that must be entirely reformulated in light of a crisis the previous administration did not prevent. Twenty-seven people have been indicted. The Comptroller has recommended terminating a 5G contract tied to the same group. Businesses in the Vázquez ecosystem are reporting sales collapses of 70 percent, with closures imminent. The guaraní became, in the space of days, the worst-performing currency in the world. These are not the symptoms of an external shock. They are the symptoms of a state that grew its economy while neglecting to grow its institutions at the same pace.

The workers of Paraguay — the ones whose pension fund is now frozen, whose year-end bonuses are delayed, whose contributions accumulated over decades are caught in a resolution procedure of uncertain duration — did not make this choice. They deposited their trust in institutions that were supposed to protect them. The lesson is not that Paraguay should grow less. It is that growth, without the institutional architecture to distribute and protect its proceeds, is not a social-democratic project. It is an opportunity for those who already have power to accumulate more of it. That is exactly what appears to have happened here, and no GDP figure changes that arithmetic.
