> LENS: Agribusiness specialist / pragmatic
> TITLE: Paraguay's Growth Mirage: Strong GDP Hides Collapsing Border Revenues
> DATE: 2026-10-06

The guaraní is doing exactly what you'd expect from a currency backed by strong soy export flows and a central bank content to let it appreciate: it's rising. Paraguay's exchange rate against the dollar has been tightening steadily for weeks, making the country one of Latin America's top currency performers this quarter. On paper, that's a mark of macroeconomic health. In practice, for a country whose entire growth model depends on agricultural commodity exports, it's a slow-acting poison — and the numbers on customs revenue are already showing the first symptoms.

Customs receipts have fallen 9.1% year-to-date, according to data reported by ABC Color, even as the Banco Central del Paraguay projects GDP growth near 6.6% and the economic activity indicator shows 5.6% expansion through July. That divergence is not a statistical quirk. It reflects a structural feature of Paraguay's export economy that the investment-grade narrative consistently glosses over: when the guaraní strengthens and commodity margins compress simultaneously, the revenue base shrinks faster than the headline growth rate suggests. Agricultural prices climbed 13% in international terms this year, but production costs rose in parallel, compressing farmer margins. A stronger currency magnifies that compression on the local-cost side while doing nothing to improve it on the revenue side. The farmers are earning less in guaraníes per dollar of soybeans sold, and the state is collecting less at the border per unit of goods crossing it. The BCP's monetary policy rate hike, which is pushing domestic financing costs upward at exactly this moment, adds a third vector of pressure on the productive sector.

Fitch's decision to keep Paraguay at BB+ with a positive outlook — deferring investment grade for another cycle — is being read in Asunción as a patience problem, a question of rating agencies taking their time. That reading is dangerously complacent. Former central bank president Benigno López put the real issue more accurately: agencies assess institutional quality alongside macroeconomic fundamentals. Former Finance Minister Manuel Borda was blunter still, identifying debt, deficit, and corruption as the three factors blocking the upgrade. The 2027 budget currently under parliamentary scrutiny is not reassuring on any of those fronts. Specialists are flagging a widening deficit, an expanding public debt profile, a 9.9% jump in central government payroll spending, a 12.9% increase in debt interest payments, and a reduction in productive investment in favor of current expenditure. The MEF placed PYG 230.527 billion in Treasury Bonds domestically this week, which signals the financing machine is running, but the IMF's list of eleven pending tasks for the country — corruption, fiscal deficit, money laundering — is not a list that a single bond placement addresses.

Here is the specific contradiction that deserves more scrutiny than it has received: Paraguay is simultaneously benefiting from and being constrained by its own commodity dependence in a way that the investment-grade conversation obscures. The guaraní strengthens because soy export flows are robust. Robust soy flows inflate the headline growth number. The headline growth number supports the BB+ narrative. But inside that loop, customs revenue is falling, the underground economy accounts for between 40% and 47% of GDP by DNIT's own estimates, the formal tax burden sits at 10% of GDP against a target of 12%, and DNIT's director is promising to close that gap with an entity that has just seen a 9.1% drop in its main revenue stream. The Cámara Nacional de Comercio warned explicitly this week that the EU-Mercosur agreement — Paraguay's most-cited long-term bet — will not translate into real investment without a domestic competitiveness agenda that does not yet exist. That warning should be taken seriously, because it points to the central weakness of a growth model that runs on commodity flows and multilateral goodwill but has not built the institutional infrastructure to convert either into durable industrial or export diversification.

The decision to study eliminating tax exemptions on electric and hybrid vehicle imports is a small but instructive case study. The DNIT is floating the elimination of an incentive precisely when import volumes in that segment are rising, which means the fiscal motivation is obvious and the policy coherence is questionable. A country serious about building a competitive manufacturing base — one that might eventually give it something to export to the EU beyond soybeans and beef — does not send mixed signals on green industrial policy at the same moment it is courting foreign investors with promises of regulatory stability. These are the kinds of institutional decisions that Fitch and Moody's actually do read, and that Borda and López are referring to when they say the institutional quality assessment is what's holding the rating back.

Paraguay's macroeconomic management under Santiago Peña is meaningfully better than what the region has delivered on average, and the structural decision to merge tax and customs administration into DNIT is a genuine reform with long-term revenue potential. But the path to investment grade runs through closing the gap between headline growth and fiscal reality, not through waiting for rating agencies to catch up with a narrative that customs data is already quietly undermining. A 9.1% drop in border revenues during a year of 5-6% GDP expansion is not a rounding error. It is the signal that the growth model's fiscal transmission mechanism is broken, and fixing it requires institutional changes that the 2027 budget, as currently structured, is not delivering.
