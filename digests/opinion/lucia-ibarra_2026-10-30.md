> LENS: Regional sovereigntist
> TITLE: Paraguay's Paradox: Growth Thrives While Treasury Bleeds From Mercosur's Monetary Powerlessness
> DATE: 2026-10-30

The story the headlines don't want you to read this week isn't Brazil's market rout — dramatic as it is. It's Paraguay, where a country posting the fastest growth in Latin America is simultaneously watching its Treasury bleed out, its finance minister resign, and its business sector take to the streets. But even Paraguay, for all its paradoxes, is a symptom of something larger: the persistent vulnerability of Mercosur's smaller economies to the monetary decisions and market sentiments of powers they have no voice in shaping.

Look at what happened in Asunción this week. The guaraní — led by robust soybean export flows and a favorable interest rate differential — became Latin America's best-performing currency. Under any standard reading, this should be cause for celebration. Instead, it triggered an emergency. Ten business chambers demanded urgent meetings with the Ministry of Economy. The Intergremial de Empresarios announced protests outside the ministry's doors. Banana producers reported direct losses. Cattle ranchers began lobbying for financial instruments to stabilize the exchange rate. And the outgoing finance minister, Carlos Fernández Valdovinos, left office with a phrase that deserves to be carved into the wall of every regional finance ministry: "The economy is fine. The Treasury is not."

That sentence is the distilled essence of a trap that repeats itself across Mercosur's smaller economies generation after generation. Paraguay's fiscal revenues are denominated in guaraníes, but a decisive share of taxable economic activity — soybeans, beef, agro-industry — is transacted in dollars. When the dollar falls, the guaraní-denominated value of those revenues collapses with it, even as macroeconomic aggregates look enviable on paper. The Ministry of Economy's own tax authority, the DNIT, warned explicitly that the 14.3% nominal increase in 2027 revenue projections it is promising may be eroded before the year even begins. Legislators pressed the MEF in budget hearings on precisely this gap, and received no satisfactory answer, because there is none within the current framework.

This is not primarily a story of Paraguayan mismanagement. It is a story about the structural subordination of small open economies to exchange rate dynamics they do not set and cannot control. Paraguay's guaraní strengthens not because Asunción chose to tighten monetary policy for development reasons, but because short-term capital flows respond to interest rate differentials in a global financial system calibrated to the needs of the Federal Reserve, the European Central Bank, and, to a lesser extent, Brazil's own Selic rate — which itself, as this week's catastrophic post-election collapse demonstrates, is set under conditions of permanent anxiety about New York's reaction. The smaller economies of the region absorb these shocks as pure weather.

What makes Paraguay's case especially instructive — and especially maddening from a regional sovereignty perspective — is the contrast with what the country is genuinely building. Itaipú's energy surplus, long underutilized and long the subject of asymmetric treaties that favored Brazil's needs over Paraguay's, is now being repurposed as the foundation for an artificial intelligence hub, with U.S. firms Araico and Crusoe paying between USD 48.91 and USD 56.39 per megawatt-hour for access. ANDE projects revenues of up to USD 10 million per month once fully operational. The Bioceanic Route Bridge is in its final stretch. The country's international reserves exceed USD 11 billion. By any measure of productive potential, Paraguay in 2026 is a country with real assets and real leverage. And yet it cannot stabilize its own fiscal position because the dollar's trajectory is set in Washington, and its revenues are hostage to that fact.

This is precisely the argument for regional monetary coordination that Mercosur has debated, shelved, resurrected, and abandoned for three decades. A common exchange rate buffer mechanism, even a partial one, would allow Paraguay's fiscal authorities to hedge against the kind of revenue volatility that just forced a finance minister out of office and pushed the Caja Fiscal to its lowest levels since 2009. Deeper regional trade invoicing in local currencies would reduce the dollar-dependence that makes guaraní appreciation simultaneously a macroeconomic success and a fiscal disaster. These are not utopian proposals — they are instruments that ASEAN economies have used for years, that the Gulf states deploy through their currency pegs, and that Brazil itself has flirted with when its own interests were at stake in bilateral trade.

The IMF, to its credit, visited Asunción and produced a list of eleven pending structural reforms. What the Fund's report does not contain — because it never does — is any mechanism for insulating Paraguay from the monetary spillovers of decisions made in Washington or São Paulo. The IMF's toolkit is national. The problem is regional and systemic.

Paraguay does not need lectures from multilateral creditors about fiscal discipline. It needs, and deserves, regional partners willing to build the monetary architecture that would make its own hard work less vulnerable to forces entirely outside its control. That is what Mercosur, at its most serious, was always supposed to be about. This week's events in Asunción are a reminder of how far the bloc still is from delivering it.
