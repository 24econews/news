> PERSONA: Mariana Coelho
> LENS: Agribusiness specialist / pragmatic
> TITLE: Argentina's Oil Boom Masks a Dangerous Squeeze on Global Food Security
> DATE: 2026-11-17

The numbers coming out of Banco do Brasil are arresting, and the political theater in Brasília over the Moretti plan has consumed most of the analytical oxygen this week. But the most genuinely contestable agri-trade story sits not in Brazil's Center-West but in Buenos Aires — specifically in the data point that crude oil has dethroned soybean meal as Argentina's top export.

That displacement deserves more serious interrogation than it has received. Between January and September 2026, Argentine crude exports reached USD 7.753 billion, up 58.6% year-on-year and six times the value recorded five years ago. Soybean meal — a product that has anchored Argentina's foreign-exchange earnings, employment base, and crushing industry since the turn of the century — came in second at USD 6.971 billion. The shift is being read in some quarters as evidence that the Milei government's deregulation agenda is working, that Vaca Muerta is finally delivering on its geological promise, and that Argentina has found a new export engine to replace its dependence on volatile agricultural commodity cycles. The same reporting period shows total agri-industrial exports surpassing 101 million tons over ten months, a thirteen percent increase and an all-time record. So the story, at first glance, looks like unambiguous good news.

It isn't. Or rather, it is complicated in ways that matter operationally for anyone thinking about food security and regional agri-trade.

Start with the price effect. Brent crude has averaged twenty-three percent above the prior year, lifted by the closure of the Strait of Hormuz following the US-Iran conflict that began in February. Vaca Muerta's output is real and growing, but the revenue figures flattering the crude column in Indec's tables are substantially a price windfall, not purely a volume story. Soy crushing margins, by contrast, have been under structural pressure: the multilateral real exchange rate that the Central Bank places at Milei-era lows — described by economist Lucas Llach as the lowest unified-market level since 2001 — is compressing the competitiveness of the agro-industrial complex precisely as the peso is held as an exchange-rate anchor. The soy complex is not collapsing, but it is operating with a currency that makes Argentine meal progressively less attractive relative to Brazilian competitors whose real has depreciated sixteen percent since Lula's reelection. The gap between the two producing giants in effective export competitiveness is widening, and the data on Argentine car exports to Brazil — down 7.8 percent in the first ten months of the year — shows that exchange-rate misalignment has sectoral consequences that extend beyond agriculture.

The food-security implication is worth spelling out. Soybean meal is a protein input for global livestock and aquaculture supply chains. Argentina is the world's largest exporter. When Argentine crushing margins tighten, the response over time is not simply lower profits for Rosario's industrial complex; it is reduced incentive to crush domestically, which eventually shows up in global protein-meal availability and prices. The oilseed processing industry requires sustained capital investment to maintain throughput; that investment does not flow toward sectors operating at real exchange rates the market has already flagged as unsustainable. The energy sector, by contrast, benefits from RIGI incentives, fiscal exemptions, and a pricing reference — Brent above USD 100 — that is explicitly tied to a geopolitical shock rather than to structural demand growth. Treating these two export columns as equivalent indicators of productive health is an analytical error.

The governance risk compounds the operational risk. Los Grobo Agropecuaria, one of the emblematic names in Argentine soy farming, has just transferred its entire grain trading operation to Brazilian group Amaggi under bankruptcy proceedings. That a firm billing in the billions has been liquidated in this fashion is not incidental. It reflects a cycle in which the Argentine soy complex took on leverage during commodity supercycles and is now being restructured during a period of currency overvaluation and credit tightening — the same dynamic, at a different scale, that is producing the delinquency crisis at Banco do Brasil with midsize soy producers in Mato Grosso. The lesson is structural: commodity farming cycles punish leveraged operators with particular brutality when the currency and credit environment turns simultaneously.

None of this means Vaca Muerta is a mirage or that the government is wrong to accelerate energy investment. The TGS infrastructure projects under RIGI, the Toyota plant, the first gas export agreements with Brazilian firms — these are real capital formation events with decade-long payoffs. The argument here is narrower: the displacement of soy meal by crude at the top of Argentina's export table should not be read as a structural portfolio upgrade. It is a price-cycle artifact occurring against a backdrop of real exchange rate overvaluation that is quietly eroding the competitiveness of the agro-industrial sector that feeds the world's protein supply chains. When Brent normalizes and the RIGI projects are still four years from their first export shipment, the question of what Argentina's foreign-exchange earnings look like will once again come down to the soy complex — and to whether that complex has been maintained, invested in, and kept competitive during the years the crude column looked so flattering.

The record agri-industrial export volumes are genuinely encouraging. But volume records achieved at compressed margins and under a currency regime the market itself is pricing as unsustainable are not the same thing as a healthy agri-export sector. Food security analysts tracking Southern Cone protein supply should be watching the Argentine crushing industry's capital allocation decisions over the next eighteen months, not the crude export headline.
