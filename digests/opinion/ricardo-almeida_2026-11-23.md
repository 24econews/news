> TITLE: Paraguay's Fiscal Virtue Was Always Regulatory Theater
> DATE: 2026-11-23

The story that most deserves a sharply argued opinion column this week isn't Brazil's fiscal theater, Argentina's bond rout, or Chile's copper curse. It's Paraguay — specifically the Ueno Bank collapse, and what it reveals about a country that wore the badge of regional fiscal virtue while its regulatory apparatus was being quietly hollowed out.

This is the most genuinely contestable story in the material: Paraguay had real macroeconomic achievements to point to, real growth numbers, real institutional reforms — and yet a catastrophic supervisory failure unfolded in plain sight, implicating state-owned entities, the central bank itself, and the binational hydroelectric giants. Reasonable people can disagree about how much blame falls on the government versus inherited dysfunction, and about whether the DNIT tax merger and ministerial consolidation represent genuine reform or cosmetic reshuffling. That's the column.

---

Paraguay built a reputation as the region's quiet overachiever — low taxes, open trade, a currency that didn't implode every decade, and growth rates that made its neighbors look incompetent. The Ueno Bank collapse has just handed that reputation to a shredder. The question worth asking now isn't merely how a single institution accumulated a $1.5 billion hole. It's how a country that lectures the region about fiscal discipline allowed state entities — Itaipú, Yacyretá, the IPS pension fund — to park public money in a bank that didn't meet its own internal eligibility criteria, while the regulator watched, and in some cases apparently helped.

The numbers are damning on their own terms. The Banco Central del Paraguay confirmed a preliminary shortfall of up to $1.5 billion at the intervened institution. The Ministry of Economy acknowledged that at least $888 million in public funds is directly at risk — equivalent to roughly two percentage points of GDP in a country whose fiscal deficit had already reached 2.3% of GDP through October. The Chamber of Deputies passed a 2027 budget projecting a 3.9% deficit, a figure the private sector calls unsustainable and that one former finance minister described as evidence that "investment grade went to the economic leadership's head." These are not the fiscal coordinates of a model economy. They are the coordinates of a country that mistook low tax rates for sound governance.

What makes the Ueno scandal structurally significant rather than episodically scandalous is the institutional fingerprint it leaves behind. An internal Itaipú document required five years of market track record for any institution receiving its deposits — Ueno didn't qualify. The BCP's own Superintendent of Banks and its chief legal officer have been arrested. The new Itaipú director signed the resolution enabling the deposits and claims ignorance. A director at Yacyretá deposited $10 million at a bank in which he was simultaneously a shareholder, and the unions shielded him. This is not a rogue operator who slipped through the cracks. This is a regulatory and governance failure that implicates multiple layers of the Paraguayan state at once. The outgoing economy minister's parting line — "the economy is fine, the Treasury is not" — will stand as one of the more candid admissions of institutional compartmentalization in recent regional memory.

The market-liberal case for Paraguay was always grounded in a specific argument: low tax burdens and open trade create conditions for private dynamism, and the state's role should be limited and competent. The first part of that bargain held. Paraguay's tax burden is among the lowest in South America, its maquila regime attracted real investment, and its IMF-projected growth rate remains the fastest in the region. But the second part of the bargain — a limited state that is nonetheless professionally managed — collapsed the moment it became clear that the BCP was not competent to supervise the institutions it licensed, that the binational hydroelectric entities operated as political fiefdoms rather than professionally run funds, and that the finance ministry's projection of investment grade status was more aspiration than anchor. Free markets require referees. Paraguay's referees were on the field playing for the wrong team.

The incoming Economy Minister Óscar Lovera's pivot from "war economy" to "intelligent austerity" is the right rhetorical move, but rhetoric doesn't recover $888 million in committed public funds or reconstitute central bank credibility after the arrest of its own legal director. The real test of the Peña government's reformist credentials will not be the ministerial nomenclature it uses, nor the DNIT tax merger — however technically sound that reform may be — but whether Paraguay can demonstrate, before 2027 begins, that it has built a supervisory architecture capable of catching the next Ueno before it becomes a systemic event. The Fitch affirmation of systemic stability is a floor, not a ceiling. Sovereign bond markets have already started repricing the ceiling downward.

Paraguay had the rarest asset in South American economic policy: a track record of doing the boring things right. Low public debt, stable currency, no hyperinflation episodes, genuine foreign investor interest. Squandering that asset through supervisory capture and state-entity recklessness is not a technical mishap. It is a political failure dressed in bureaucratic clothing. The region should take note — not to gloat, but because the lesson generalizes: fiscal conservatism without institutional integrity is not conservatism. It's just a tax rate with no enforcement behind it.
