> PERSONA: Ricardo Almeida
> LENS: Market-liberal / fiscal conservative
> TITLE: Argentina's Export Boom Masks a Domestic Economy Collapsing Under Reform
> DATE: 2026-08-31

Argentina has been accumulating the most genuinely contestable story in this week's material — not because of its size, but because of a tension that reasonable people really do read differently: an export and macroeconomic framework that is working by almost every conventional metric, sitting on top of a domestic economy where nearly a third of borrowers are in default, formal employment is falling, and consumption has contracted for six consecutive months. That coexistence — celebrated abroad, disputed at home — is the opinion-worthy question. The Brazil coverage, while voluminous, is largely a catalogue of predictable consequences: high Selic, corporate stress, fiscal drift. Paraguay's phantom debt and Bolivia's subsidy crisis are real stories, but both have been ably covered by other columnists in this space recently. Argentina's two-speed economy is the sharpest analytical puzzle of the week, and it deserves a direct answer rather than a both-sides shrug.

---

The numbers coming out of Argentina this week are, depending on which set you read, either a vindication of Javier Milei's program or an indictment of it. Both readings are wrong — or rather, both are selectively right in ways that obscure the only question that actually matters: is this stabilization credible enough, and fast enough, to survive the social pressure it is generating before the electoral cycle absorbs everything?

Start with what is genuinely working, because intellectual honesty requires saying so plainly. The Central Bank ran 22 consecutive sessions of net FX purchases in August, accumulating more than USD 14 billion since January. Gross reserves sit at roughly USD 49.8 billion, the highest of the Milei cycle. Oil production hit 916,200 barrels per day in July — a record — with Vaca Muerta contributing 70% of the total, and crude exports generated USD 5.7 billion in the first seven months of the year, up nearly 60% year-on-year. The San Matías Pipeline just closed a USD 900 million syndicated loan arranged by Citi, JP Morgan, Santander, and Itaú under a seven-year project finance structure, which is not the behavior of banks that think Argentina is headed off a cliff. The RIGI now has 24 approved projects with USD 56 billion in committed investment. IDB Invest put USD 700 million behind Posco's lithium operation in Salta and Catamarca. These are not paper victories. International capital markets are voting with actual money.

Now read the other set of numbers. Nearly 5.8 million borrowers — close to a third of all Argentine debtors — are at least three months behind on payments, according to Equilibra's data as reported by the Financial Times. Personal loan delinquency reached 16.4% in June. Industrial sector non-performing loans multiplied more than fivefold in eighteen months, from 0.7% to 3.8%, affecting roughly 4,300 companies. Mass consumption fell 3.7% year-on-year in the second quarter, with Greater Buenos Aires down 6.8%. Fuel sales posted their worst year-on-year drop in two years in July. Stellantis is cutting Córdoba production to a single shift; Granja Tres Arroyos, once the country's largest poultry operation, shuttered with workers fearing they won't collect severance. The UBA's research institute has documented the loss of 241,000 formal private-sector jobs since November 2023 and a cumulative 40.5% fall in the real minimum wage. Nearly seven in ten young people between 18 and 25 are inactive, unemployed, or informally employed.

The standard libertarian response to this divergence is that it is temporary and structural: the export sector is flourishing because it has been freed from the distortions of currency controls and overregulation, and the domestic consumption squeeze reflects the unavoidable unwinding of a decade of monetary expansion that destroyed real purchasing power. That argument is not wrong. It is, however, incomplete as a policy stance, and it is becoming politically unsustainable at the speed the data is moving. Former BCRA president Hernán Lacunza — a figure of impeccable market-liberal credentials — did not say "leaving so many people by the roadside is not an option" because he has gone soft on fiscal discipline. He said it because he understands that an adjustment program that generates a permanent extraction economy — exporting dynamism, absorbing destitution — will eventually produce a political backlash that undoes the adjustment itself. Lacunza is not wrong about the macroeconomics; he is right about the political economy.

What this week's data makes clear is that the Milei government has a narrowing window. The deferred fuel tax increase — postponed again, this time in full, to October 1 — and the mortgage program funded through ANSES reserves are signs that the economic team knows the social tension is real and is trying to address it through instruments that do not require touching the fiscal surplus, the exchange rate, or monetary emission. Miguel Kiguel's EconViews called the mortgage scheme "one of the few stimulus policies that doesn't require touching the sacred cows," which is exactly right as a description and slightly worrying as a compliment. A government that can only stimulate the domestic economy through instruments that are structurally incapable of scale — USD 2 trillion in UVA mortgage auctions against a consumption deficit measured in tens of percentage points — is a government whose domestic toolkit is nearly exhausted. The BCRA charter reform, which the lower house passed with 144 votes and which now awaits the Senate, permanently prohibits financing the Treasury through money issuance. Good. Essential, even. But it does not by itself generate formal employment, and the 2027 electoral calendar will not wait for Vaca Muerta's full multiplier effects to propagate through the domestic labor market.

The case for Milei's program remains stronger than its critics allow and more fragile than its supporters admit. The external and resource sectors are delivering. The fiscal anchor is holding. The monetary reform, if the Senate passes it, will be genuinely durable. But an adjustment that produces a 40% fall in the real minimum wage, 5.8 million borrowers in distress, and youth informality approaching 70% is not delivering what stabilization is supposed to ultimately deliver: a foundation for broad-based growth. International capital will keep flowing to Vaca Muerta and the lithium triangle regardless of what happens to Argentine domestic consumption. The political coalition that keeps the adjustment program viable will not.

The honest market-liberal position here is not to cheer the export numbers and dismiss the consumption collapse as a necessary transition cost. It is to acknowledge that a two-speed economy — one plugged into global supply chains, one structurally excluded from the recovery — is not a market success story. It is a market failure with some impressive commodity data attached to it. Milei has done the hard, correct, politically costly work of breaking Argentina's monetary addiction. Whether he can translate that into an economy that works for people who are not in the energy sector is the question that will determine whether this stabilization is a turning point or just the most internationally legible version of Argentina's recurring cycle.
