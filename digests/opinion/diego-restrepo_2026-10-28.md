> PERSONA: Diego Restrepo
> LENS: Techno-optimist / critical of hype
> TITLE: Fiscal Crisis, Not Politics, Will Define Brazil's Tech Future
> DATE: 2026-10-28

Lula's fourth term is, from a technology policy standpoint, the dog that didn't bark — and that silence is itself the signal worth reading.

Let me be direct about the limits of my angle this week: press reports contain essentially nothing about Pix, digital-banking regulation, open finance, or AI-sector policy in the context of this election. That is not a gap in my knowledge; it reflects where the market's attention actually was. Every analyst, fund manager, and ratings agency in these seven days of coverage was laser-focused on the fiscal trajectory, interest-rate path, and the identity of the next Finance Minister. Tech-sector policy didn't register as a primary variable in any of the reporting. Drawing on statistics from outside this week's coverage to fill that gap would be dishonest. So I'll do what the evidence actually supports: build the tech-sector argument from the fiscal and monetary facts on the ground, because those facts will determine the operating environment for every fintech, deeptech investor, and digital-bank executive in Brazil for the next four years.

Start with the market reaction, because it tells you everything about the baseline. The Ibovespa fell 13.88% on Monday, October 26, in its worst single session since March 2020. The National Treasury canceled its bond auctions and executed an emergency buyback at yields above 15% on 2029 paper. BTG Pactual projected that long-term real yields could exceed 8% and stay there — against a historical average of 6.7% during Lula's third term. The real depreciated as much as 12% in futures markets before settling. These are not abstract numbers for the tech sector: they are the cost of capital. Every startup seeking a Series B, every digital bank pricing its credit book, every deeptech fund calculating IRRs runs directly through this rate environment. When long real yields spike toward 8%, venture multiples compress, growth-company valuations fall, and the patient capital that early-stage tech requires becomes dramatically more expensive to raise or deploy.

The Selic already sat at historically elevated levels before the election. According to press reports, household consumption growth had decelerated sharply — from 5.1% in the fourth quarter of 2024 to just 0.9% in the second quarter of 2026. The Tesouro Prefixado 2032 retreated from 14.15% before the first round to 12.89% at Friday's close, leaving it exposed to a full reversal. Bradesco's 90-day delinquency rate reached 4.6% by the third quarter. These are the conditions inside which every Brazilian fintech's credit portfolio operates right now. The payment-infrastructure layer — Pix, instant payments, open finance — sits on top of a credit economy that is under serious stress.

This matters for a distinction that tech commentary often blurs: infrastructure is not the same as credit. Pix as a payment rail is largely indifferent to who governs; the pipes work regardless. But digital banking — the business of extending credit through those pipes — is acutely sensitive to the Selic and to delinquency cycles. And AI adoption in financial services, which Brazilian banks have been pursuing aggressively (Itaú Unibanco's R$2.925 billion acquisition of NeoSpace, announced the day after the election, is the clearest single signal of that commitment in this week's coverage), requires the kind of long-horizon capital allocation that is harder to justify when rates are spiking and CFOs are on the phone with their board about circuit breakers. Itaú moving on NeoSpace during the worst single market day in years is notable precisely because it signals that at least one major institution is looking past the immediate shock — but it is one data point, not a trend.

The fiscal overhang is the structural problem. The coverage documents an electoral spending package that the consultancy Pinotti & Schwartsman estimated at R$356.3 billion in cumulative fiscal impact, of which R$88.5 billion were added in September alone. Fitch, according to the reporting, was blunt: tighter fiscal policy in 2027 is inevitable regardless of who won, driven by arithmetic rather than ideology. That tightening, when it comes, will be delivered through some combination of higher revenues and compressed expenditures — and regulatory bandwidth in a fiscal-adjustment environment narrows. Ministries prioritize spending cuts and revenue measures. Structural regulatory agendas — open finance frameworks, AI governance standards, regulatory sandboxes for fintech — require sustained political attention that is harder to sustain when the Finance Ministry is in crisis management mode.

The choice of Finance Minister is the single most important near-term variable for this assessment. Press reports name Piauí Governor Rafael Fonteles as the leading candidate, with Geraldo Alckmin circulating as an alternative, according to Folha de S.Paulo and Valor Econômico. A former Treasury Secretary was quoted in Valor calling for action before year-end. If the government moves quickly and credibly on the fiscal package — the reporting mentions a constitutional amendment that would allow targeting the center of the fiscal framework — then long rates stabilize faster, the cost of capital recedes from panic levels, and the operating environment for digital finance becomes manageable again. If the fiscal signal is slow or unconvincing, the rate environment stays punishing and tech-sector investment continues to reprice downward.

If the government pursues the fiscal consolidation path it signaled even before the results were certified, the scenario for Pix and digital banking is continuation with elevated cost-of-capital headwinds. If instead the spending impulse of the campaign period persists into 2027, expect fintech credit portfolios to deteriorate further and institutional AI investment to decelerate. The infrastructure layer will survive either way; the credit and investment layer will not.

One genuinely positive structural note worth registering: tax reform is moving forward regardless. Press reports describe 25 working groups established by Receita Federal and the IBS Steering Committee the day after the election, with the unified VAT rate expected to be announced within days. Regulatory rationalization in the tax system is, over the medium term, good for fintech and digital commerce — it reduces compliance overhead and creates cleaner infrastructure for embedded financial services. That agenda appears insulated from the political turbulence around the election result.

The bottom line for anyone in tech watching this result: the re-election doesn't change the architecture of Brazil's digital financial infrastructure, and it doesn't reverse the institutional momentum behind open finance or AI adoption in banking. What it does is raise the cost of capital, extend the credit stress cycle, and create fiscal uncertainty that narrows regulatory bandwidth. Brazil's tech sector has repeatedly demonstrated resilience through adverse macro cycles. It will again. But it will do so in a tighter environment than the market was pricing three weeks ago, and anyone who built their 2027 investment thesis on the assumption of falling rates and fiscal consolidation needs to rebuild it from scratch.
