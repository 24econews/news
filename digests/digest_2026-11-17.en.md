# Argentina: The Economic Pulse — 2026-11-17

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-11-17*

Argentina's country risk closed Monday at 855 basis points, its highest level in weeks, after the MSCI Merval index dropped 5.3% in pesos and YPF fell 5.9% on Wall Street to USD 35.65, its lowest price since March. Argentine ADRs returned to 2026 lows in a session that combined external pressure with domestic signals complicating the electoral horizon. The question dominating today's economic and financial debate is no longer whether the stabilization program works, but whether it is enough to carry the country through to the October 2027 presidential elections without an episode of FX stress derailing it. Today, that question has more uncomfortable answers than it did three months ago.

The international backdrop worked against Argentina. The governance crisis at Nvidia—whose stock fell 4.1% after its board ousted Jensen Huang as CEO, with quarterly results due Tuesday—dragged the Nasdaq down 2.5% and the S&P 500 down 1.9%, generating risk aversion that hit all emerging markets. For Argentina, pressure also came from the south: the dollar in Brazil climbed another 2% to 5.78 reais, accumulating a 16% rise since Lula da Silva's surprise reelection, which triggered a 13.88% drop on the São Paulo exchange in the first session following the vote. The real's depreciation erodes Argentina's relative competitiveness and, as Infobae noted, is already putting pressure on the multilateral real exchange rate, though for now without significant pass-through to the official dollar, which rose just five pesos to $1,575 during the session.

That, precisely, is the main asset the economic team has on display. In an environment where the Brazilian real collapsed, the Argentine peso barely moved, the Central Bank continued buying FX, and the stock market's caución rate normalized around 20.6% annualized without rate intervention. Minister Luis Caputo, speaking on Radio Mitre, declared that the country is entering 2027 "stronger than ever" and that next year's financial program is "fully closed," also anticipating that investments under the Large Investment Incentive Regime (RIGI) will rise from USD 8 billion in 2026 to roughly USD 15 billion next year. However, that official narrative faces direct challenges. Economist Fernando Marull noted that the economic slowdown began before the external shock, and Carlos Melconian publicly argued that the program "has a ceiling": stirring the pot was necessary but not sufficient, and the question of how the movie continues remains without a visible answer.

What complicates the 2027 picture is not just the political noise. IERAL of Fundación Mediterránea calculated that the Treasury and the Central Bank must face maturities of between USD 28 and 29 billion next year, with an estimated shortfall of USD 17 billion once currently available funding sources are factored in. The BCRA's pace of reserve accumulation fell by more than half between August and October compared with the average for the first seven months of the year, and the institute warned that the government let pass windows of capital market access when country risk was below 500 basis points, an opportunity that today, at 855 points, looks distant. Morgan Stanley, in its weekly Latin America outlook, warned that pre-electoral portfolio dollarization could weaken the peso and interrupt disinflation even if the policy framework remains intact, projecting year-over-year inflation of 21.7% by the end of 2027. JP Morgan, for its part, estimates that the country will reach the elections with inflation still running around 2% monthly.

October's inflation print, which surprised consensus at 2.2% when 1.8% to 1.9% was expected, reopened the stagflation debate. Core inflation fell to 1.5%, its lowest level in 15 months, and the government clung to that figure. But economists like Miguel Kiguel and Orlando Ferreres warned that inflation inertia remains elevated—"we've been near 2% for almost 24 months and it isn't coming down," Kiguel summarized—and that November won't be a comfortable month either. October's acceleration coincided with declines in industry, construction, retail, and auto registrations, generating the backdrop that fuels the stagflation debate. Meanwhile, UCA's Observatorio de la Deuda Social published methodologically adjusted estimates that practically wipe out the poverty reduction shown by official statistics, and a Zentrix survey—which has already angered President Milei—again found that 67.7% of Argentines run out of income before the 20th of the month.

In contrast with that picture of macro fragility, the real-investment newsflow was robust. Toyota confirmed at a meeting at Casa Rosada with President Milei that its new Zárate plant will produce the hybrid Tacoma pickup starting in 2030, with an investment of more than USD 1.3 billion under RIGI and projected export capacity of USD 1.28 billion annually. TGS received approval for two RIGI projects worth USD 3.245 billion for natural gas liquids infrastructure in Vaca Muerta, slated to begin operating in 2030 and contribute some USD 1.1 billion annually in exports. San Juan has accumulated private investment projects worth USD 22.4 billion, with four mining ventures approved under RIGI accounting for more than USD 22.1 billion of that total. And crude oil, driven by Vaca Muerta and Brent above USD 100, has already displaced soybean meal as the country's top export, with foreign sales of USD 7.753 billion between January and September, up 58.6% from a year earlier. Agro-industrial exports, meanwhile, surpassed 101 million tons in ten months, an all-time record with a 13% year-over-year increase.

The government also modified the RIGI rules through Decree 1366/2026, eliminating the requirement that local supplier firms have at least 51% Argentine capital: now it will suffice to be incorporated and domiciled in the country, regardless of shareholder origin. The measure broadens the universe of eligible suppliers but creates tensions with domestic SMEs that see the 20% local sourcing quota being opened to subsidiaries of multinationals. A second RIGI Round is scheduled for December 2 at the Hipódromo de San Isidro, with a focus on oil, gas, and energy, and more than 2,000 supplier firms are already expected. The mass-consumption M&A cycle—Molinos acquired Tía Maruca, Newsan picked up Reckitt's home care brands, De Narváez and Manzano bought Dia and Burger King, and CMPC sold its Argentine forestry assets to the Faro Verde group for USD 170 million—paints a picture of accelerated consolidation of the local market under domestic capital, partly precipitated by an imminent regulatory change in competition policy.

What to watch closely in the coming weeks is the November inflation print—which several economists anticipate will not be low—the evolution of country risk amid heightened global volatility linked to the AI sector and U.S. midterm elections, Brazilian FX dynamics and their potential pass-through to Argentina's real exchange rate, and the third FGS funding auction of $200 billion scheduled for November 17, which aims to expand mortgage credit. The gap between the official narrative on 2027 and what Argentine bonds are pricing in has never been wider.

## Corporate Watch

### Toyota Motor Corporation (NYSE: TM / TSE: 7203)
Toyota confirmed at Casa Rosada that its new Zárate plant will manufacture the hybrid Tacoma pickup starting in 2030, with an investment of more than USD 1.3 billion under RIGI and projected export capacity of USD 1.28 billion annually, primarily destined for South America, Central America, and the Caribbean; the project represents the country's first automotive RIGI and the largest single investment in Argentine industrial history.

### Los Grobo Agropecuaria / Amaggi
Los Grobo, the company in bankruptcy proceedings founded by Gustavo Grobocopatel, transferred the entirety of its grain trading operation—eight owned storage facilities, positions in seven other leased plants, and nearly one hundred employees—to Brazilian group Amaggi, which bills around USD 8.5 billion annually; the agreement, subject to court approval, will run for 24 months from January 2027 with a fixed fee of USD 398,326 monthly plus VAT and a variable component, and all grain purchasing operations will now be conducted under the Brazilian firm's tax ID.

### Molinos Río de la Plata / Newsan
Molinos Río de la Plata, owned by the Pérez Companc family, acquired the Tía Maruca cookie brand in Argentina and certain regional countries, reinforcing its bet on the cookie segment following its recent purchase of an industrial complex in Tucumán; in parallel, Newsan agreed to acquire VEH Argentina and VEH MFG Argentina—which operate brands such as Procenex, Air Wick, and Woolite, previously held by Advent International following its purchase of UK-based Reckitt's Essential Home division—with transfer scheduled for the first quarter of 2027, consolidating the trend of local capital absorbing assets from multinationals retreating from the Argentine market.
