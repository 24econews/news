# Argentina: The Economic Pulse — 2026-10-14

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-10-14*

The BCRA bought $291 million in the FX market on Tuesday, its largest daily intervention since late July, even as JP Morgan's country risk index simultaneously edged toward 600 basis points. The coexistence of both data points — accelerated reserve accumulation and persistent pressure on sovereign spreads — captures with precision the central tension defining Argentina's economy today: an external balance improving at record pace, weighed down nonetheless by an international financial backdrop that refuses to let up.

With Tuesday's purchase, the BCRA has now accumulated more than $15.2 billion in 2026, a figure already exceeding by more than 50% the $10 billion annual target agreed with the IMF. Gross reserves climbed to $49.839 billion, the highest level since mid-September. Analysts at Cohen Aliados Financieros noted that the recent acceleration does not reflect a regime change but rather the availability of supply, fed by the settlement of corporate and sub-sovereign negotiable obligations. The wholesale dollar closed at 1,512.50 pesos, its lowest in four weeks, while the official exchange rate has risen just 4% so far in 2026 against inflation of nearly 24% year-to-date, leaving the BCRA's multilateral real exchange rate index around 24% below its historical average — the most severe appreciation gap since the final years of the convertibility regime, according to market analysts.

That currency backdrop accompanied the release of September inflation data: INDEC reported a monthly print of 1.9%, two-tenths above August but in line with the Market Expectations Survey consensus. Core inflation held at 1.7%, while seasonal and regulated items — driven by gas and electricity tariffs and the behavior of vegetables — pushed up the headline figure. With this, the year-on-year rate ticked down by just one-tenth, to 33.4%, and the nine-month cumulative stands at 23.6%. The market reaction was muted: the Merval rose 0.5% in pesos, Argentine ADRs on Wall Street posted gains of up to 3.6% in IRSA while sovereign bonds lost an average of 0.2%, and country risk stabilized at 597 basis points, hovering around the psychological 600 threshold that it only barely managed to break away from last week on the back of the Bolsonaro effect in Brazil.

For the government, September's 1.9% is, in the words of presidential spokesman Adrián Ravier, a good number. The underlying goal is to bring year-on-year inflation down from the current 33.4% toward the 20% range ahead of the October 2027 midterm elections, taking advantage of the statistical effect of an ever-higher base of comparison. Fernando Marull estimates October could return to 1.7%, the year's low, though CyT Consultores projects another 1.9%. The key will be the evolution of food prices: tomatoes rose 34% in September in Greater Buenos Aires, but lettuce fell by double digits, reflecting a volatile seasonal mix.

In Bangkok, Minister Luis Caputo met with Kristalina Georgieva on the sidelines of the IMF and World Bank annual meetings. The Fund's managing director described Argentina's fiscal discipline as "exemplary in the world, despite global shocks," praise the minister circulated on X before confirming that technical teams will continue working to close the third program review. At stake are $869 million in pending disbursements, but the review has yet to reach a staff-level agreement: the first half showed a primary surplus of 6.29 trillion pesos, below the 6.86 trillion target. In parallel, the IMF published its World Economic Outlook, cutting its growth projection for Argentina to 2.1% in 2026 from 3.5% in April, while raising its average annual inflation forecast to 32.5%. The Fund's new chief economist, Tucumán-born Silvana Tenreyro — the first Argentine to hold the post — warned that global resilience is reaching its limit, in a world where advanced-economy deficits are approaching their highest levels since the pandemic and 10-year Treasury yields are near 24-year highs, around 5.25%.

It is that external context, rather than local fiscal dynamics, that explains why Argentina's country risk has risen more than 170 basis points between July and early October, a deterioration which, according to economist Miguel Ángel Broda, was surpassed over the same period only by war-torn Ukraine. Citi's chief economist for Argentina, Ricardo Dessy, noted that the fiscal adjustment "raised the walls of the castle" but recommended letting the exchange rate rise somewhat to relieve the hardest-hit sectors. The debate over the exchange rate level, which Milei brought to Cabinet itself with a presentation by former Goldman Sachs chief economist Alberto Ades, remains open and unresolved.

The weakness of domestic demand is the other side of the picture. Formal private employment fell for the fourteenth consecutive month in July, with more than 100,000 formal jobs lost so far this year, and manufacturing industry has accumulated the destruction of more than 66,000 jobs over two years. Delinquency on bank consumer portfolios reached 14.7%, more than double a year ago, and close to 26.5% of non-bank credit is in arrears. Against this backdrop, Domingo Cavallo publicly endorsed a critical analysis of the economic program that denounces over-adjustment, anemic investment, and excessive dependence on a handful of export sectors — energy, mining and agriculture — to sustain the trade surplus and FX flow.

What is advancing without pause is the export front. Agro-industrial exports totaled 88.9 million tons in nine months, surpassing the 2021 annual record, according to the Rosario Board of Trade, on the back of the best harvest on record and unprecedented external demand. Lithium exports reached $1.467 billion between January and August, 191% more than in the same period of 2025, and Catamarca will inaugurate four plants this month with combined investments of $2 billion. From Texas, some thirty companies will arrive next week in Vaca Muerta in search of partnerships. The horizon projected by private consultants, combining energy, mining, agriculture and the knowledge economy, puts Argentine exports at up to $180 billion by 2035 — a scenario that, in 2023, when the country accounted for just 0.29% of world trade, would have sounded implausible.

The variables to monitor in the coming days are clear: the evolution of country risk relative to global sovereign debt yields, the formal closing of the third IMF review and the disbursement of the $869 million still pending, the October inflation print — which will determine the December pension adjustment — and the October 21 legislative session, where the ruling party will try to advance the 2027 Budget while the opposition presses to debate DNU 70. Carry trade remains the year's most profitable strategy in dollar terms, with Boncer yielding 23.2% measured in CCL, suggesting the market still trusts nominal stability. But country risk at 597 points is a reminder that such confidence does not come for free, and that the margin for negative surprises has narrowed.
