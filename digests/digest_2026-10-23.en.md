# Argentina: Economic Pulse — 2026-10-23

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-10-23*

Argentina's country risk broke above 700 basis points this week for the first time since October 2025, and although it pared back partially to 674 points by Thursday's close, the signal left by markets is more serious than the number itself: Argentine equities measured in dollars touched exact one-year lows, erasing the entire post-midterm recovery of 2025, while August's EMAE confirmed that economic activity has been stagnant for five months and that the third quarter will almost certainly close in technical recession. The convergence of an adverse external shock with a domestic economy that fails to lift off is testing, for the first time in stark terms, the political sustainability of Javier Milei's program heading into the 2027 presidential election.

The international backdrop was the trigger of the week's most volatile session. The Nasdaq shed 2.6% on doubts over AI financing costs, with Meta falling 7% and Nvidia 4.4%, while Brent crude climbed to USD 113 per barrel on Middle East tensions. That combination triggered a flight to safety into US Treasuries that drove the ten-year yield from 5.1% to 4.94%, a move that under normal circumstances would have provided relief to emerging markets. In Argentina's case, however, it was not enough: Globales gave up 0.5% on average and JP Morgan's country risk touched 710 points intraday, a twelve-month high. The problem, as flagged by Max Capital and the Adcap research team, is that the Argentine spread is decoupling from its B-rated peers by around 265 basis points, reflecting a growing idiosyncratic component that goes beyond global volatility.

That idiosyncratic component has a name: the real economy is not responding. August's EMAE posted a year-on-year drop of 1.7%, the largest of the year, with eleven of sixteen sectors in the red, led by manufacturing (-3.2%) and commerce (-3.5%). The seasonally adjusted reading showed only a 0.1% rebound after July's 2.8% collapse, insufficient to prevent the third quarter from closing in contraction, given that September would have needed to grow an impossible 8.5% month-on-month to avoid it. The trend-cycle indicator, the Government's preferred gauge, has shown no change since April. Supermarket sales have strung together eight consecutive months of year-on-year decline, with a 3.7% drop in August, and mass consumption as measured by Scentia fell 1.2% year-on-year in September, with wholesalers down 3.4%. The only relevant exception was e-commerce, up 30.5% year-on-year.

The tension between these data and the official narrative was laid bare at two simultaneous forums. At the Buenos Aires Insurance Summit, Finance Secretary Federico Furiase assured that the 2027 financing program "is already closed," that there is more than USD 125 billion in ammunition to defend the exchange rate, and that "there will be no exchange rate or interest rate volatility in the election year." At the same event, economist Ricardo Arriazu — one of the most closely heard voices by the Government — again questioned monetary policy with a cutting remark: "We should be growing above 5% and we are stuck below 2%; the explanation is monetary policy." His argument is technically precise: the Central Bank is accumulating at a pace of USD 1.1 billion in October, a monthly record, but is doing so by absorbing pesos without an equivalent increase in local currency demand, which destroys domestic credit and depresses activity. Former deputy minister Joaquín Cottani, even more blunt, said that "the program is exhausted, but there is time to revise it," and economist Miguel Broda called for taking the dollar to $1,750 to prevent the slowdown from compromising the 2027 outcome.

The official exchange rate hit a new nominal high of $1,528 in the wholesale market, with an accumulated October increase of just 0.7%, well behind inflation that has run above 24% year-to-date. The exchange rate gap is hovering around 6%, low in historical terms but trending higher in recent weeks, while the CCL dollar traded around $1,633. ECLAC cut its growth projection for Argentina from 3.3% to 1.9% for 2026, in line with the IMF's prior cut to 2.1%, and the consensus of more than fifty banks and consultancies compiled by FocusEconomics converged on 2.3%.

Against this backdrop, the visit of Under Secretary of State Christopher Landau to Buenos Aires and the formal backing of USD 20 billion in US investment and financing through 2027 — nearly triple the package announced in September — represents the week's most visible counterweight. Landau's meeting with Caputo, alongside presidential adviser Santiago Caputo and Ambassador Alec Oxenford, sought to reinforce the signal of external support. The Treasury also confirmed November 13 as the signing date for the contract selling 90% of AySA to the consortium led by Rowing SA for USD 340.2 million, one of the most tangible privatizations of the ongoing process.

But the week also left contradictory signals about the Government's ability to attract private investment at scale. Grupo México Transportes, controlled by Germán Larrea, announced its withdrawal from the Belgrano Cargas tender, arguing that the open access scheme — which forces the concessionaire to allow third-party operator access in exchange for a toll — is structurally inefficient and has led to bankruptcies in other markets. The Government, which had been warning privately about the possibility of postponing the November 11 bid opening, now faces the question that La Nación framed with precision: why does the capital that applauds Argentina from abroad fail to actually come in? Neither the Hidrovía nor AySA nor Belgrano Cargas attracted major international operators without a local partner or special conditions.

On the energy front, the resignation of Daniel González as Secretary of Energy and Mining Coordination — who will not be replaced, according to sources at the Palacio de Hacienda — closes a cycle in the management of the economy's two most dynamic sectors. His departure coincides with a week in which oil production hit a new all-time record of 973,700 barrels per day in September, up 15.5% from a year earlier, approaching one million. Vaca Muerta contributed 71% of the total. Aluar inaugurated phase V of its wind farm in Chubut with 582 MW of installed capacity, and Toyota received formal approval of its RIGI project for USD 1.341 billion to produce a full HEV hybrid pickup in Zárate, the largest investment in the history of Argentina's automotive industry. Argentina's 2026 paradox is captured in those numbers: records in the export sector, stagnation or retreat in everything that depends on domestic consumption.

What the market will be monitoring in the coming weeks is whether the Government decides to recalibrate monetary policy — easing peso absorption or allowing a greater slide in the exchange rate — before third-quarter activity data confirm what analysts already take as inevitable. The US Supreme Court's decision on whether to accept review of the YPF case, with the deadline for Argentina's response extended to December 2, adds another front of uncertainty to the financing program. And September's activity reading, due in November, could be the trigger that forces an answer to the question that Arriazu, Cottani, and Milei himself are formulating in different ways: how long can a macro stabilization be sustained without the real economy regaining traction before voters head to the polls?
