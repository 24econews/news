# Paraguay: The Economic Pulse — 2026-11-13

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-11-13*

The collapse of Ueno Bank has ceased to be exclusively a domestic financial crisis and has become a test of institutional credibility with direct implications for Paraguay's sovereign financing costs — and the outcome of that test is being settled in real time.

The scale of the collapse is difficult to overstate. According to the Banco Central del Paraguay (BCP), the Deposit Guarantee Fund disbursed USD 644 million in the first week of intervention, reaching 1.8 million affected savers. Nearly 1,200,000 of them have already received their funds, and the BCP confirmed that the credit process reached 100% of beneficiaries entitled to the guarantee, although technical blockages persist in some cases. The cost of this operation will consume roughly 50% of the Deposit Guarantee Fund's total resources — a figure that immediately reveals both the scale of the problem and the fragility of the systemic cushion available for future shocks.

What sets the Ueno case apart from an ordinary bank failure is the reach of the state's tentacles inside the intervened institution. Itaipú Binacional, the hydroelectric plant that generates a substantial share of Paraguayan state revenue, had USD 163.8 million and G. 115.2 billion deposited at Ueno Bank, and has already filed a criminal complaint against its executives to recover the funds. Yacyretá announced similar actions. State telecommunications company Conatel transferred G. 70 billion to the bank barely a month before its collapse, prompting the opening of proceedings against Nubicom — a firm linked to the controlling shareholder conglomerate — for allegedly unauthorized share sales. The Comptroller General's Office recommended terminating the 5G network rollout contract awarded to Nubicom, describing the situation as "a very evident potential risk," and referred its report to the Public Prosecutor. The Caja Fiscal, the pension fund for public employees, reportedly placed funds equivalent to USD 39 million under data that Economy Minister Óscar Lovera described as false, putting that exposure at risk. The Instituto de Previsión Social (IPS) is considering using G. 784.239 billion of its own reserves to cover current-year pension payments given the freezing of its deposits, and has confirmed that the entity's Investments director held meetings with President Santiago Peña on the matter — a revelation that adds political fuel to the crisis.

The criminal investigation is advancing rapidly. The Public Prosecutor's Office opened three criminal cases based on complaints that reach former BCP president Carlos Fernández Valdovinos himself and the former Superintendent of Banks, who has been arrested. A prosecutorial team entered the Central Bank. According to Última Hora, the chronology reconstructed by the BCP indicates the entity allowed Ueno to operate for ten months with massive loans to related companies before intervening — which analyst Manuel Ferreira put bluntly: "They stole a bank." The BCP's new board, whose previous members resigned or were removed, stated that its priority is to "recover credibility" and to intervene actively in the foreign exchange market.

The market reaction was sharp. The guaraní posted one of the steepest declines of any currency in the world against the dollar in the period immediately following the collapse, though it later recouped some of the lost ground. Paraguayan sovereign bonds adjusted lower. Moody's warned that the Ueno case "highlights governance weaknesses," though it expressed its expectation of an orderly transition. Fitch, for its part, ruled out signs of systemic contagion and reaffirmed the soundness of the rest of the financial system. The Economy Minister insisted that the investment grade "is not in danger at this moment" — a temporal qualifier that markets did not miss.

The fiscal impact is projected into the 2027 budget. The Ministry of Economy sent to the Chamber of Deputies an addendum to the General Budget Bill with a USD 276 million cut in operating expenses, in what the government labeled a "war economy" stance. Deputies approved the Executive's version with adjustments, though business associations demanded a deeper revision that would eliminate privileges and superfluous spending. Economists warned that the fiscal adjustment will pressure vulnerable sectors and raise the cost of private financing: analysts noted that the Ueno collapse will weigh on future Paraguayan corporate bond issuance in international markets, particularly given that the entity had bonds placed in New York that could lead to lawsuits from foreign creditors.

Against this backdrop, the activity data offer a striking counterpoint. The BCP reported accumulated growth of 5.5% through September, with September itself showing a 3.9% expansion, driven by services and industry. The IMF projects that Paraguay will grow at nearly double the global average, consolidating its position as the fastest-expanding economy in South America. This disconnect between macroeconomic dynamism and institutional vulnerability is precisely what concerns analysts: Paraguay faces the Ueno crisis not from a position of economic fragility but from a governance weakness that sustained growth had kept hidden until now.

What remains to be defined in the coming weeks is whether the ongoing institutional cleanup — the renewal of the BCP board, the criminal investigations, the budget addendum and the creation of a government council to revive companies tied to the bank — will be enough to restore international debt market confidence before Paraguay has to tap those markets. The answer will determine whether the cost of this crisis is measured in months or in years of additional risk premium.

## Corporate Watch

### Ueno Bank
The digital bank taken over by the BCP confirmed that its sound assets exceed its pension liabilities according to the regulator, although the institution has accumulated more than 27 indicted individuals in criminal investigations over massive loans to related companies that led to its collapse; Itaipú Binacional, Yacyretá and the Caja Fiscal are among the largest institutional creditors affected, with combined exposures exceeding USD 200 million.

### Nubicom
The telecommunications firm linked to Grupo Vázquez — a Ueno Bank shareholder and holder of the sole 5G network rollout contract in Paraguay — faces proceedings opened by Conatel over allegedly unauthorized share sales, while the Comptroller General recommended terminating the 5G contract and referred its report to the Public Prosecutor, putting both the concession and the future of the country's digital infrastructure at risk.
