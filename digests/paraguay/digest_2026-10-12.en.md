# Paraguay: Economic Pulse — 2026-10-12

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-10-12*

The guaraní has emerged this week as Latin America's best-performing currency, propelled by robust agricultural export flows, and that currency appreciation—which benefits importers and the State in terms of local-currency denominated debt—is paradoxically creating mounting pressure on industrial sectors that depend on external competitiveness. The motorcycle industry, one of the country's most dynamic manufacturing segments, has publicly escalated its demand for exchange-rate predictability before the Banco Central del Paraguay, warning that the sustained decline of the dollar is eroding margins and making its production relatively more expensive compared to imported competition. It is the classic tension between a commodity-exporting economy that appreciates on the back of its own successes and a local manufacturing base that pays the price of that strength.

That currency dilemma coexists with a macroeconomic picture that, in aggregate terms, remains enviable by regional standards. The World Bank projects 4.7% growth for Paraguay in 2026, above the Latin American average, while the IMF anticipates an expansion that would nearly double the global mean. The economy had already posted a 5.8% expansion in the first quarter of the current year, according to BCP data, though the pace moderated in the second quarter, suggesting that the peak of the agricultural cycle has passed. The soybean complex generated USD 2.492 billion through May, consolidating agriculture as the structural anchor of growth, though analysts insist that the climate dependency of that engine remains the model's main vulnerability. Yacyretá, meanwhile, issued an alert over the rising Paraná River, forecasting high waters through year-end, adding a hydrological variable to the short-term horizon.

The most urgent debate, however, is unfolding not in the markets but in Congress, where the deliberation process on the 2027 General National Budget is exposing the fiscal contradictions that lie beneath the growth narrative. Specialists warn that the projected budget implies a widening deficit, greater indebtedness and a contraction in real public investment, in a context where salaries, pensions and debt service already concentrate nearly 70% of public financing. The National University, in its presentation before the Bicameral Commission, requested an additional USD 61 million for 2027, exemplifying the expansive pressure of current spending. The Ministry of Public Works, meanwhile, faces legislative scrutiny over budget execution for the current fiscal year, including the handling of subsidies and contracts under Public-Private Partnership schemes, with public works still significantly below projections three months from year-end. Fitch Ratings, for its part, has warned that the reform of the Caja Fiscal—the public-sector pension system—proved insufficient, an alarm signal that feeds into the debate over the investment grade that Paraguay has spent years aspiring to reach.

It is precisely that objective—investment grade—that frames the most revealing political and economic tension of the moment. A former Finance Minister publicly described the latest BCP report presented to Congress using terms that in Guaraní equate to "disorderly" and "imprecise," warning of the risk of eroding institutional confidence. Economists consulted by local media identify weakness in legal certainty and the persistence of corruption as the main brakes on the qualitative leap that investment grade would represent, a diagnosis that aligns with a recent report from the United States government. CAF and the IDB, at the Cavialpa forum, noted that such a credit status would open the door to financing infrastructure with private savings, reducing dependence on sovereign borrowing. The equation is clear: without investment grade, the cost of capital remains unnecessarily elevated for a country with solid macroeconomic fundamentals.

Against that backdrop of institutional uncertainty, the Ueno Bank case continues to generate ripples that extend beyond the financial system. The BCP detected maneuvers by the entity to conceal links with related companies, while it was revealed that the bank manages more than USD 800 million in public deposits, including Itaipú funds. The president of the Unión Industrial Paraguaya openly expressed concern that the crisis between the regulator and the bank could affect the real economy and employment, a signal that the episode has transcended the technical-prudential sphere to become a perceived risk to business confidence. The meeting convened by President Peña and the BCP with representatives of the banking system to address the situation did not dispel all doubts, according to participants.

On the energy front, the negotiation of Annex C of Itaipú—the instrument that regulates the compensation Brazil pays Paraguay for ceded energy—faces a geopolitical complication of the first order: the advance of Bolsonarism in the Brazilian Congress means that any agreement signed with the Lula government is potentially reversible or unviable to ratify. Itaipú supplied 17.5% more energy to ANDE in the analyzed period but remitted USD 23 million less to the Paraguayan State under the current Annex C, an operational paradox that illustrates the centrality of the tariff issue. Paraguay and Brazil are also holding broader talks on energy interconnection, with active diplomatic engagement.

On domestic prices, diesel has posted its sixth consecutive increase, reaching an all-time high, which pressures transport and logistics costs in an economy where distances and road infrastructure make fuel a cross-cutting input. The MEF placed G. 230.527 billion in Treasury Bonds during the week, in a routine operation reflecting the continued need for local-currency financing. Central Government social spending accumulated USD 4.816 billion in the first nine months of the year, as the ministry executes the payment schedule for pensioners and suppliers.

What to watch closely in the coming weeks is the resolution of the Ueno Bank case and its impact on the perceived soundness of the financial system, exchange-rate dynamics in a context of a strong guaraní and industrial pressure, the pace of Itaipú Annex C negotiations in light of Brazilian political dynamics, and the finalization of the 2027 budget in Congress, which will be the first real test of whether the Peña government is prepared to defend fiscal discipline against sectoral demands or whether the pressure of current spending will ultimately give ground before the close of the legislative session.
