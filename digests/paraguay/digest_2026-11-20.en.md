# Paraguay: Economic Pulse — 2026-11-20

The slide in Paraguay's sovereign bonds on international markets captures in a single data point what has unfolded in the country over recent weeks: the collapse of Ueno Bank has moved beyond the confines of a local banking crisis to become a systemic event that is shaking public finances, eroding institutional confidence, and forcing the government to simultaneously manage a financial emergency, a cabinet transition, and a 2027 budget already under legislative debate.

The Ministry of Economy and Finance confirmed that public funds committed to resolving Ueno Bank total USD 888 million, drawn from state entities including the Instituto de Previsión Social, Itaipú, and Yacyretá, among others. Worse still, as reported by ABC Color and Última Hora, the Banco Central del Paraguay estimates that the preliminary total deficit at the collapsed institution could reach USD 1.5 billion — a gap that turns full depositor recovery into a lengthy and uncertain process. The Chief of Staff publicly guaranteed that savers will recover one hundred percent of their funds, but the mechanics of achieving that remain contested: the Deposit Guarantee Fund has made initial payments to smaller depositors, and the BCP has released funds from savings deposit certificates, though a significant share of those affected is still awaiting resolution. Savers protested outside the central bank's headquarters demanding solutions, while the BCP launched a digital platform to validate the amounts to be refunded.

The political dimension of the scandal deepens by the day. Prosecutors ordered the arrest of the BCP's legal director on suspicion of tampering with reports, and the institution's former head, Carlos Carvallo, turned himself in to authorities. The new director of Itaipú signed the resolution that allowed Ueno Bank to act as a receiver of funds from the binational entity, though he claimed to have no knowledge of the deposits. Zacarías Irún, the now-removed director general, has also been swept up in the crisis. An internal Itaipú document revealed that a rule prohibited depositing with institutions less than five years old in the market — a condition Ueno Bank did not meet. From the Brazilian side, the binational's partner publicly questioned the Paraguayan decision to deposit with that institution, according to Vice President Nicanor Duarte Frutos. The Yacyretá unions, for their part, backed director Benítez, who has been singled out for having deposited USD 10 million at Ueno Bank, in which he was a shareholder.

The immediate market impact was tangible. Paraguayan sovereign bonds extended their decline following the collapse, reflecting the deterioration in the country's risk perception among international investors. The Chamber of Commerce warned of knock-on effects in the FX market, and the Unión de Gremios de la Producción complained that the dollar is falling "without a parachute," calling on the economic team for a coordinated response. The guaraní has appreciated at precisely the wrong time for agricultural exporters. The BCP reaffirmed the neutrality of its monetary policy, arguing that the real economy remains dynamic and prices remain under control, with cumulative expansion of 5.7 percent through August and an IMF projection that places the country as the region's fastest-growing economy for 2026. Even so, economists warn that the Ueno crisis could weigh on consumption, cool private investment, and complicate the budget debate at a particularly delicate moment.

That debate is unfolding alongside operational urgency. The Senate is expected to begin consideration of the 2027 General Budget this week, with a vote anticipated for Thursday or Friday. The Executive submitted an addendum implying a net cut of USD 276 million, within which ANDE absorbs a USD 37 million reduction. The private sector is pushing further: the Unión Industrial Paraguaya and other business chambers are calling for "major surgery" equivalent to one percentage point of GDP, with a fiscal deficit not exceeding 2.9 percent. Former minister Ferreira warned of accounting "window dressing" in the presented figures. Public debt grew by USD 1.343 billion in just five months, an acceleration that new minister Óscar Lovera will have to explain to international organizations when he travels to France to meet with the IMF and other multilateral institutions.

Lovera's arrival at the helm of the Ministry of Economy marks a deliberate change of tone. Where his predecessor Carlos Fernández Valdovinos used the phrase "war economy" to describe the fiscal squeeze, Lovera on his first day opted instead to speak of "intelligent austerity," explicitly discarding the martial rhetoric and promising signs of improvement in revenue collection. The Chief of Staff, however, maintained the call for ministers to "tighten their belts," even as concrete data show that Central Government salaries grew nine percent during the period of supposed austerity — a contradiction the business sector has not let pass. The government and business chambers also agreed to create an "economic momentum cabinet," a signal that the Executive recognizes the need to rebuild confidence with the productive sector after weeks of uncertainty.

What comes next will depend on how quickly the State can resolve three simultaneous fronts: the orderly recovery of deposits at Ueno Bank and the eventual state administration of companies linked to the collapsed entity — a model that both the Centro de Importadores and former officials warn the State lacks the capacity to manage; approval of a 2027 budget that is credible to the markets without choking off public investment; and filling the two vacancies on the BCP's board, whose independence and credibility have been severely called into question by the arrests and revelations of recent weeks. The IMF, which this week began its periodic review of the Paraguayan economy, will have more than enough material to sharpen the tone of its observations.
