# Paraguay: The Economic Pulse — 2026-11-18

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-11-18*

The collapse of Ueno Bank has turned what was Latin America's most celebrated growth story into an unprecedented institutional crisis, and the scale of public funds at risk—confirmed by the Ministry of Economy and Finance at $888 million, with estimates that according to Última Hora could exceed $1 billion—reveals something more disturbing than a simple banking failure: a State that deposited its citizens' money in an institution with which its own officials had shareholding ties.

The case concentrates in a single episode all the structural vulnerabilities that coexist, with growing discomfort, alongside the macroeconomic success narrative of Santiago Peña's Paraguay. Economic activity grew 5.5% year-to-date through September, with a 3.9% expansion in that month driven by services and industry, according to the Banco Central del Paraguay, and the IMF has ratified the country as the region's most dynamic projected economy for 2026. But that upbeat backdrop makes it even harder to explain why State entities—Petropar with nearly $46 million, Conatel with the equivalent of some $10 million, funds from the Caja Fiscal and IPS—held sizable positions in a bank whose regulatory supervision failed systemically.

The financial system's response has been swift in form, if not always sufficient in substance. The BCP confirmed that Ueno's healthy assets exceed its pension liabilities and that IPS will be able to recover its funds; 1.5 million smaller savers have already received 100% of their deposits through the Guarantee Fund. The central bank temporarily loosened reserve requirements to provide liquidity to the securities market, waived default interest for affected borrowers and cardholders, and approved exceptional measures for MSMEs. ANDE suspended disconnections for arrears through December 31 and announced it would not apply tariff adjustments for a year. The Executive sent Congress a bill to keep operational the companies tied to Miguel Vázquez—the bank's owner—and announced the creation of a business reactivation council.

What cannot be managed so easily is the institutional damage. The prosecutor's office ordered the arrest of the BCP's chief legal officer, the Superintendent of Banks and other former executives. The central bank is operating with minimum quorum following the resignation of several directors, and the Executive has proposed Fernando Rivarola to join the board. The Brazilian side of Itaipú, according to statements by Presidential Secretary Nicanor Orsi, internally questioned the deposits of binational funds in Ueno—a sign that alarms crossed borders before the Paraguayan regulator acted with sufficient determination. The timeline published by Última Hora is devastating: the BCP allowed the bank to operate for ten months with loans to related parties before stepping in.

The impact on public finances amplifies a fiscal strain that was already serious before the collapse. The annualized fiscal deficit reached $2.176 billion at the close of October—2.3% of GDP, according to La Nación—with spending growing at 10% and revenues at 1%. The Caja Fiscal shortfall exceeded $333 million in ten months. The addendum to the 2027 Budget, sent by the MEF to the Chamber of Deputies with a cut of $276 million, was approved with modifications that keep the projected deficit near 3.9% of GDP, a figure the private sector considers insufficient: business associations are calling for "major surgery" equivalent to a full point of GDP. The new Economy Minister, Óscar Lovera—who headed the DNIT and whose appointment remains in place following the departure of Carlos Fernández Valdovinos—publicly rejected the term "war economy" but acknowledged signs of improvement in revenue collection and promised to put the budget on track in 2028 after debt payments. He will travel to France to meet with international organizations, at a moment when the IMF begins its periodic review of the Paraguayan economy.

Two price moves reflect opposing tensions the market is trying to resolve simultaneously. The guaraní has appreciated with enough force to prompt a formal complaint from the Unión de Gremios de la Producción to the economic team, which warns of a dollar falling "without a parachute" to the detriment of the export sector. At the same time, the government halved the selective consumption tax on diesel—a measure already passed through to the pumps by Petropar and the private brands—as a signal of relief to transporters, who had threatened to blockade Asunción. International reserves rose by $600 million amid the uncertainty, which the BCP presented as evidence of the system's soundness and a basis for its neutral monetary policy stance.

Economist Dionisio Borda captured the paradox of the moment with a phrase now circulating widely: "Investment grade went to the economic leadership's head." The warning is that the investment grade credit rating, affirmed by Moody's, may have generated an institutional complacency that facilitated the exposure of public funds to a poorly managed bank. The fact that Ueno's bonds placed in the New York market now open the possibility of international lawsuits—according to analysis published by Última Hora—adds an external dimension to what began as a domestic financial supervision problem.

What the market and analysts will be watching in the coming weeks is threefold: whether the Senate approves the 2027 PGN with additional cuts that restore fiscal credibility before the IMF and international debt markets; whether the arrests at the BCP translate into structural reforms to banking supervision or remain a criminal chapter without regulatory consequences; and whether the consumption crisis—Asimcopar has already reported a drop in sales and warned about inventory replenishment, while the October consumer confidence index ticked up slightly but remained in pessimistic territory—deepens enough to compromise growth projections for 2025 and 2026.

## Corporate Watch

### Petropar (state-owned company, not publicly listed)
The state oil company confirmed it had nearly $46 million deposited at Ueno Bank at the time of the intervention, making it one of the largest individual exposures of public funds; the Executive is analyzing financing options to offset the operational impact on the company, which simultaneously lowered fuel prices following the ISC cut on diesel.

### Ueno Bank (bonds listed in New York markets)
With healthy assets that the BCP confirmed exceed its pension liabilities, the bank's liquidation is advancing with Senabico's seizure of real estate, vehicles and aircraft belonging to controlling shareholder Miguel Vázquez; analysts warn that holders of bonds issued in New York could initiate international lawsuits that compromise Paraguay's access to external capital markets.

### ANDE (state-owned company, not publicly listed)
The state electricity distributor, which recorded 16% demand growth between January and October, announced the suspension of disconnections for arrears through December 31 as a relief measure amid the Ueno crisis and confirmed it will not apply tariff adjustments over the next twelve months—a decision that will have a direct impact on its financial equation in an environment of widening fiscal deficits.
