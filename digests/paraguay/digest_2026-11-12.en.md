# Paraguay: The Economic Pulse — 2026-11-12

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-11-12*

The collapse of Ueno Bank has concentrated in just a few weeks a density of institutional damage that Paraguay has not seen in decades: public funds trapped in an intervened bank, a former Central Bank president under arrest, an international rating agency warning about governance weaknesses, and the guaraní ranked by Bloomberg as the world's worst-performing currency at the moment the presidential resignation was announced. What began as the fall of a digital bank has become the most severe credibility test for the country's institutions in its strongest recorded period of economic growth.

The scope of public-sector exposure to the bank is hard to overstate. Itaipú Binacional held US$163.8 million and G.115.2 billion there, amounts for which the entity was still refusing to specify how much had effectively been frozen after the intervention. The Administración Nacional de Electricidad reported US$21 million in trapped claims and filed a criminal complaint against the bank's directors. The Instituto de Previsión Social, whose exposure exceeds US$500 million according to internal estimates, is weighing the use of G.784.239 billion of its reserves to honor pension payments this year. The Caja Fiscal committed the equivalent of G.250 billion —roughly US$60 million according to the Ministry of Economy— in the entity's instruments. Conatel transferred G.70 billion to the bank just one month before its collapse. Yacyretá has a director who, according to ABC Color, is a shareholder of the bank and authorized placing US$10 million from the binational entity's pension fund there, something the entity has not clearly denied. Senave filed a complaint to recover G.40 billion. The picture that emerges is that of a bank that systematically captured state resources by leveraging a network of political and institutional ties that nobody wanted to, or was able to, interrupt in time.

The judicial dimension is advancing in parallel. The Public Prosecutor's Office reinforced its investigation team, raided Itti, a company of Grupo Vázquez —identified as the operational node through which resources were triangulated toward shell companies— and requested reports from Seprelad on the accused individuals and related firms. Former BCP president Carlos Carvallo turned himself in to the courts. The Comptroller's Office welcomed the installation of the new BCP board, calling Carvallo's resistance to stepping down "illegal." Meanwhile, a prosecutorial delegation entered the Central Bank to investigate Carvallo himself, former minister Carlos Fernández Valdovinos, and the former president of the bank. Moody's was more restrained in its language but not in its diagnosis: the case highlights governance weaknesses, and the agency is looking for an orderly transition, without specifying what would happen if that transition failed to materialize. The Deposit Guarantee Fund, for its part, will have used roughly 50% of its resources to cover those affected, leaving the financial system more exposed to any future event.

The government's response has been reactive but operationally visible. The BCP reported that 93% of depositors entitled to coverage have already received their advance payment, with nearly 1,200,000 savers credited, although blockages persist in specific cases and the BCP website momentarily crashed from the surge in demand when queries were opened. The DNIT announced it will reschedule tax deadlines and waive fines and interest for those affected, while the interim minister ordered that Ueno-affected taxpayers not pay surcharges. The Ministry of Economy and the MOPC coordinated actions to ensure the continuity of public works, several of which had been paralyzed —including the project known as Asunción's "Puerto Madero," whose contract with the ANNP was under the control of the intervened bank.

Markets have priced in the impact with precision. The guaraní suffered the largest drop in the world against the dollar in the period immediately after the presidential resignation announcement, according to Bloomberg, trading around G.6,500 per dollar at the peak of political tension. The currency partially recovered —the new BCP head signaled that he would back interventions in response to sharp drops— but the move left a signal: FX markets responded not only to the financial scandal but to the political uncertainty that accompanied it. Sovereign bonds also adjusted, and analysts consulted by La Nación warn that Paraguayan debt will be more expensive in the international market. Ueno's bonds issued in New York also open the door to international lawsuits that could complicate the country's future access to external financing, according to Última Hora. A rating agency has already halted the positive outlook for the AFD, citing doubts about BCP's controls.

Against this backdrop, the underlying macroeconomic fundamentals remain solid but their shine has become paradoxical. The BCP reports cumulative expansion of 5.7% through August, the IMF projects that Paraguay will grow at nearly double the global average, and international reserves rose by US$600 million amid the uncertainty, a sign that the central bank retains room to maneuver. But the Ueno case has shown that economic dynamism can coexist with severely deteriorated institutional governance, and that the gap between the two can become visible in the worst possible way. The Senate granted its consent to the appointment of Schreiner as head of the BCP and of Fernando Rivarola to the board, a signal that the institutional rebuild of the regulator is underway, although its credibility before international markets will depend on judicial outcomes and the actual scope of the supervisory reforms that are implemented. In the coming days, attention will focus on the treatment of the 2027 General Budget in the Chamber of Deputies, with the Ministry of Economy having already submitted an addendum with a US$276 million cut, in what Minister Lovera describes as the start of a fiscal realignment process he hopes to complete by 2028. The question the market is asking is whether that consolidation process can be sustained politically in an environment where the Ueno crisis has shaken the foundations of trust in the very institutions that were supposed to prevent exactly this.

### Ueno Bank
The bank's non-performing loans jumped from 0.10% to 14.49% before the intervention, according to ABC Color, confirming that portfolio deterioration was abrupt and possibly concealed from regulators; the entity had issued bonds in the New York market, opening the door to international litigation with implications for the future access of Paraguayan companies to external financing.

### Nubicom (Grupo Vázquez)
Conatel opened a proceeding against the telecommunications company belonging to the same conglomerate that owns Ueno Bank, alleging "unauthorized sale of shares"; the firm had installed equipment at Copaco and left outstanding debts, while the DNCP ordered BNF to reassess the bids in the tender for its core banking system in which Itti, another group company, had participated, casting doubt on multimillion-dollar public contracts linked to the conglomerate.
