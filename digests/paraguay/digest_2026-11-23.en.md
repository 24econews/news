The institutional architecture that Santiago Peña's government has built since August 2023 is now showing its first concrete signs of fiscal impact — and what emerges today from a balance sheet of his administration is a deliberate bet on state reform as a growth lever, at a moment when the region faces mounting fiscal pressures and emerging markets are watching the solidity of Paraguayan fundamentals with heightened attention.

The creation of the Ministry of Economy and Finance, the first law enacted by Peña on August 23, 2023, was far more than a bureaucratic reorganization exercise. By absorbing the Ministry of Finance, the Technical Planning Secretariat, and the Civil Service Secretariat under a single institutional umbrella, the government wagered on eliminating the fragmentation that had historically diluted the coherence of Paraguayan economic policy. Concentrating budgetary, planning, and personnel management functions in a single portfolio follows a logic similar to that adopted in recent years by countries such as Colombia and Peru, where ministerial unification sought to give greater agility to fiscal execution without sacrificing internal controls.

The second major structural reform was the merger of the Undersecretariat of Taxation and the National Customs Directorate into the new Dirección Nacional de Ingresos Tributarios (DNIT), enacted in the final days of Mario Abdo Benítez's term but designed by Peña's technical team. The stated objective is ambitious: lift the tax burden from 10 to 12 percent of GDP and generate some USD 400 million in additional annual revenue, according to projections by director Óscar Orué. For a country whose tax take ranks among the lowest in South America, that jump would mark a real inflection point in the state's capacity to finance infrastructure and social spending without resorting to external debt.

The reform was not without friction. From the accounting sector, critical voices such as Nora Ruoti questioned the design of the merger, while then-Customs director Julio Fernández warned of the operational risks of integrating an institution that processes 5,000 operations a day and sees 100 staff rotations per month. Fernández's concern points to a real dilemma: customs management autonomy, particularly in foreign trade, can erode when decisions are subordinated to a longer chain of command. If the new centralized structure slows border-level operational resolution, the cost could be measured in longer clearance times and deteriorated logistics competitiveness — just as Paraguay seeks to consolidate its position as a regional trade hub.

The macro backdrop matters here. Paraguay has maintained one of the region's most sustained growth rates over the past decade, driven by agribusiness, hydroelectric power and, more recently, by investment flows linked to the maquila regime and the financial sector. But that strength coexists with a narrow tax base that constrains public investment and leaves the country vulnerable to drought cycles or commodity price downturns. The fiscal reform, if executed with the efficiency it promises, could correct that structural asymmetry.

What the balance sheet of Peña's first year also reveals, though with less media noise, is that the modernization of the Paraguayan state is advancing in parallel with domestic political tensions — including episodes within the prison system that have exposed the fragility of institutional security — and against an external backdrop in which the U.S. Federal Reserve maintains restrictive rates, raising the cost of international financing for emerging economies seeking capital for public investment.

What is worth monitoring over the coming months is threefold: first, whether DNIT can demonstrate measurable revenue gains before the 2024 fiscal year closes, which would validate the government's reformist thesis; second, how foreign trade operators respond to the new customs architecture, particularly in the highest-volume border zones; and third, whether the Ministry of Economy and Finance can articulate a medium-term planning strategy that transcends the political cycle. Paraguay has the fundamentals to justify investor confidence — but institutional reforms only create value when they are executed, not when they are enacted.
