# Paraguay: The Economic Pulse — 2026-10-16

The guaraní emerged this week as Latin America's best-performing currency, lifted by an export surplus that reflects both the strength of agribusiness and the strains that same appreciation is placing on the productive sector. It is that paradox—robust growth that simultaneously sows sectoral concerns and institutional fragilities—that defines Paraguay's economic moment.

Economic activity accumulated growth of 5.7% through August, according to Banco Central del Paraguay data, cementing a pace that the IMF already projects at 4.5% for the year, a figure several local players estimate could approach 5%. ECLAC, for its part, projects a 19% expansion in exports for 2026, a signal of external confidence in the agro-export chain that underpins much of the current dynamism. The second quarter, however, showed a slowdown relative to the first half, suggesting the economy is not free of internal frictions.

The guaraní itself embodies that contradiction. Its appreciation—which made it the region's top-gaining currency—is a direct consequence of export flows, but the Unión de Gremios de la Producción has warned the economic team of what it termed a "parachute-less" dollar decline, a phenomenon that erodes margins for agricultural producers and exporters who settle in foreign currency but incur costs in guaraníes. The meeting between President Santiago Peña and the BCP with private banks sought to contain the unease, though participants revealed that no explicit intervention commitments emerged. The MEF placed G. 230.527 billion in Treasury bonds this week, with the total stock of bonds in the local market now reaching some US$ 1.2 billion, a sign that domestic debt financing remains the preferred instrument of an administration determined to preserve its international fiscal reputation.

That reputation was on display in Washington, where Economy Minister Óscar Lovera and the BCP president took part in the IMF and World Bank meetings, while Paraguay secured the backing of a US$ 300 million World Bank loan aimed at strengthening economic resilience. The central bank also raised its monetary policy rate, a decision reflecting the need to anchor expectations in a context where central government wage spending grew 9.9% and public debt interest payments rose 12.9%—pressures that specialists flag as the chief risks to medium-term fiscal accounts.

The 2027 budget debate—whose Bicameral Commission is in the thick of hearings—lays bare the model's tensions. The Universidad Nacional is requesting an additional US$ 61 million. The Defense Ministry projects a budget of US$ 488 million, of which 81% will go to personnel spending, a proportion openly questioned by economists. Wages, pensions and debt service already account for close to 70% of public financing, leaving scant room for investment and countercyclical policy. Specialists warn of the widening deficit, debt dynamics and the relative contraction of public investment, arguing that the official narrative of a "dynamic economy" masks a rigid spending structure that the next administration will inherit.

Against that backdrop, the Ueno Bank case commanded the financial system's attention with unusual intensity. The BCP detected maneuvers by the bank to conceal ties with related companies, while the lender acknowledged that its public deposits include funds from binational entities—though Itaipú, whose reserves in the Paraguayan banking system jumped from US$ 57 million to US$ 537 million without the entity disclosing which banks hold them, did not respond to queries. Former minister Benigno López demanded greater transparency from the BCP and warned of potential risk to IPS funds, the country's largest pension system. The Unión Industrial Paraguaya added its voice, expressing concern over the crisis's impact on employment and business confidence, while a former minister described the BCP's report to Congress as "kachiãi"—superficial—and warned of the reputational damage that an opaque handling of the episode could inflict on the financial system.

The energy front also generated warning signs. Yacyretá opened its spillway in response to rainfall five times greater than forecast and anticipated high water levels on the Paraná near Ayolas, which could affect generation. Large industrial energy consumers called for clear tariff rules through 2037, amid the uncertainty generated by the renegotiation of Annex C of the Itaipú Treaty. Petropar, meanwhile, is weighing an adjustment to the price of diesel without altering that of gasoline, a move that would directly affect logistics costs for the agricultural and transport sectors.

On the institutional front, President Peña announced the merger of the Ministry of Industry and Commerce with the National Tourism Secretariat, an administrative reform that follows the logic of institutional consolidation begun with the creation of the Ministry of Economy and Finance and the merger of the DNIT. On the latter, director Óscar Orué pledged to raise the tax burden from 10% to 12% of GDP and collect an additional US$ 400 million annually—an ambition markets will watch cautiously until execution data back it up.

In the coming days, monitoring of the Bicameral Commission's budget hearings for four key institutions, the evolution of the exchange rate under year-end export pressure, and the regulatory resolution of the Ueno Bank case will constitute the three most relevant barometers for gauging whether the macroeconomic solidity Paraguay projects internationally also holds up from within.
