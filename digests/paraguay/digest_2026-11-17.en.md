# Paraguay: Economic Pulse — 2026-11-17

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-11-17*

The Ueno Bank crisis has ceased to be a contained financial problem and has become the gravitational axis of Paraguay's entire economic policy, dragging with it the Central Bank, the national budget, the country's regulatory credibility and, according to Fitch Ratings, the sovereign rating itself.

The scale of the damage is revealed in the accumulation of emergency responses that the Banco Central del Paraguay has had to deploy within days. The BCP temporarily eased the legal reserve requirement on deposits to inject liquidity into the securities market, waived default interest and surcharges on loans and credit cards for those affected, and confirmed that its sound assets exceed pension-related liabilities — a signal intended to contain panic, though its effectiveness depends on how quickly confidence can be restored. The institution also completed the Deposit Guarantee Fund crediting process at 100% for its 1.5 million savers, though blockages persist that prevent a portion of beneficiaries from actually accessing their funds. Jorge Schreiner, an economist quoted by ABC Color, was blunt: the immediate priority is for the BCP to recover its national and international credibility as quickly as possible. That sentence, uttered about the very institution that was supposedly supervising the intervened bank, captures the depth of the institutional problem.

What compounds the crisis is the political dimension. The Prosecutor's Office ordered the arrest of the BCP's legal director and detained the Superintendent of Banks, while two former executives of the institution were also arrested and a third remains a fugitive. The Comptroller General's Office, by contrast, praised the openness of the BCP's new board, labeling its predecessor's conduct "illegal." In parallel, the binational entity Yacyretá faces accusations that one of its directors deposited USD 10 million in Ueno Bank while being a shareholder in the same institution, even as the entity's unions shield him. Conatel, the telecommunications regulator, transferred 70 billion guaraníes to Ueno just one month before its collapse. Petropar has funds trapped that its own officials — some with monthly compensation of 117 million guaraníes — are alleged to have concealed. Institutional contagion is, in practical terms, systemic.

The effects on the real economy are already visible. Retailers and event organizers describe the situation as comparable to the pandemic. Asimcopar reported falling sales and warned of difficulties in restocking merchandise. MSMEs, which account for a large share of Paraguay's productive fabric, are calling for the creation of a Fogapy 2 — a credit guarantee fund similar to the one implemented during Covid — and are warning of a "string of casualties" that the formal financial system has yet to process. Consumer confidence, according to recent data, rose in October but remains in pessimistic territory.

Fitch Ratings placed Paraguay's sovereign rating on negative watch following Ueno's collapse, though it also clarified that it sees no signs of systemic contagion and reaffirmed the overall soundness of the financial system. That distinction — between reputational damage to the regulator and the health of the rest of the system — is the one the government is trying to establish as its official narrative. The new Economy Minister, Óscar Lovera, appointed to replace Carlos Fernández Valdovinos — who bid farewell with a "mission accomplished" that sounded like unintended irony given the context — rejected the "war economy" label coined by his predecessor and promised signs of improvement in tax collection. The Union of Production Guilds was more categorical: with the Ueno crisis "the most precious thing was lost, confidence."

Against this backdrop, the Chamber of Deputies approved the 2027 General Budget, with the Executive's addendum including a USD 276 million cut in operating expenses. Nonetheless, the projected deficit remains around 3.9% of GDP, a figure the private sector considers insufficient: business guilds are demanding a reduction equivalent to 1% of GDP, the elimination of privileges and superfluous spending, and more surgical reform. The Chief of Cabinet has already instructed ministers to "tighten their belts," while the new MEF promises to bring the budget into line by 2028 — a horizon that, given the pace of events, feels distant.

On the broader macro front, the data remain surprisingly robust: economic activity grew 5.6% cumulatively through May and 3.9% in September, driven by services, agriculture and industry. The IMF began its periodic review with Paraguayan authorities in a meeting that takes on added relevance given the current juncture. Paraguay remains the fastest-growing economy projected in Latin America by the Fund, with an expansion that will nearly double the regional average. That underlying macroeconomic strength is the authorities' main argument for containing the Ueno impact — and the main factor that, for now, prevents the banking crisis from tipping into recession.

The exchange rate adds another layer of pressure: the dollar is showing a downward trend that the UGP has described as a fall "without a parachute," with asymmetric effects on exporters and importers. The BCP reaffirmed the neutrality of its monetary policy, but the combination of an appreciated guaraní, relaxed reserve requirements, an elevated fiscal deficit and a regulatory system under judicial scrutiny creates a picture that international markets will be watching closely.

What will need to be monitored in the coming weeks is multifaceted: the passage of the 2027 Budget through the Senate and whether the cuts will be enough to satisfy Fitch and avoid a formal downgrade of the sovereign rating; the progress of criminal investigations against former BCP officials and their impact on the institution's governance; the effectiveness of the "economic momentum cabinet" agreed between the government and business guilds; and the speed with which the new Minister Lovera can restore a credible fiscal narrative before international organizations, including his upcoming meeting with representatives in France.
