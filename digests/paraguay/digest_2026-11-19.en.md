# Paraguay: The Economic Pulse — 2026-11-19

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-11-19*

The collapse of Ueno Bank has ceased to be an isolated financial problem and has become the most serious institutional crisis facing Paraguay in Santiago Peña's tenure to date, and markets are already reading the situation that way. Paraguayan sovereign bonds extended their losses in recent sessions, according to ABC Color, a sign that international investors are recalibrating country risk amid a cascade of revelations that, day by day, widens the magnitude of the hole and complicates the stability narrative the government had carefully constructed.

The number that matters most today is $1.5 billion. That is the preliminary shortfall the Banco Central del Paraguay identified between the assets and liabilities of the intervened institution, according to information published by ABC Color. At the same time, the Ministry of Economy and Finance confirmed that at least $888 million in public funds is directly committed, a figure equivalent to roughly two percentage points of GDP in a country whose fiscal deficit already reached 2.3% of GDP at the end of October, according to La Nación. The arithmetic is brutal: Paraguay faces extraordinary fiscal pressure precisely at the moment the new Economy Minister, Óscar Lovera, takes office with the pledge that the budget can only be brought back on track by 2028.

The Ueno crisis is not simply a bank failure. It is a corporate and regulatory governance scandal that is spreading like an oil slick across several state institutions. The Prosecutor's Office ordered the arrest of the BCP's Superintendent of Banks and the central bank's chief legal officer, according to La Nación, while the new Itaipú director signed the resolution that allowed Ueno to receive deposits from the binational entity, although he claims to be unaware of the details. More revealing still: there was an internal Itaipú document that explicitly required five years of market track record for an institution to be eligible to receive its funds, which turns the deposits placed there into a flagrant violation of the company's own procedures. Meanwhile, the Yacyretá unions shielded a director of that binational entity who deposited $10 million in the failed institution, in whose capital he was also a shareholder, according to ABC Color.

The government responded with institutional speed, but with limited resources. The BCP approved exceptional measures to assist affected MSMEs, the Guarantee Fund began paying advances to individual depositors, and the Executive branch introduced a bill to keep companies linked to the Ueno group operational. Fitch, for its part, dismissed signs of systemic contagion and reaffirmed the broader financial system's soundness, according to La Nación, an assessment the market received with moderate relief but one that was not enough to halt the pressure on sovereign paper. The UGP, the country's most influential rural lobby, was blunt in its diagnosis: with the Ueno crisis "the most precious thing was lost, confidence." Those words carry specific weight in a country whose economic model rests on trade openness, exchange rate stability, and regulatory predictability.

The exchange rate adds another dimension of tension. The UGP complained to the economic team that the dollar was falling "without a parachute," at a time when agricultural markets were facing renewed pressures during 2026, according to ABC Color. For a country whose soybean and beef exports are the backbone of its foreign exchange earnings, the appreciation of the guaraní directly erodes the competitiveness of the sector that generates the most employment and income in the productive interior. Analysts revised their forecast for the greenback upward, anticipating that it could settle near 6,200 guaraníes by year-end, according to Última Hora.

In this context, the arrival of Óscar Lovera at the Ministry of Economy has the flavor of a halftime adjustment. His predecessor, Carlos Fernández Valdovinos, bowed out with a line that will go down in the annals of ministerial candor: "The economy is fine, the Treasury is not." Lovera dismissed the "war economy" narrative installed by the Chief of Staff, which had generated resistance in the private sector, but acknowledged that tax collections felt the impact of the Ueno collapse in November, according to Última Hora. Meanwhile, the Senate is preparing to debate the 2027 General Budget this week, after the Chamber of Deputies approved it maintaining a projected deficit of 3.9% of GDP, a figure the private sector deems unsustainable and which economist Dionisio Borda himself interpreted as evidence that "investment grade went to the economic leadership's head."

There are, nonetheless, signals the new minister can use to buy time. Electricity demand grew 16% between January and October 2026, according to La Nación, pointing to real activity that is still dynamic. ANDE announced it will not apply tariff adjustments for a year. The IMF began its periodic review of the Paraguayan economy and the BCP president reaffirmed the neutrality of monetary policy in an economy that the multilateral itself projects to grow at twice the regional average. That macroeconomic resilience is real, but it is being subjected to an institutional stress test with no close precedent.

What will define the coming weeks is the speed with which the judiciary advances on criminal responsibilities in the Ueno case, how the Senate handles the 2027 budget, and whether the Finance Ministry manages to recover a substantial share of the $888 million in committed public funds before the fiscal impact translates into service cuts or additional borrowing. The creation of the so-called "economic boost cabinet," agreed between the government and business associations, may be a gesture of political stabilization, but markets are waiting for numbers, not committees.
