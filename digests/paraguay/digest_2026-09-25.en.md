# Paraguay: Economic Pulse — 2026-09-25

*AI-generated analysis | 2026-09-25*

British firm Atome Energy has become the epicenter of Paraguay's economic conversation today, and what is at stake goes well beyond a green hydrogen contract: it is a litmus test for the country's institutional credibility at a moment when President Santiago Peña's government is simultaneously accumulating solid macroeconomic signals and fiscal fragilities that the IMF is no longer willing to overlook.

The deadline expires on September 30. If Atome fails to meet the commitments set out in the addendum to its contract with the Administración Nacional de Electricidad, the state utility will demand payment on a USD 40 million guarantee. What complicates Paraguay's position is that, according to specialist consultants cited by ABC Color, ANDE has handled the file with notable negligence, leaving unresolved a USD 117 million complaint that the Contraloría General never processed and allowing formal expectations to build that Atome now invokes to threaten arbitration proceedings. The company argues that the Paraguayan State made concrete promises and generated legitimate expectations; ANDE's president, for his part, insists that Atome has nothing to claim. Economists consulted by the same outlet describe the situation as governmental "improvisation." The opacity is striking: with only days to go before the deadline, there is no official information on the outcome of the technical meeting between the two parties. Any international arbitration, should it materialize, would arrive precisely as Paraguay seeks to position itself as a reliable destination for clean-energy investment, turning this episode into something more than a contractual dispute.

The paradox of the moment is that this institutional noise coexists with genuinely robust macroeconomic data. The Central Bank reported GDP growth of 6.6% in 2025 — what the BCP dubbed, with a certain pride, a "war economy" — and economic activity was accumulating expansion of 5.6% through the end of July, according to La Nación. Surveyed economic agents are raising their projections toward 5% for the current year, and the IMF acknowledged the solidity of Paraguay's performance, though it attached a list of eleven pending tasks, including improving tax efficiency, broadening the fiscal base, and cracking down on money laundering. This qualified recognition matters: Paraguay is growing, but with a tax burden hovering around 10% of GDP — one of the lowest on the continent — and the head of the new Dirección Nacional de Ingresos Tributarios, Óscar Orué, has pledged to lift it to 12% as a result of the merger between SET and Customs, a reform that Hoy's article attributes to Peña's team as part of its legislative legacy.

The 2027 budget, which the Bicameral Commission took up again this Monday with four entities under scrutiny, condenses the structural tensions. Specialists warn of a widening deficit, public debt whose interest servicing rose 12.9% year-on-year, and shrinking public investment. Central government wage spending grew 9.9%, while IPS admitted to lawmakers a monthly deficit of up to USD 15 million and accumulated debt exceeding USD 1.2 billion. The Ministry of Economy — an institution created barely two years ago and already on its second minister with the arrival of Óscar Lovera — projects that the budget will only get back on track in 2028, after honoring debt commitments. The MOPC, meanwhile, will see its budget increased by 33%, a sign of an infrastructure bet that contrasts with the slow execution that lawmakers themselves denounce.

The FX market adds another layer of complexity. The guaraní has recorded sustained appreciation against the dollar in recent weeks, a phenomenon that ABC Color attributed in part to the absence of a BCP liquidity window, which accelerated the drop in the exchange rate. The real estate sector — also fueled by USD 732 million in annual remittances — is feeling the impact, and agricultural and maquila exporters face compressed margins. Importers and consumers of dollar-denominated goods, however, are getting some relief, in a context where diesel prices have accumulated increases of up to 43.8% and the private sector is questioning the "political prices" that Petropar keeps artificially in place, distorting the fuel market. The fifth round of price increases at private brands began this week, and the question of how long the state firm can hold out without adjusting is becoming ever more urgent.

President Peña, for his part, announced new credit lines for the productive sector and the merger of the Ministry of Industry and Commerce with the Secretaría Nacional de Turismo, a move that the MIC's head framed as part of a package of "deep reforms" for industry. Paraguay is also advancing talks with Brazil on energy interconnection and is pushing for equal quotas in the EU-Mercosur agreement. Bank delinquency remains at 2.3%, slightly below the regional average, a sign that credit is flowing in relatively healthy fashion. And yet economists warn that consumption is decelerating and that signs of deterioration in domestic demand are already visible.

What to watch closely in the coming days is threefold: the resolution — or escalation — of the Atome case before September 30; the progress of the budget debate in the Bicameral Commission, where IPS figures and public debt promise tough clashes; and the trajectory of the exchange rate, which the BCP will have to manage with growing attention if the guaraní's appreciation begins to erode export competitiveness in a year when the country most needs to consolidate its growth numbers.
