# Paraguay: The Economic Pulse — 2026-11-26

The collapse of Ueno Bank has ceased to be a purely financial problem and has become a state-level crisis that simultaneously entangles bilateral diplomacy with Brazil, the integrity of the 2027 budget, and the confidence of Paraguay's private sector — all in the past forty-eight hours.

Today, November 26, Superintendent Rivarola publicly detailed the catalogue of failures that led to the bank's extinction: governance deficiencies, concentrated asset exposure and, according to ABC Color, a "five-year rule" on deposits which, according to director Zárate, was never presented to the board and whose documents will only be handed over to the courts. The most politically explosive revelation is that funds belonging to Itaipú Binacional — the hydroelectric facility Paraguay co-manages with Brazil — deposited at Ueno total roughly USD 220 million, a figure the Government has yet to explain how it will replace. Paraguay's Foreign Minister pledged to his Brazilian counterpart the restitution of those resources during a meeting held in Asunción this week, but offered no specific mechanism or timeline. ABC Color warns that breach of the conditions on those deposits could contaminate the Itaipú tariff renegotiation itself, a process already delicate on its own terms: energy sector sources indicate that ANDE would accumulate losses of USD 389 million in 2026 under the current tariff scheme. The government is also preparing a presidential meeting between Alliana and his Brazilian counterpart, which raises the diplomatic stakes at a moment of negotiating vulnerability for Asunción.

The international dimension of the case worsened with the revelation that Luxembourg deposited environmental cooperation funds — the so-called "green funds" — at Ueno, capital that is now also at risk. For Paraguayan exporters, particularly in the sesame sector, the concern goes beyond finance: according to La Nación, the exporters' guild fears reputational damage to the country in European markets, precisely as Paraguay negotiates compliance with the European Union's anti-deforestation regulation, under which the first soybean exports are expected in 2027. The Mercosur-EU business forum, which will bring together more than 500 executives in Uruguay, takes place against this backdrop of image fragility.

On the domestic front, the contamination of the Ueno case extends to Grupo Vázquez — the construction and services conglomerate whose bankruptcy left thousands of workers unpaid — and to the Ministry of Economy. A congresswoman alleged that former minister Fernández Valdovinos signed a contract with Ueno worth USD 20,000 monthly plus profit-linked bonuses after leaving office, and lawmakers are calling for an investigation into whether disbursements from the MEF ended up channeled into the bank now in resolution. The Executive, meanwhile, submitted on an urgent basis a bill to advance up to five minimum wages to former Grupo Vázquez employees, framing it as an "advance, not a subsidy," though without any agreement yet to reactivate the group's companies.

The financial industry's response was immediate but cautious. Banking leaders ruled out a systemic crisis, pointing to two specific "sins" committed by Ueno — risk concentration and governance opacity — that would not be replicated across the system. The BCP confirmed that the system retains liquidity and stability, and set a withdrawal cap of G. 30 million weekly for unit-holders of the Ueno mutual fund, while the search continues for alternatives for those affected, who have gathered outside the regulator's offices. Even so, La Nación gathers accounts of citizens who withdrew savings to keep them "under the mattress," and the events sector reports a 30% drop in bookings as an indirect effect of the bank's closure.

Against this backdrop of uncertainty, the Senate convened an extraordinary session to debate the 2027 General Budget, valued at USD 25.5 billion after adjustments by the Finance Committee, which cut USD 200 million from the original proposal and capped the Zero Hunger program at 155 days. The Multisectoral Forum today labeled the 2027 PGN "fiscal irresponsibility," and an analysis published by ABC Color describes it as a scheme to "pay for the past with future debt." In parallel, the Ministry of Public Works announced a disbursement of up to USD 300 million for the construction sector — with a first confirmed tranche of USD 150 million via loan — in an attempt to reactivate a payment chain disrupted by the collapse of Grupo Vázquez and the funds trapped at Ueno.

What should be watched closely over the coming days is the Senate's plenary session on the 2027 PGN, the concrete mechanism the Government offers Brazil to replace the Itaipú funds, and the progress of judicial investigations into MEF flows into Ueno. Any of those three fronts has the capacity to reshape Paraguay's political and economic landscape before the year is out.
