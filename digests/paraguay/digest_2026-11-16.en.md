# Paraguay: The Economic Pulse — 2026-11-16

The Chamber of Deputies this week approved the 2027 General Budget of the Nation with an addendum trimming USD 276 million, but that figure, far from closing the fiscal debate, has blown it wide open with a ferocity not seen in Asunción in years. The data point that best captures the Paraguayan moment is not economic growth — robust, with cumulative expansion of close to 5.7% according to the Banco Central del Paraguay — but rather the simultaneity of two contradictory realities: an economy leading regional growth and a state that has entered a fiscal, political and institutional credibility emergency without recent precedent.

The immediate trigger is the collapse of Ueno Bank, whose resolution has unleashed a chain of consequences that continues to branch out. In the first week of the crisis, the Deposit Guarantee Fund disbursed approximately USD 644 million to cover roughly 1.8 million retail savers, and according to the BCP, the crediting process has been technically completed for 100% of beneficiaries, though operational bottlenecks persist. Yet what is beginning to emerge with clarity is that the real impact of Ueno far exceeds the problem of individual depositors. Petropar, the state oil company, had nearly USD 46 million trapped in the institution; the Instituto de Previsión Social has over USD 500 million in exposure — a figure that on its own raises questions about pension sustainability beyond 2032, according to analyses cited by ABC Color — and the Administración Nacional de Electricidad has had to suspend disconnection of delinquent customers through year-end precisely because its own finances have been compromised, threatening the continuity of IDB-financed works, including the new 500-kilovolt line.

The political dimension of the scandal has escalated at the same pace. The Attorney General's Office ordered the arrest of the Superintendent of Banks at the BCP and the institution's chief legal officer. The BCP itself is operating today with a minimum quorum following the resignation of several of its directors. The Comptroller General's Office highlighted the new board's opening posture as an act of institutional goodwill, while labeling as "illegal" the prior conduct of former central bank president Carvallo. Fitch Ratings, which had placed Paraguay's rating on negative watch after Ueno's collapse, subsequently published that it detects no signs of systemic contagion and reaffirms the general soundness of the financial system — an important distinction the market will need to process carefully. To sustain securities market liquidity in this context, the BCP temporarily eased the legal reserve requirement on deposits, an emergency measure signaling how quickly monetary authorities had to improvise responses.

What makes the narrative even more complex is that the Ueno scandal has collided directly with the budget process. The Ministry of Economy and Finance submitted an addendum to the 2027 PGN with USD 276 million in cuts concentrated in operating expenses. Public debt rose USD 1.343 billion in just five months, placing the new Economy Minister, Óscar Lovera — appointed after the exit of Carlos Fernández Valdovinos, whose management was described by business leaders as having caused "irreparable damage" — at a first-order fiscal crossroads. Lovera has softened the rhetoric, swapping the "war economy" formula for "intelligent austerity," and has pledged to steer the budget toward 2028 after paying down accumulated debts. His first international mission will be to meet with multilateral agencies in France, a signal that Asunción needs to reassert its narrative of stability before external creditors at a delicate moment.

The private sector is not waiting patiently. MSMEs, which describe a "trail of wounded" from the Ueno crisis, are calling for the creation of a Fogapy 2 — a second guarantee fund for credit reactivation — while Fedemipymes warns that the economy is "completely stalled." The Unión Gremial del Paraguay declared that with the Ueno crisis "the most precious thing was lost: trust." ANDE is seeking alternatives so that the crisis does not paralyze its electrical infrastructure projects. Paraguay's sovereign rating, recently validated by Moody's and Fitch, remains under scrutiny.
