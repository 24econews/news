The largest private investment bet in Paraguay's history —a $12 billion project whose execution is reportedly already underway according to ABC Color— arrives at a moment when the country has accumulated 24 consecutive months of growth exceeding four percent and is emerging as the most dynamic economy in the region. The figure, if confirmed, would represent an unprecedented structural transformation for a country whose GDP hovers around $45 billion, and signals that the narrative of stability built over the past decade is attracting capital on a scale previously unseen.

The macroeconomic backdrop surrounding this announcement is genuinely solid, though not without tensions. The Banco Central del Paraguay reported GDP growth of 6.6 percent for 2025 —internally characterized as a "war economy" for its emphasis on austerity and spending restraint— and the first quarter of the current year closed with an expansion of 5.8 percent. The economy grew 5.1 percent in the first four months of the year, according to La Nación, with expansion across all sectors. Fitch Ratings maintains the country at BB+ with a positive outlook, and the IMF has acknowledged the strength of the model, albeit with a list of eleven pending tasks that include the fiscal deficit, public debt and, notably, money laundering.

That tension between the growth headlines and the structural vulnerabilities is precisely what defines Paraguay's economic moment. The 2027 general budget —whose sectoral defenses before Congress continue following the municipal elections— is being scrutinized by specialists who warn of financial "window dressing," in the words of former minister Ferreira. Wage spending in the central government rose 9.9 percent, while interest payments on public debt grew 12.9 percent, two figures that legislators are examining with increasing attention. The MEF placed 230.527 billion guaraníes in Treasury bonds on the local market, where the total stock of these instruments reaches approximately $1.2 billion, a sign that the domestic capital market is deepening although it still depends significantly on external credit: Paraguay has $5.625 billion in external loans under execution and manages more than $1.6 billion in additional funds for public works, development and liquidity.

On the currency front, the guaraní has experienced an appreciation that has sparked debate among traders and analysts. The absence of the BCP's liquidity window accelerated the drop in the dollar's quote in recent weeks, according to an economist consulted by ABC Color, although the greenback showed signs of recovery at the start of the week in line with the rebound of the DXY index, which rose nearly three percent to surpass 101 points year-to-date. The meeting between President Santiago Peña, the BCP and private banking representatives revealed concerns about exchange rate dynamics, although participants did not preview concrete measures.

The government is advancing in parallel with an agenda of institutional and sectoral reforms. President Peña announced additional credits and the merger of the Ministry of Industry and Commerce with the National Secretariat of Tourism, a consolidation that the MIC minister presents as part of "deep reforms" for the industrial sector. The Dirección Nacional de Ingresos Tributarios —the result of the merger between the SET and Customs, originally driven by the Honor Colorado team— aims to raise the tax burden from ten to twelve percent of GDP, which would equate to roughly $400 million in additional annual revenue. In parallel, the DNIT is evaluating the elimination of the tax exemption on electric and hybrid vehicles, precisely when imports in that category are showing an upward trend, which illustrates the dilemma between capturing fiscal revenue and maintaining incentives for the energy transition.

Fuel prices remain a point of political and social pressure. Several service stations have already initiated the fifth consecutive hike, while analysts warn that the adjustment is "imminent for everyone." Petropar, the state-owned company, is maintaining its prices as an anchor for now, but the sustainability of that position is being questioned. In the energy sector, large industrial consumers are demanding clear tariff rules through 2037, a demand that reflects the regulatory uncertainty coexisting with investor optimism. Fishermen on the Paraná River, meanwhile, are announcing protest measures over the binational Yacyretá entity's failure to meet its commitments to riverside communities.

On the geopolitical and trade front, Paraguay is pressing for equitable conditions in the quotas of the European Union-Mercosur agreement and is strengthening its outreach to the OECD at meetings held in Uruguay, two processes that reflect a more assertive economic diplomacy under the Peña administration. Family remittances, which total $732 million annually, continue to underpin domestic consumption and the real estate market, while the underground economy —estimated at between 40 and 47 percent of GDP according to various sources— remains the Achilles' heel of a model that the IMF praises in public but demands be corrected in private.

What to watch in the coming weeks is multifaceted but focused: confirmation and details of the $12 billion investment project —its sector, its actors and its financing scheme—; the evolution of the exchange rate given the potential reopening of the BCP's liquidity window; the outcome of the 2027 PGN budget negotiations in Congress; and the real impact of the fifth fuel price hike on inflation expectations in a context where growth optimism hovers around five percent but fiscal risks remain unresolved.
